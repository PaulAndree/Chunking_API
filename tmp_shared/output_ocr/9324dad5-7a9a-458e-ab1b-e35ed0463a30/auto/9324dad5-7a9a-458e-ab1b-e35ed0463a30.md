# INTRODUCTION

The particular interest of this symposium is to review all relevant knowledge which may help in the assessment of carcinogenic and mutagenic risk, in particular the risk arising from diverse environmental and metabolic conditions. Of special interest are the origins of somatic and germ line mutations. A priori, the majority of deleterious mutations are recessive, which as germ line mutations may cause more victims than the dominant mutations. The reason is that the majority of dominant mutations are readily recognized in the first carrier; either their propagation is directly stopped in the case of lethal mutations or they can be controlled in the case of non-lethal mutations. Recessive mutations are usually not recognized either in the first carrier or in later heterozygous carriers and they thus spread in the population repeatedly causing victims among the homozygous progeny. Homozygosity arises in the offspring of heterozygous parents by the Mendelian rule of random chromosome assortment. Homozygous somatic cell lineages can arise in heterozygous individuals by chromosomal rearrangements (mitotic recombination, deletion, non-disjunction). Since the normal human population contains roughly 1% heterozygous carriers for any given recessive gene mutation, a normal individual may carry on the order of 103 diverse recessive mutations, some of which when homozygous may cause severe cellular metabolic defects including cancerous growth. Therefore, when trying to estimate risk from carcinogenic and other deleterious mutations, one cannot underestimate the role of chromosomal rearrangement in expression of pre-existing as well as of newly arising recessive mutations. mutations in humans may become possible through development of new sensitive Early routine detection of recessive (and other) molecular probes.

Mutation can be defined in molecular terms as any alteration in some original genomic nucleotide sequence. (Traditionally, the terms mutagenesis and mutation referred to relatively rare stochastic events which are stable, i.e., revert back to the original sequence as rarely as the occurence of the initial wild type -> mutant alteration. This traditional notion of mutation is distinct from the pre-programmed DNA rearrangments which are genetically controlled and which can occur frequently and reversibly and fulfill a defined biological function). 1 Thus some mutations can profoundly affect a biological function whereas others can be phenotypically neutral.

Mutations arise either "de novo", as errors in DNA replication, or "de antiquo" by the scrambling of pre-existing sequences in the course of diverse DNA recombination events. 1,2

I shall consider here only the "de novo" mutagenesis which can arise either as errors in the course of replication of unmodified DNA templates or as modified-template-directed errors. Errors in the course of replication of unmodified templates can be inducible (e.g., by the presence of chemically modified deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates or by unbalanced pools of the four normal deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates or by the presence of some divalent cations such as Mn++ and Be++) 3 or they may arise simply as the consequence of the limited fidelity of the DNA replication machinery and/or as the consequence of the limited efficiency of postreplicative mismatch correction. " Template-directed errors may occur either by direct miscopying of the modified (miscoding) base in the DNA or indirectly as a complex metabolic consequence of the presence of non-coding DNA lesions in replicating DNA. A number of DNA lesions are known to cause such a stereochemical change of the original base as to make its hydrogen bonding and stacking characteristics resemble those of another normal base more than the intact original base (e.g., Ob-methyl-guanine pairs better with thymine than with cytosine, see Reference 5 for this and more examples). Base analogs and small alkylating agents are typical for this group of mutagens.

A majority of the known potent chemical mutagens/carcinogens cause noncoding lesions in DNA which under normal high fidelity replication conditions block the elongation of the newly synthesized strands. These agents are potent mutagens because they induce a cellular mutator activity which is required for fixation of mutations opposite non-coding lesions (so called targeted mutations) but which appears to cause mutations also in undamaged DNA (so called untargeted mutations). This discussion will deal with these inducible mutagenic processes, which are just one part of a complex cellular metabolic response to DNA damage, the so-called SOS induction.5-8 Since the E. coli mismatch repair system has been used to characterize SOS-induced mutagenesis, a brief review of mismatch repair is presented below.

# DIRECTED AND UNDIRECTED MISMATCH REPAIR IN E. COLI

# Correction of replication errors

In order for a process of mismatch repair to be able to correct replication errors, it must be able to distinguish newly synthesized strands from parental strands and must selectively correct the copy according to the original and not vice versa. In E. coli, a means of strand discrimination for the mismatch repair system is provided by adenine methylation in GATC sequences. 4 Mismatch repair is greatly reduced in regions of DNA in which GATc sequences are fully adenine methylated and repair occurs preferentially, if not exclusively, on unmethylated strands of

hemimethylated heteroduplexes.,10 Because newly synthesized strands are transiently undermethylated, i.e., methylation lags somewhat behind replication, mismatch repair in $\underline { { \mathbf { E } } } _ { \leq } \underline { { \mathsf { c o l i } } }$ is believed to occur primarily on newly synthesised strands immediately behind the replication fork. The mismatch repair system eliminates over 99 percent of replication errors.11

# Specificity of mismatch repair

The repair of all classes of non-complementarities between the two strands was tested in heteroduplex DNA's of bacteriophage A transfecting E. coli. The transition mismatches (G:T and A:C) appear to be efficiently repaired, whereas three of the six transversion mismatches (G:A, C:T and C:C are not.10,1213 All four single unpaired bases in frameshift:wild type heteroduplexes are well recognized and repaired on either of the two strands. 10 Thus, it appears that replication errors involving either deletion or insertion of a single base are corrected. 14 However, the system does not appear to act on large unpaired regions (about 800 base pairs) suggesting that there is a maximum size of unpaired region that the system can recognize as a mismatch. Further experiments are required to determine what that size is.

Mismatches in GC rich regions are more efficiently repaired than mismatches in AT rich regions (M. Jones, R. Wagner and M. Radman, unpublished). Petruska and Goodman have shown that the in vitro error rate of T4 phage DNA polymerase is proportional to the GC content of the environment. Therefore, it appears that the mismatch repair system acts most efficiently on the most frequent replication errors (e.g., G:T, A:C, frameshift and transversions, Pu:Pu and Py:Py, in GC rich regions). The final effect of mismatch repair on DNA replication is the elimination of over 99 percent of mutations resulting in equilibration of the occurence of different types of mutations along the genome.

CFNETTC DIAGNOSIS OF UNTARGETED AND TARGETED MUTATIONS

(1) The E. coli mismatch repair system normally operates by excislonresynthesis and thus can correct replication errors only if the parental template strand is intact. When the parental DNA strands are damaged, and when the Sos system is induced, two kinds of mutations can occur: targeted mutations, which arise opposite DNA lesions by the error-prone copying of the damaged template, and untargeted mutations, which arise in the course of error-prone replication of intact DNA. It was shown that, as expected, only untargeted mutations (which arise as genuine mismatches) are subject to mismatch correction.17 Excision of the targeted mutation could not lead to its correction as long as the initial lesion remains in the template. Indeed, it was found that the E. coli mismatch repair system decreases untarreted mutagenesis but has no effect on targeted mutagenesis.

COPYING OF THE NON-CODING DNA LESIONS: "BYPASS REPLICATION" IN VIVO AND IN VITRO

The notion of mutagenic lesion-bypass replication was proposed along with the notion that such a mutagenic process is part of the inducible SOS induction program triggered by DNA damage.6 Sedgwick's analysis of the replication of UV irradiated E. coli DNA supported this model but the mechanism was not clear (e.g., recombination repair and bypass replication were equally plausible). Caillet-Fauquet et al. 21 analysed the fate of UV-irradiated single-strand 0X174 phage DNA in single infection and demonstrated that UV-induced SOS-mutagenesis in E. coli is correlated with acquisition of the capacity to elongate growing DNA chains across the sites of initial blocking lesions. However, their work did not address the still unknown mechanism of the "bypass", e.g., did the "bypassed" lesions (ovrimidine dimers) remain in the unaltered form?

WHAT HAVE WE LEARNED FROM SEQUENCING OF INDUCED MUTATIONS?

The first sequences of Uv-induced mutations in $\underline { { \mathtt { E } } } _ { \bullet }$ coli DNA clearly indicated that adjacent pyrimidines are frequent sites of Uv-induced mutations.29 There is still some controversy as to the relative contribution of pyrimidine dimers versus $6 , 4$ photoproducts (see contributions by Drs. Hutchinson and Glickman and their coworkers, this volume). The analysis of mutations induced by carcinogens which attack predominantly guanines also supported the notion of targeted mutations. 30

Targeted (Uv and gamma ray) and untargeted substitution mutations were first sequenced in M13 phage 32 and then extensively analyzed in a forward mutation system. $3 3 - 3 5$ Now, a large body of data in phage $\gimel$ and $\mathbf { \underline { { E } } _ { * } }$ coli DNA allows the conclusions that both targeted and untargeted mutations do occur, that the nature of the damage determines the specificity of targeted mutations and that, in general, frameshift (one base deletion) mutations are produced in both targeted (e-g., Uv and apurinic site induced) and untargeted (e.g., Uv-induced) mutagenesis $3 2 - 3 5$ in addition to base substitutions.

Models, or working hypotheses, for mbypass replication" in $\underline { { \mathtt { E } } } _ { \texttt { c o l d } }$ must account for the following reviewed charaeteristics: (1) targeted mutations are generated by the replication of damaged parental DNA, such that the viable progeny molecules contain a high proportion of (sometimes only) the mutant sequence (viz., strand loss), (2) the nature of the targeted mutation is influenced by the nature of the lesion, thus diverse base substitution mutations should be possible as well as one base deletion (frameshift) mutations, (3) low-fidelity replication of undamaged DNA may accompany the "bypass"32

Recent genetic studies clearly show that the enzymology of "bypass replication" is complex. The initial idea that untargeted mutagenesis is the necessary consequence of the low-fidelity requirement for the "bypass" may not hold (G. Maenhaut-Michel, A. Brotcorne-Lannoy, and P. CailletFauquet, personal communication), because mutants exist which affect only targeted mutagenesis (e.g., umuC,D) or only untargeted mutagenesis (po1A, some din mutations, etc...). Two studies implicate DNA polymerase III as being involved, directly or indirectly, in at least the untargeted mutagenesis.37,38 Furthermore, the genetically-induced SOS mutator effect (e.g., recA441 mutation) and the Uv-induced mutator effect appear to be different (G. Maenhaut-Michel, A. Brotcorne-Lannoy, and P. CailletFauquet, as above).

Hence, there are not sufficient elements available to draw a detailed model for SOS mutagenesis. Figure 3 illustrates a simplified model which, although deficient in enzymology, makes a few specific suggestions about the possible structures of the damaged single-stranded template and of the damaged double-stranded DNA, which are compatible with all three requirements stated above.

The principal suggestion is that the misincorporation event opposite DNA damage (step 1, Figure 3) may be facilitated both by the decrease in the intrinsic fidelity of DNA polymerases and by the modification of the DNA structure at the site of damage. There is considerable experimental evidence that the inhibition of both base selection 26-29 and the $3 ^ { 1 }$ to 5' exonuclease facilitates the "bypass". It was recently suggested that the SOS mutator effect 18 and the misincorporation event (1 in Figure 3)38 do do not require functional UmuC,D proteins, but appear to require some other inducible activity. Thus the umuC,D gene products may be involved in steps 2 and/or 3 in Figure 3. However, attempts to complement the umuC,D gene requirement in targeted mutagenesis by the mutD mutation (which severely inhibits the 3' to 5' exonuclease proofreading associated with DNA polymerase III) 39 has given negative results so far (A. Brotcorn-Lannoy, personal communication). Since the in vitro "bypass" replication of apurinic sites is facilitated by pyrophosphate depletion, it was suggested that polymerase-associated pyrophosphate metabolism may be affected by Sos induction.8,40

I propose that all three steps in Figure 3 could be facilitated, and some circumvented, if the damaged base (or a base-loss site) could swing out of the helix, allowing the stacking of the two undamaged flanking bases in the template. Thus facilitated "bypass" replication would produce a onebase deletion mutation. There is considerable evidence (from model building (reference $4 7$ and M. Radman, unpublished), photochemistry (reference $\mathfrak { q } 0$ and K. Minton, personal communication) and N.M.R. studies) 42- that such extrahelical structures can occur both in single-stranded and in doublestranded DNA, even in the absence of damage.

If the extrahelical conformation of the damaged base could induce the extrahelical conformation of the base opposite the damage in double-stranded DNA (as illustrated in Figure 4), then it is conceivable that parental DNA strands could strand-direct mistakes at the same site, presumably involving single-base detection. In other words, the damaged strand could affect the complementary strand in its coding properties. I presume that special inducible proteins could be responsible for the stabilization of the extrahelical base (or damaged base) conformations in Figures 3 and 4. UmuC,D and RecA proteins are obvious candidates. These and probably some other proteins could affect steps 1 and 2 in the "bypass" (Figure 3). The RecA protein was shown to inhibit the polymerase III-associated 3' to 5' exonuclease activity and to cause decreased fidelity of DNA polymerase III in yitro. 45 On the other hand, the single-strand binding protein (SSB) is expected to prevent the extrahelical base conformation by its cooperative binding to the phosphate moiety.

The intention of this speculation is just to create hypotheses to explain how damaged double-stranded DNA could yield (largely) pure mutant progeny in the pathway of targeted mutagenesis. A less speculative phenomenon which can contribute to pure mutant progeny of damaged DNA is the strand loss phenomenon, which occurs with phage λ DNA even in the absence of damage.46 Strand loss is of regular occurence when only one of the two phage λ DNA strands is UV irradiated (M. Radman, R. Wagner, and Z. Trgovevic, unpublished experiments). The genetics of targeted mutagenesis dictate that the UmuC,D proteins should be involved in the strand loss phenomenon and/or in the stabilization of the extrahelical conformation in Figure 4, if these speculations are relevant to the molecular mechanism of targeted mutagenesis.
