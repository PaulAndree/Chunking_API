# Ultraviolet Reactivation and Ultraviolet Mutagenesis of $\lambda$ in Different Genetic Systems

M. DEFAIS,1 P. FAUQUET,1 M. RADMAN,2, 3 AND M. ERRERA

Laboratoire de Biophysique et Radiobiologie, Faculté des Sciences, Université libre de Bruxelles, Brussels, Belgium

Accepted October 26, 1970

The ${ \mathit { l e x } } ^ { + }$ function is necessary for the induction of clear mutations of bacteriophage $\lambda$ by ultraviolet irradiation.

Ultraviolet reactivation of λ phage is also dependent on the ${ \mathit { l e x } } ^ { + }$ function.

Ultraviolet mutations are affected to a small extent by the red+ function of $\gimel$ phage whereas this function seems to play an important role in the spontaneous mutation process.

When organisms like Escherichia coli or bacteriophage λ are irradiated with moderate UV doses, the preponderant lethal and mutagenic lesions in their DNA consist of pyrimidine dimers (cf. Howard-Flanders, 1968; Witkin, 1969; Radman et al., 1970). These can be eliminated either before DNA replication or during and/or after DNA replication by DNA repair mechanisms (cf. Howard-Flanders, 1968). Excision repair (controlled by bacterial uvr+ functions) seems to be responsible for the elimination of dimers before DNA replication; recombinational repair (controlled by bacterial $r e c ^ { + }$ and λred+ functions) then removes the remaining unexcised dimers (Rupp and Howard-Flanders, 1968; Radman et al., 1970). Among the survivors, mutants can be found; this fact suggests that either some lesions remain, DNA repair processes being occasionally inaccurate (Witkin, 1969), or perhaps that repair processes enable mutated organisms to survive.

Most studies on UV mutagenesis have been carried out with $E$ coli (see Witkin, 1968); the essential conclusions can be summarized as follows:

1. Mutations are expressed after DNA replication (Haas and Doudney, 1960; Lieb, 1960). 2. Synthesis of proteins (Witkin, 1956) and of RNA (Doudney and Haas, 1959) is required.

3. The exr function of $E$ coli B (similar to the lex function in $E$ coli K12)(Witkin, 1967) and the $r e c A ^ { + }$ function (Miura and Tomizawa, 1968; Witkin, 1969) are required for UV mutagenesis. Excision repair by the uur+ functions does not seem to be mutagenic.

4. The majority of UV-induced mutations are single base substitutions as cited by Witkin from the data of Yanofsky on the A chain of tryptophan synthetase of $E$ . coli (Yanofsky, see Witkin, 1969).

In the present study, we have used the phage $\lambda / E$ coli system in order to study mutation induction by irradiation of extracellular phage.

UV reactivation is the increased survival of infective centers when UV irradiated phage infects a UV irradiated host, as compared with infection of unirradiated bacteria (Weigle, 1953). UV irradiation of both bacteriophage and host cell is also an essential condition for extensive mutagenesis of bacteriophage λ (Weigle, 1953; Miura and Tomizawa, 1968).

The results of different studies on UV reactivation (Weigle, 1953; Miura and Tomizawa, 1968; Kneser, 1968) and UV mutagenesis (Jacob and Wollman, 1955; Devoret, 1965; Miura and Tomizawa, 1968) of $\lambda$ phage are sometimes at variance, because the doses given to phage and host cell, respectively, were different.

We report here a study of UV reactivation and UV mutagenesis of λ phage as a function of low UV doses using various bacterial strains mutant in their rec, uur, or lex functions.

It is known that the bacterial recA function is necessary for UV reactivation of $\lambda$ phage and that this function is also required for $\lambda$ mutagenesis (Miura and Tomizawa, 1968). We therefore checked both UV reactivation and UV mutagenesis with bacterial strains carrying a mutation in the lex gene which is, as mentioned earlier, analogous to the exr gene of $E$ coli $\mathbf { B }$ and necessary for bacterial UV mutagenesis (Witkin, 1967).

# DISCUSSION

# UV Reactivation

The results show that of the known bacterial functions affecting radiation sensitivity at least two of them recA and lex are essential for UV reactivation of λ phage. Genes controlling excision repair (uurA, uvrB and uvrC) do not seem to be required for this phenomenon (Radman and Devoret, in press). However, the UV reactivation peak obtained with uvr mutants is strongly dose dependent, i.e. the capacity for UV reactivation of these mutants is limited with respect to the dose to both λ and host cell. This is probably why some authors did not find UV reactivation in excision-deficient mutants (Miura and Tomizawa, 1968) and others found only a very small effect (Harm, 1963; Kneser, 1968; Kneser et al. 1965).

The mechanism of UV reactivation is completely unknown. Weigle (1953) found that the mutation frequency among UV reactivated $\lambda$ phages is strongly increased. We have confirmed here his observation that the same functions involved in UV mutagenesis of $\lambda$ (recA- and lex functions) are also necessary for UV reactivation.

Two nonexclusive hypotheses might explain UV reactivation. On the one hand, Garen and Zinder (1955) suggested that recombination between $\mathcal { E }$ coli DNA and λ DNA could lead to an increase both of viability of $\lambda$ and of mutation frequency. This hypothesis could explain the requirement of recombination functions for UV reactivation. However, it is difficult to imagine that the "heterologous" recombination could eliminate dozens of pyrimidine dimers in λ DNA and lead to such a high increase in survival. In addition, the absence of attachment regions from the host and λ chromosomes does not affect UV reactivation (Radman and Devoret, in press).

The following hypothesis seems attractive: irradiation of the host might induce some bacterial function necessary for UV reactivation. A strong argument for this hypothesis is the absence of UV reactivation in the absence of protein and RNA synthesis (Ono and Shimazu, 1966).

# UV Mutagenesis of λ Phage

For the induction by UV of λ clear mutations both bacterial recA and lex functions are necessary. As in the case of UV reactivation, irradiation of both $\lambda$ and host cell is required for extensive mutagenesis of λ. In addition, the dose relationships for $\lambda c$ mutations and UV reactivation are extremely similar for each strain used. This suggests that there may be some steps in common in the two processes. Isolation of bacterial mutants which are deficient in only one of them might give more information about the relations between UV reactivation and UV mutagenesis in phage.

# UV-Induced Mutation and Recombination

Our results confirm Witkin's suggestion (Witkin, 1968) that excision repair is an accurate repair mechanism for eliminating premutational lesions: uur functions are not required for UV mutagenesis of $\lambda$ phage. Recombination repair in which both $r e c A ^ { + }$ and ${ \it l e x ^ { + } }$ functions are involved appears to be inaccurate and lead to mutation (see Witkin, 1969). Recombination repair (HowardFlanders, 1968) is probably involved in both UV reactivation and UV mutagenesis of $\lambda$ phage and is enhanced when bacteria are UV irradiated. For equivalent levels of UV reactivation or mutagenesis, uvr mutants require much lower doses than uvr+ bacteria, as is the case for prophage induction. The dose reduction factor for UV reactivation, UV mutagenesis, and UV induction of $\lambda$ in uur bacteria corresponds to the excision repair factor indicating the essential role of the residual pyrimidine dimers in these phenomena. In addition, the necessity of protein synthesis for UV mutagenesis (Witkin, 1956; Doudney and Haas, 1958), UV reactivation (Ono and Shimazu, 1966) and UV induction of prophage λ (Tomizawa and Ogawa, 1967) also suggests that one (or more) bacterial function(s) are induced by UV. This hypothesis is now being tested.

It is worthwhile to notice that both recA and exrA (equivalent to lex) bacterial mutations suppress pleiotropically UV mutagenesis, UV reactivation, and prophage induction (Donch et al., 1970) suggesting that some common pathways might be involved in these phenomena.

UV induced recombination (Jacob and Wollman, 1955) seems to be a consequence of recombination repair (Radman et al., 1970) which is thought to occur after DNA replication (Rupp and Howard-Flanders, 1968). If this is true, one would expect a certain correlation between UV-induced recombination and mutation. Therefore, we have plotted the recombination frequencies determined by Baker and Haynes (1967) using the same phage and bacterial strains, and our mutation frequencies against the UV dose given to the phage (Fig. 3). UVinduced recombination (Baker, 1970) and mutation is nonirradiated phage (Table 1) both increase in a similar way (by a factor of 2 to 3) with the UV dose to the host cell.

Similar correlations were found between spontaneous mutations and crossovers in meiosis (Magni and von Borstel, 1962) as well as for acridine mutations and recombination (Streisinger et al., 1966). If these correlations are correct then recombination as a mutagenic process has a key role in evolution.
