Water loss or desiccation is among the most life-threatening stresses. It leads to DNA double-strand breakage, protein aggregation, cell shrinkage, and low water activity precluding all biological functions. Yet, in all kingdoms of life, rare organisms are resistant to desiccation through prevention or reversibility of such damage. Here, we explore possible hallmarks of prokaryotic desiccation tolerance in their proteomes. The content of unstructured, low complexity (LC) regions was analyzed in a total of 460 bacterial and archaeal proteomes.

It appears that species endowed with proteomes abundant in unstructured hydrophilic LC regions are desiccation-tolerant or sporulating bacteria, halophilic archaea and bacteria, or host-associated species. In the desiccationand radiation-resistant bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans, most proteins that contain large hydrophilic LC regions have unassigned function, but those with known function are mostly involved in diverse cellular recovery processes. Such LC regions are typically absent in orthologous proteins in desiccation-sensitive species. D. radiodurans encodes also special LC proteins, akin to those associated with desiccation resistance of plant seeds and some plants and animals. Therefore, we postulate that large unstructured hydrophilic LC regions and proteins provide for cellular resistance to dehydration and we discuss mechanisms of their protective activity.

Different kinds of environmental stresses impose strong natural selection and cause adaptive phenotypic changes in living organisms. Desiccation is among the most severe challenges to living cells: it causes DNA double-strand breaks and oxidative lesions, as well as damages to RNA, proteins, lipids, and cellular metabolites.1,2 Although water is essential to terrestrial life, a number of organisms can survive prolonged periods of desiccation by entering a dormant state called anhydrobiosis.2 Anhydrobiosis (“life without water”) occurs across all kingdoms of life: bacteria, archaea, fungi, plant, and animals.2 For example, they include the bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans, the resurrection plant Craterostigma plantagineum, bdelloid rotifers, tardigrades, and nematodes.1–3

The molecular mechanisms assuring anhydrobiosis are not fully understood, but the role of disaccharides, mainly trehalose and sucrose, has been recognized.2 Yet, the abundance of sugars seems to be only a part in the provision of resistance to desiccation stress.3 Two particular genes have been found to be upregulated during desiccation stress in the nematode Aphelenchus avenae: a protein called anhydrin and a late embryogenesis abundant (LEA)-related protein, both highly enriched in hydrophilic amino acids.4 It has been known that genes encoding LEA proteins are upregulated during seed formation, accounting for their desiccation tolerance, and during desiccation of plant somatic tissues.5 Both bioinformatics analyses6 and in vivo studies7 have shown that these proteins might have water retention or even chaperone activity during desiccation stress, thereby preventing protein aggregation and enzyme inactivation in water-deprived cells. Sequence analysis shows that LEA proteins are characterized by abundance of hydrophilic amino acids, as well as glycine residues, with an important feature that majority of them display low complexity (LC), compositionally biased sequences.6 An exhaustive computational analysis resulted in classification of LEA proteins into six groups.6 Groups 2 and 3, whose members were recognized to contribute to desiccation resistance, are found to be natively unstructured but become structured upon water loss.6

At this point, LC sequences have been mostly identified in eukaryotes as protein sequences that mediate protein–DNA, protein–protein, or protein– ligand interactions in various cellular processes.8 Similar functions have been proposed for LC regions in rare prokaryotic proteins.8

By using the SEG program,9 we have made exhaustive search for LC sequences among 460 sequenced prokaryotes (420 bacteria and 40 archaea), with the aim of identifying novel putative functions of the LC sequences among prokaryotes. We have investigated the potential involvement of hydrophilic LC sequences in the desiccation tolerance and addressed possible mechanisms of their action. Until now, desiccation tolerance among prokaryotes has been attributed to disaccharide abundance, their ability to efficiently repair DNA double-strand breaks and scavenge free radicals produced during periods of prolonged desiccation. By its remarkable capacity to survive extremely high doses of ionizing radiation, and prolonged periods of desiccation, the bacterium D. radiodurans is the most popular robust prokaryote.1 Key events accounting for its remarkable DNA repair capacities have recently been described.10 However, how proteins and lipids survive the consequences of extreme radiation and desiccation stresses is far from understood. Therefore, in studying the putative functions of LC regions, particular attention was given to this microbe.

# Prokaryotic proteomes are abundant in LC regions

We have performed the analysis of LC regions in 460 proteomes. All bacterial and archaeal proteomes available at the time of the beginning of this work were collected from HAMAP (High-quality Automated and Manual Annotation of microbial Proteomes database). Lifestyle of each species was assessed based on the data from various literature sources. The number of LC regions was estimated for all individual proteins by using the SEG program.9 In brief, an LC sequence is a protein segment with low Shannon entropy whose composition is significantly different from the average composition of the entire protein and exhibits overrepresentation of a small number of different amino acids.9 They sometimes comprise a large part of proteins but their evolutionary and functional roles are poorly understood. So far, they have been described mainly as protein–protein and protein–DNA interfaces involved in many cellular events in both eukaryotes and prokaryotes. However, it was a general consensus that their abundance is mainly a property of eukaryotic proteins.8 In addition, it has been shown previously that the sequence complexity is indicative of protein propensity for being disordered in space: LC sequences with high content of charged and hydrophilic amino acids have been shown to correlate with increased propensity of disorder.11,12

The distribution of number of LC regions (normalized to the proteome size) among 460 prokaryotic proteomes is presented in Fig. 1 together with the data on mean number of LC regions, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis. The distribution of the data has been estimated to be chi-squared distribution. It should be noted that the number of LC regions per protein ranges from a minimum of 0.69 for Anaplasma phagocytophilum to a maximum of 3.65 for the endosymbiont Carsonella ruddii. Two more species have more than three LC regions per protein: Anaeromyxobacter dehalogenans (3.08) and Burkholderia pseudomallei (3.07). For comparison, we generated a “random proteome”, whose size and amino acid composition correspond to that of $D$ . radiodurans. The random proteome was generated by a Python script with predefined length (length of the $D$ . radiodurans proteome, all proteins concatenated) and amino acid frequencies (frequencies within the $D$ . radiodurans proteome). Within such randomly generated proteome, not more than a total of $1 8 ~ \mathrm { L C }$ regions appear, the largest one being 29 amino acids in length (data not shown). Among eukaryotes, it has so far been detected that Drosophila melanogaster has 3.1 LC sequences per protein, which is to date the largest number observed for eukaryotes in systematic studies.8

In addition, we have computed the distribution of sizes of all LC regions found in the data set of 460 proteomes (Supplementary Fig. 1). LC regions of 10–30 amino acids in length are most abundant in the data set. Among them, the hydrophilic ones most likely represent loops in proteins and the hydrophobic ones most likely represent their transmembrane regions.

# Desiccation tolerance, sporulation, and halophilicity correlate with the abundance of hydrophilic LC regions

The species that have the number of LC regions per protein larger than the top limit of the standard deviation $1 6 \%$ of studied species) have been considered here as species abundant in the LC regions and their lifestyles have been analyzed in further detail. At this point, we hypothesized that, if abundant, the solvent accessibility of such hydrophilic residues within LC regions would be high, as they would be of disordered structure,12–15 which is relevant in the context of a cell losing water. Therefore, we have proceeded with the computation of mean hydrophobicity according to Kyte and Doolittle's16 scale for (i) all individual proteins from the 460 proteomes and (ii) all LC regions detected in the data set. From the set of all LC regions, we have focused only on those that have the mean hydrophobicity smaller than zero, that is, which are hydrophilic. The number of proteins with hydrophilic LC regions larger than 40 amino acids has been normalized to the size of the proteome (Supplementary Table 1). This is the subgroup of LC regions that is considered in further analysis: protein sequences that are highly hydrophilic and larger than 30–40 amino acids have been described as parts of proteins lacking any specific secondary structure,13 a property of interest in this study.

To avoid biases of representation in the sequence database, of the total of 61 species (Supplementary

Table 2) that resulted from the described procedure, we have excluded data on different strains of the same species. The list of remaining 45 species together with their HAMAP acronyms and descriptions of their lifestyle is summarized in Table 1. Interestingly, species that contain the highest number of LC regions per protein are most frequently desiccation-resistant bacteria, halophilic archaea and bacteria, or sporulating bacteria. The rest of the species are those in some way associated with a host: either endosymbionts or pathogens. In order to test if there is a correlation of desiccation tolerance, sporulation capacity, halophilicity, and host association with the number of hydrophilic LC regions larger than 40 amino acids, we have done the following. The distribution of the number of hydrophilic LC regions larger than 40 amino acids has been compared using the Mann–Whitney test for the following: sporulating versus non-sporulating species, halophiles versus non-halophiles, desiccationtolerant versus desiccation-sensitive species, and free-living versus host-associated species (endosymbionts, pathogens). The test has given $p$ values of 0.0002 for desiccation tolerance, ${ \bar { < } } 0 . 0 0 0 1$ for sporulation capacity, $< 0 . 0 0 0 1$ for halophilicity, and $< 0 . 0 0 0 1$ for host-associated species, which has confirmed the significance of the result. It should be noted at this point that what desiccation and high salinity stress have in common is the decrease in water activity they cause within cells. While we are aware that sporulation is a complex regulated process followed by changes in gene expression, loss of water is one of the events taking place during spore formation. It is with this particular event that we wish to connect the phenotype we observe in this study. In the case of host-associated species, other mechanisms are more likely: interaction promiscuity provided by the flexibility of such regions may be very important for protein–protein and protein– DNA interactions of these species interacting with the host.

It is interesting to observe the distribution of hydrophobicity of these protein parts. The distribution of mean hydrophobicity values for all studied sequences is confronted to the distribution of mean hydrophobicity values of the detected hydrophilic LC regions in Fig. 2. The two distributions were compared using the Mann–Whitney test. The mean hydrophobicity of the two distributions do not differ significantly $( { \dot { p } } = 0 . 4 4 )$ . However, it cannot be excluded that, being a part of a protein, such regions may increase the affinity of proteins towards water, that is, protein solubility. In general, protein sequences or their parts that are highly hydrophilic and larger than 30–40 amino acids have been described as parts of proteins lacking any specific secondary structure.13 Such highly hydrophilic protein sequences are termed natively disordered and have previously been shown to be unable to fold into a compact structure.12 Rather, they usually form large random conformations, parts of the protein that exhibit an ensemble of different structures. Due to these particular properties, such regions give rise to large solvent accessibility and provide sites for numerous interactions with a polar solvent such as water. Therefore, while they may increase the solubility of a protein, one can also imagine that they mimic water molecules in their absence by the interaction between their polar side chains and the protein surface. In host-associated species, it is possible that the abundance of LC regions provides flexibility to their proteins, an important property for their interaction with the host cells: natively unfolded domains enable their proteins to interact with a variety of partners. In addition, such hydrophilic LC regions often take part in protein–protein and protein–DNA interactions. However, we cannot exclude the possibility of a bias present in proteomes of endosymbionts and pathogens due to genome shrinkage.

Other possible functions of unstructured hydrophilic protein domains are considered in details elsewhere.14,15 Generally, the opinion that the lack of structure necessarily means the lack of function has been abandoned, in particular when considering “protein diseases” such as Alzheimer's, Huntington's, or Parkinson's.17

# Proteins with hydrophilic LC regions participate in the recovery processes of D. radiodurans

What are the roles of natively disordered protein regions in prokaryotes? In order to tackle this question, we have separately focused on the extremophile bacterium $D$ . radiodurans by analyzing in detail which are the proteins tagged with a hydrophilic LC region larger than 40 amino acids that is absent from the corresponding family of orthologous proteins.

The majority of these proteins are hypothetical proteins whose function has not yet been annotated (Supplementary Table 3). However, 10 of the recognized proteins have annotated function and the results of their analysis are summarized in Table 2. We have detected $\tau$ subunit of DNA polymerase III (DR_2410), MutT/nudix hydrolase (DR_0550), and ABC transporter (DR_2145): proteins that are important to the bacterium's amazing recovery process after exposure to ionizing radiation. Figure 3 presents schematic presentations of nudix hydrolase (Fig. 3a) and τ subunit of DNA polymerase III (Fig. 3b). The location and size of the large hydrophilic LC regions in these two proteins can be noted: the region is situated at the N terminus in the case of nudix hydrolase or within the protein as in the case of $\tau$ subunit of DNA polymerase III. Other examples are rotamase (DR_1893), the enzyme that catalyzes the rate of protein folding, serine protease (subtilase family, DR_1459), and adenine-deaminase-related protein (DR_A0268) that is involved in salvage pathways of nucleotides and nucleosides. Rotamase has already been suggested to be contributing to the radiation resistance of the microbe.18 It is tagged with an 80-amino-acid-long hydrophilic LC region near its N terminus. In addition, adenine-deaminase-related protein has 190 amino acids in LC regions throughout the protein. This protein is involved in salvage pathways of nucleotides and nucleosides and is likely to be crucial in the DNA synthesis-dependent DNA repair following desiccation and ionizing radiation.9 Interestingly, all of these proteins are involved in either DNA metabolism or protein fate. Serine/threonine protein kinase-related protein (DR_1769), S-layer homology (SLH) protein (DR_1124), and a tetratricopeptide repeat family (DR_0937) member have been detected as well; however, their functions have not been described in sufficient details. SLH proteins, in general, may also provide environmental protection to the microbe, especially against lack of water.18 The SLH protein detected in this study has a 51- amino-acid-long LC region that may be enhancing its capability to protect the cell against desiccation. It is also interesting that a subtilisin-like serine protease is tagged with an LC region of 43 amino acids. The protein may be contributing to the degradation of damaged proteins in the earliest stages of rehydration and increase the general protein turnover. We emphasize that a large number of hypothetical proteins with no detectable homologs in the database appears also to be tagged with an LC region. In general, $D$ . radiodurans has more than 1000 unique open reading frames (third of its genome) that might be contributing to its amazing recovery capacities in ways yet to be discovered.19 However, until their functional assignment, we do not know of their potential role in this species' desiccation resistance.

A homolog of the LEA (DR_1172), or hydrophilin, protein also emerged from the computation. According to the classification of the LEA proteins, we have classified this deinococcal protein as the member of group 3 LEA proteins. Generally, groups 2 and 3 of LEA proteins are widespread in anhydrobiotes6 and better understanding of their function would contribute significantly to the body of knowledge concerning desiccation tolerance. It was suggested that LEA proteins exhibit chaperone activity, yet classical chaperone activity is not expected from them since they are structurally very different from the majority of chaperones.20 Interestingly, it has been found that eukaryotic LEA proteins are induced upon onset of desiccation and are able to suppress protein aggregation and inactivation under water stress conditions.20 It has been estimated for the nematode A. avenae LEA protein (AavLEA1) that its degree of hydration is approximately 20 times higher than that for a typical globular protein of equivalent size.21 Furthermore, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopic analysis has demonstrated that, when dried, the protein undergoes the transition from a random-coil conformation, via the poly-proline type II helix, to $\alpha$ structure while releasing water from the protein into the surroundings.21

# Evolution and mode of action of hydrophilic LC regions in D. radiodurans

For the LC regions present in $D$ . radiodurans proteins that are absent in orthologous proteins of related species (closest relatives of $D$ . radiodurans, Deinococcus geothermalis, and Thermus thermophilus), it is tempting to speculate about their emergence in $D$ . radiodurans genes or loss from other species' genes. The two possibilities are:

(1) The common ancestor contained the LC regions that were lost in the close relatives of $D$ . radiodurans. In the framework of this scenario, one could imagine that LC regions detected today initially contained single amino acid array encoded by the same codon—a triplet repeat DNA sequence that can readily be deleted by DNA slippage.

(2) The common ancestor did not have extensive LC region but $D$ . radiodurans acquired such region, for example, by expansion of a short DNA triplet repeat. Such hypothetical triplet repeat regions would present an advantage by providing much faster evolution of the open reading frames at given predetermined positions. The conservation of the expansion would occur concomitantly with the sequence divergence by point mutations within the repeat. Hydrophilicity and disordered conformation exposed to water might have been the principal selective pressure on the expanded triplet repeats in organisms exposed to desiccation. Although water is the milieu of life, research on desiccation is sparse. Surely, cell membranes shrink, DNA breaks down, and proteins tend to denature and aggregate. Our study suggests that the prokaryotes consistently facing water deprivation (arid environments, sporulation, or high salt concentrations) might be protected by the presence of highly hydrophilic proteins and LC regions. One can see that a fraction of functional folded proteins in each of these organisms are tagged with a long hydrophilic chain. It is a general opinion that there are numerous redundant mechanisms in $D$ . radiodurans (as well as other resistant bacteria) to cope with desiccation. Some of the familiar ways to resist long desiccation periods in $D$ radiodurans are the presence of the sugar trehalose, S-layer that is surrounding cells, as well as the expression of the LEA protein homolog. These mechanisms could be described as “altruistic”, that is, working for the benefit of entire cell. On the other hand, presence of the unstructured regions in individual proteins, proposed in this study, could be an additional way to defend individual proteins from the lack of water enabling them to survive periods without water by avoiding aggregation. In this case, it is possible to speculate that only crucial functions actually have such a region. It is premature to propose the exact mechanism by which such LC regions could contribute to desiccation tolerance (hydrophilins). The short list of already discussed possibilities is as follows: (i) the disordered hydrophilic regions behave like sponges21,22 that adsorb water during its abundance with capacity up to 20 times higher than a globular protein and release it in its absence while undergoing a conformational change over a poly-proline type II helix into an $\alpha$ helix; (ii) it is possible that such regions, when long enough, are able to act as chaperones of dehydrating globular proteins by polar amino acid side chains mimicking water molecules;20 and (iii) instead of acting as a chaperone, such long hydrophilic polypeptides can act as molecular shields, protecting aggregation-prone proteins from aggregating and also preventing membrane fusion.7 Finally, we should also emphasize that the remaining 395 species may also contain desiccation-resistant species or may have proteins able to withstand periods without water. However, in the framework of this study, we have focused merely on those with the strongest signal represented in the abundance of LC regions. Further details will be described elsewhere.

In conclusion, we found that proteomes of desiccation-resistant and/or sporulating bacteria, as well as several halophilic bacteria and archaea, are abundant in long hydrophilic LC regions. We speculate that hydrophilic LC regions contribute to the cellular fitness during the periods of water deprivation. Based on analysis of the D. radiodurans proteome, it appears that several proteins functionally involved in DNA metabolism or protein fate are tagged with LC regions. Thus, this study lends support to the hypothesis that hydrophilic LC regions are part of adaptive mechanisms accounting for the desiccation tolerance.
