# 1. INTRODUCTION

Short-term tests in bacteria for predicting carcinogenicity measure the ability of an agent to damage DNA. Such damage can be measured by assaying for mutations, for increased killing of DNA-repair-deficient strains of bacteria, or for induction of lysogenic bacteria. There is increasing evidence that DNA damage in somatic cells can give rise to cancer (and possibly other diseases) and that DNA damage in germ cells can cause heritable genetic defects; hence, results from tests that measure DNA damage can be useful in assessing the potential of a chemical to cause cancer and/or heritable genetic effects. Although these tests can be used very effectively in distinguishing different types of DNA damage, there is at present no evidence as to which of the specific types of DNA damage are causally related to the initiation of carcinogenesis in humans. Bacterial tests are particularly well suited for inclusion in screening batteries, and for detecting and identifying mutagenic components in complex mixtures, for detecting mutagenic trace contaminants in industrial chemicals or drugs, for studying metabolic activation pathways, for testing human body fluids and in host-mediated assays, and for analysis of the specific types of DNA damage caused by carcinogens and mutagens. Among the various short-term tests available (see this volume), those using bacteria are among the simplest to conduct, are very rapid (some take only a few hours) and are inexpensive. The genetics of bacteria are also well understood; this has enhanced the usefulness of bacterial tests by permitting the development of several strains with greater sensitivity than the wild type to a wide range of mutagenic agents.

The recognition that many carcinogens require metabolic activation to reactive electrophiles led to the development of assays that combine mammalian metabolic systems with a variety of these sensitive bacterial strains. It has become increasingly apparent, however, that tests that include mammalian metabolic systems are subject to limitations imposed by the particular metabolizing systems used (for a discussion of metabolizing systems see Report 10, p. 277). It is therefore likely that in using bacterial test results to predict a carcinogenic/mutagenic risk to mammals most problems arise from the metabolizing systems rather than from the genetic endpoint. In most test systems, a $9 0 0 0 \texttt { x g }$ supernatant (s9) fraction from animals (rats) treated with an enzyme inducer is used. It is highly desirable, wherever possible, to corroborate results using an alternative metabolizing system.

The predictive value of these tests, as for all short-term tests, is being established empirically, using for the most part data from carcinogenicity tests in experimental animals. The relevance of results from bacterial tests for mammals is thus being established largely by a comparison of how well the results from short-term tests agree with those that show chemicals to be either positive or negative in animal carcinogenicity tests.

Since carcinogenicity tests in animals have limitations in their ability to predict carcinogenicity in humans (see Report 1, p. 21), this validation process must be an imperfect one, and some degree of noncorrelation is therefore to be expected. When results do not correlate, it must be decided whether it is the short-term test or the animal carcinogenicity test that is incorrect. There is considerable variation in the extent to which the various test systems have been validated by comparison with results of long-term animal carcinogenicity studies; nevertheless, in those cases where detailed studies have been made, there was qualitative correlation between results obtained from animals and those obtained from bacterial and the other short-term tests.

The amount of available information on heritable mutagenic effects in mammals is relatively small. Thus, even though this criterion cannot at present play an important role in the evaluation of results from shortterm tests, certain categories of genetic damage may turn out to be important in causing heritable effects, and it might become an important factor in the selection of short-term tests.

The Working Group was aware of several validation studies in progress, the results of which may provide further data in relation to the recommendations made herein. In the following discussion, several of the most commonly used bacterial assays are outlined (for other reviews, see Hollstein et al., 1979, and Norpoth & Garner, 1980).

# 2. CRITERIA FOR SELECTING TESTS AND INTERPRETING RESULTS

A number of criteria are important in evaluating the usefulness of the various tests. A decision to use one test, or a combination of several tests, should rely on a compromise, taking into account the various criteria. The importance given to each criterion may depend on the general purpose of the testing being done; for example, extreme rapidity might be preferred in purifying biologically active compounds from mixtures, even if this test is not valid for many other compounds. Two other criteria should always be considered before selecting a test: these are 'quantitation' and 'present experience'. 'Quantitation' refers to a reproducible dose-response effect and is an essential condition of any reliable test. 'Present experience' refers to how many chemicals (including carcinogens, noncarcinogens and chemicals of unknown carcinogenicity) and how many chemical classes have been tested, how many laboratories are using the test, and for how many years the test has been used. The complexity of the variables involved in doing even the simplest biological test necessitates a certain degree of pragmatic evaluation over time with a variety of chemicals and laboratories. However, these criteria are only one element among others discussed below and should not be taken by themselves as a complete measure of the usefulness of the test.

Other technical criteria are:

(1) reproducibility in the same laboratory (a measure of the assay) and reproducibility between laboratories;

2 sensitivity, on the basis of three parameters: (a) the minimum dose that will give a positive response, (b) the possible range of variation of the minimal to maximal response, and (c) the maximum dose level that can be tested. The maximal obtainable response may be limited by nongenetic toxic effects on the cells. Differences in sensitivity of bacterial tests may be due to several factors: permeability, differences in repair, experimental procedures (e.g., the fluctuation test as compared with the plate test);

(3) rapidity, including how long it takes to do a single experiment and how many experiments (including controls and replicate testing) are needed for a complete test of a chemical. The bacteria used in these tests have very short generation times (of the order of an hour or even less), and the various tests take little time;

(4) manpower and cost: the range of costs of the various bacterial tests is small. Cost considerations should not be made at the expense of quality. In many tests, omission of a single control or lack of replicate

testing can completely invalidate results, and there is a certain level of complexity below which one should not go for the sake of economy;

(5) complexity: bacterial assays are relatively easy to conduct, but they require a certain level of technical expertise and cannot be reduced to simple routine procedures;

(6) insight into basic mechanisms: bacteria are particularly well suited to detailed molecular biological analysis, and a number of the bacterial screening tests are flexible enough to permit an evaluation of DNA-damaging potential, for example, by using isogenic strains to analyse different mutational effects of a compound. A battery of bacterial tests can also be designed to detect different types of genetic damage;

(7) protocols (degree of standardization): although well-defined protocols and appropriate internal controls are crucial for obtaining reliable results from screening programmes, the methods should never be interpreted as reducible to a recipe. Nevertheless, for each test (and/or each class of chemicals) there should be a well-recognized procedure for carrying out the assay which has been used to test a reasonable number of chemicals.

# 3.1 Introduction

The section which follows describes in some detail the scientific basis and practical application of three classes of genetic tests: (i) mutation, (ii) DNA-repair and (iii) induction of lysogenic bacteria. Experience has shown that each class of test has certain strengths and weaknesses and that combinations of tests may constitute the best use of bacterial test systems.

A mutation may be defined as a stable heritable change in a DNA nucleotide sequence, which may be detected as a phenotypic change. Heritable changes in nucleotide sequences can be due to base substitution (transitions, transversions), frameshifts (deletions or additions of one or a few base pairs), large deletions, insertions and translocations. Few, if any, mutagens induce only one type of mutational change: rather, most mutagenic agents tend to exhibit a characteristic mutational spectrum which depends upon (i) the nature of the primary DNA alteration (e.g., modification of base, phosphate or sugar, strand break, incorporation of modified base), and (ii) the subsequent secondary effects of DNA repair and replication. The same mutagen may therefore produce different mutational spectra in organisms of different genetic background.

In practice, mutations are assayed by methods which detect forward or reverse mutations. Forward mutations (e.g., from drug sensitivity to drug resistance) have the theoretical advantage of presenting a large . genetic target and should indicate many types of mutagenic effects (including deletions), all of which will be expressed in the same phenotype. However, a forward mutation system may fail to detect an increase in the induced frequency of a rare event against a background of common events.

Reverse mutation assays use bacteria that are already mutated at an easily.detectable locus (e.g., amino acid auxotrophy); the frequency with which the test chemical induces a second mutation which abolishes the effect of the pre-existing mutation is then determined. The genetic target presented to the test chemical is therefore small, specific and selective. Several indicator strains or a single strain with multiple markers may be necessary to overcome effects of mutagen specificity.

Several techniques can be used to measure mutational changes in bacteria, e.g., assays using liquid incubation (Malling, 1971), the agar overlay method, with or without liquid preincubation (Ames et al., 1975; Nagao et al., 1977; Yahagi et al., 1977) and the fluctuation test (Green et al., 1976). These assays approach the theoretical limit of sensitivity because neither a single spontaneous nor an induced mutation is scored as more than one event, so that the smallest possible increase over spontaneous mutation can be detected. For most purposes, the agar overlay procedure is far less laborious than the fluctuation test; however, the fluctuation test may offer advantages for special applications. For example, it has been used with intact hepatocytes as an alternative to activation by a liver S9 preparation, and it may be more effective in detecting some toxic, weakly mutagenic agents (Green et al., 1977).

# 3.3 Tests with strains of Escherichia coli wP2 in combination with S. typhimurium

E. coli wP2 has an ochre mutation in the tryptophan synthetase operon, and the test detects reversions due to a mutation at the structural gene and at a suppressor locus. A series of derivatives altered with regard to DNA repair are available; the differential mutagenic response of these strains gives an indication of the type of DNA damage induced by the test compound and, in some cases, allows confirmation of a weak response. # 1.6 Tests using induction and mutagenesis of prophage lambd

Most bacteria, if not all, are lysogenic: they carry in their genome the DNA of a virus (phage) in a dormant state (prophage). Phage lambda, whose host is Escherichia coli Kl2, is one virus that has been most studied (Hershey, 1971).

The dormant state of prophage lambda is maintained by a 'repressor' protein which is the product of the prophage cI gene. The repressor prevents transcription of. the prophage genes by binding to the two operator sites flanking the cI gene.

DNA damage, produced by various agents, triggers the development of the prophage into vegetative phage (prophage induction). Prophage induction is caused by inactivation of the repressor synthesized by the dormant prophage. As soon as there is no repressor left in the cell, the prophage develops. Within 60 minutes, the host cell lyses and releases about 100 phage particles. When induced cells are mixed with indicator bacteria and plated on nutrient agar plates, the viruses released from the induced lysogenic cells lyse the indicator bacteria. An induced lysogenic cell is therefore recorded as a plaque on a lawn of indicator bacteria. The efficiency of lysogenic induction is indicated by the ratio of the number of plaques formed to the number of lysogenic cells present before exposure to the inducing treatment.

The use of prophage induction as a screening method to detect mutagens and potential carcinogens was suggested as early as 1953 by Andre Lwoff and was reviewed in 1971 (Heinemann, 1971). New tester strains have been developed by introducing into an E. coli K12 lysogen an envA mutation that increases permeability to many mutagens and an uvrB mutation that inactivates the excision repair system (Moreau et al., 1976). Procedures (including incorporation of a mammalian metabolic activation system) have been defined for testing for lysogenic induction (the 'inductest').

In order to detect lambda mutations ('lambda mutatest') (Moreau & Devoret, 1977), other bacterial tester strains carrying a modified prophage were constructed in which forward mutations occur at the operator regions of the modified prophage. The prophage becomes insensitive to its repressor, and infective centres are formed which produce phages that are spilt onto a lawn of indicator bacteria on which the parental phage cannot grow. Using the combined protocol, the mutagenic and inducing activities of aflatoxin B1, ethylmethane sulphonate and methylmethane sulphonate were tested in the same cell type (Moreau & Devoret, 1977).

The inductest has certain advantages over mutagenicity tests: mutations can be scored only in bacteria that survive exposure to a chemical treatment, which is often toxic as well as mutagenic, and no more than one bacterium out of a thousand is likely to be detected, e.g., as a histidine revertant, in the Salmonella system. In contrast, prophage induction can be observed, when it takes place, in the bulk of a cell population. Prophage induction is a mass effect, largely independent of whether or not cells would survive the chemical treatment; a cell that is induced to produce a progeny of phage would die in any case. The inductest can therefore continue to give a positive response for a highly toxic potential carcinogen at dose levels that would kill tester bacteria in a mutagenicity test.

The fact that the induction process occurs in a large proportion of cells permitted Levine et al. (1978), Elesperu & Yarmolinsky (1979) and Toman et al. (1980) to design a biochemical assay for prophage induction. In a bacterial strain in which, for instance, the gene for the enzyme galactokinase is linked to the lambda DNA in such a way that the lambda repressor blocks synthesis of the enzyme, the repressor is cleaved and the enzyme is synthesized; the enzyme activity thus reveals DNA damage. This biochemical assay may be the most rapid of the short-term tests: it takes only half a day to test for a potential carcinogen.

Although neither the inductest nor the lambda mutatest has yet been validated as extensively as the mutagenicity tests, tests using lysogenic bacteria provide valuable complementary assays. Perhaps more important in the long run, a biological system comprising both a cell and a dormant virus should be a valuable tool for analysing the various lesions imposed on DNA by carcinogens.

# 3.7 DNA-repair tests

These assays use isogenic DNA repair-deficient and DNA repair-proficient bacterial strains in which modification of DNA or genotoxic effects are scored as the preferential killing of the DNA repair-deficient strain. The assays can be run in several modes, involving either agar diffusion or liquid suspension procedures. The routine screening tests use broad-spectrum, DNA repair-deficient strains, i.e., polA, recA, uvrA/ recA; the use of mutants with specific deficiencies in DNA repair pathways can also provide information about the nature of the DNA lesion. Protocols using E. coli, Bacillus subtilis, Proteus mirabilis and S. typhimurium strains have been published (Adler et al., 1976; Ames et al., 1975; Kada et al., 1972; Rosenkranz et al., 1976).

Results from the testing of approximately l000 chemicals in one or more of these assays have been reported. In several studies, chemicals were tested simultaneously in DNA repair-deficient strains and in the Salmonella mutagenicity assay, and these results showed a correlation with those of carcinogenicity tests in animals (Poirier & De Serres, 1979; Rosenkranz & Poirier, 1979): $7 9 \%$ of 99 carcinogens tested preferentially inhibited the DNA repair-deficient E. coli pol $\mathtt { A } ^ { - }$ strain; in parallel mutagenesis tests using Salmonella strains, $6 5 \%$ were positive; $9 2 \%$ of the carcinogens were detected by a combination of the two procedures; and $5 9 \%$ of the carcinogens were active in both assays.

The major problems with these assays relate to the disc-diffusion procedure. In this mode, poorly diffusible chemicals give uninterpretable results, and metabolic activation is inadequate. These disadvantages can be overcome by use of liquid suspension assay procedures (as in the studies quoted above).

These assays have been used to test environmental mixtures, but there is no report of their use for the detection of substances in urine or in the host-mediated assay. Strains with greater permeability have become available", and their increased use may be useful.

# 4. GENERAL DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

# 4.1 Experimental design and evaluation of data

A satisfactory experimental protocol for bacterial mutagenicity tests must include a range of doses. For inactive chemicals, the highest dose tested should be the limit of solubility or toxicity (see also Report 10, p.277). The occurrence of extremely low levels of impurities should be

taken into consideration when testing chemicals at very high doses (see, e.g., Donahue et al., 1978). Therefore, whenever possible the chemical compound tested must be clearly characterized, i.e., its Chemical Abstracts Service number, its chemical structure, its purity and the source from which it was obtained should be specified.

The description of the experimental protocol should be detailed enough to allow independent repetition of the test. Experiments should be.of sufficient scale to allow adequate statistical analysis, and any test should be capable of providing reproducible dose-response curves susceptible to appropriate statistical analysis. Some statistical analysis of the data should be made (see Ehrenberg, 1977; Katz, 1979; Norpoth, 1978; Weinstein & Lewinson, 1978); however, it is also important to provide the 'raw' data, to enable others to make their own analyses. At the least, mean values (including the number of replicate plates used to calculate the mean) and indications of variability (for example, standard deviation) of plate counts for the negative control, the positive controls and each dose of the test compound should also be indicated. The amounts of chemical used should be expressed in weight or in molarity and not in volume.

Results should not be expressed as '+' or '-'. Nor should they be expressed as transformed data, i.e., as percentages, by subtracting spontaneous counts, or expressed as revertants per nanogram or nanomole or as a ratio of colonies on treated and control plates. Such transformations make independent evaluation impossible.

A positive result obtained in several tests, preferably with different endpoints, is of much greater value in establishing the DNA-damaging capacity of a compound than a result obtained in one test alone. In this way, advantage can be taken of the unique characteristics of the different bacterial tests.

# 4.2 Detection of DNA-damaging agents

Short-term test systems using specially designed bacterial strains proyide a powerful tool for detecting chemicals that can interact with DNA or modify its metabolism. They also allow characterization of the type of interactions and elucidation of their biological consequences at the molecular level. At present, it is theoretically possible to detect almost any type of DNA-damaging or otherwise mutagenic agent by using a battery of several bacterial tester strains. There are, however, some intrinsic limitations: for example, there are chemicals, such as mitotic spindle poisons and hormones, that are likely to produce genetic or stable epigenetic effects in mammalian cells through mechanisms that are not present in bacteria.

Addition of appropriate mammalian metabolizing systems (e.g., S9 fractions, hepatocytes) to these bacterial test systems greatly enhances the relevance of the results to higher organisms. Even so, extrapolation of results of bacterial tests to predict results in mammals is hazardous and involves many factors. For example, metabolic differences may exist between the target cells or between the in vivo and the in vitro metabolic activation and detoxification systems (Some of the limitations due to the use of the subcellular metabolic activation system can be overcome by using complementary assays based on eukaryotic cells). In addition, pharmacokinetic factors render correlations, especially quantitative ones, difficult (see Report 10, p. 277).

These tests can also be used as a biological endpoint for the preliminary investigation of complex mixtures and as bioassays for isolation of their mutagenic components. Such complex mixtures include body fluids, excreta, water, food, effluents and air samples. They can also be used to detect impurities in drugs or industrial chemicals.

The specific biological consequences that are measured as endpoints in these short-term tests are mutations, lethal effects and prophage induction. The detailed mechanisms of these phenomena at the molecular level are beginning to be understood. Since the primary DNA lesions are acted upon by various DNA repair systems (pre- and post-replicative, error-free and error-prone), several consequences of the same initial interactions are possible; although the final genetic consequences depend upon the genetic backgound of the organism.

# 4.3 Extrapolation of results

There is a qualitative correlation between results in bacterial short-term tests and those in animal carcinogenicity tests. However, it is much less certain whether a quantitative correlation can be made, and therefore a strong positive response in these tests does not necessarily indicate that the substance would have a strong carcinogenic effect. The converse is also true: a potent carcinogen would not necessarily be a potent mutagen.

The carcinogenic potency of chemicals in animal carcinogenicity tests varies by more than a million-fold (Anes & Hooper, 1978; Meselson & Russell, 1977); and there is a similarly wide range of mutagenic activity of chemicals in some short-term tests (Clive, 1977; McCann & Ames, 1977). Determination of the relationships between the potency of chemicals in short-term tests, in animal carcinogenicity tests, in tests in animals for heritable effects and, when possible, in humans, will be of importance in developing methods to assess human risk on the basis of these various sources of data. Some exploratory work on correlations between carcino

genic potency and mutagenic potency in the Salmonella/microsome test (Ames, 1979; Meselson & Russell, 1977) and in the mouse lymphoma test (Clive et al., 1979) are encouraging. However, there is not likely to be a perfect correlation, and it will be important to identify classes of chemicals for which there may be some correlation as well as classes for which no correlation exists (see, e.g., Bartsch, 1980).

# 1. INTRODUCTION

A number of 'short-term' tests have been developed for the detection of environmental mutagens/carcinogens. These employ a variety of organisms, from bacteriophages and bacteria to mammalian cells, together with the appropriate metabolic activation system. Genetic responses are measured by diverse biological endpoints.

Several experimental systems have been developed to measure speciric locus mutations in mammalian cells in culture. The most commonly used genetic markers are resistance to 8-azaguanine, 6-thioguanine and ouabain in V79 and CHO Chinese hamster cells, and to 5-bromo-2'-deoxyuridine (BUdR) in L5178Y mouse cells. The mutagenesis assay using mammalian cells has been reviewed by several workers (Abbondandolo, 1977; Arlett, 1977; DeMars, 1974; Siminovitch, 1976; Thompson & Baker, 1973). The purpose of the present report is to assess the relevance of this assay system to detect carcinogens.
