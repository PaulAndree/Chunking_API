# The extreme mutator effect of Escherichia coli mutD5 results from saturation of mismatch repair by excessive DNA replication errors

# Roel M.Schaaper and Miroslav Radman

Laboratory of Molecular Genetics, National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, PO Box 12233, Research Triangle Park, NC 27709, USA

$^ { 1 } { \mathrm { O n } }$ leave of absence from the Institut Jacques Monod, 75251-Paris Cedex 05, France

Communicated by M.Radman

Escherichia coli mutator mutD5 is the most potent mutator known. The mutD5 mutation resides in the dnaQ gene encoding the proofreading exonuclease of DNA polymerase III holoenzyme. It has recently been shown that the extreme mutability of this strain results, in addition to a proofreading defect, from a defect in mutH,L,S-encoded postreplicational DNA mismatch repair. The following measurements of the mismatchrepair capacity of mutD5 cells demonstrate that this mismatch-repair defect is not structural, but transient. mutD5 cells in early log phase are as deficient in mismatch repair as mutL cells, but they become as proficient as wild-type cells in late log phase. Second, arrest of chromosomal replication in a mutD5-dnaA(Ts) strain at a nonpermissive temperature restores mismatch repair, even from the early log phase of growth. Third, transformation of mutD5 strains with multicopy plasmids expressing the mutH or mutL gene restores mismatch repair, even in rapidly growing cells, These observations suggest that the mismatch-repair deficiency of mutD strains results from a saturation of the mutHLSmismatch-repair system by an excess of primary DNA replication errors due to the proofreading defect.

Key words: bacteriophage M13mp2/DNA replication errors/exonucleolytic proofreading/mutHLS-dependent DNA mismatch repair/transfection of heteroduplex DNA

# Introduction

Three major enzymatic mechanisms appear to control the fidelity of DNA replication: (i) base selection by the DNA polymerase (and associated proteins) (for review, see Loeb and Kunkel, 1982), (ii) $3 ^ { \prime } \to 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonucleolytic proofreading associated with the replication machinery (for review, see Kunkel, 1988), and (iii) postreplicative mismatch correction which, at least in Escherichia coli, is directed to the newly synthesized strands by the transient undermethylation of adenines in GATC sequences (for reviews, see Radman and Wagner, 1986; Claverys and Lacks, 1986; Modrich, 1987).

The diverse E. coli mutator strains (Cox, 1976) are highly revealing as to the molecular strategies that ensure the replication of bacterial DNA with high fidelity. Mutator mutations mapping in the dnaE gene (Sevastopoulos and Glaser, 1977), encoding the polymerase subunit of DNA polymerase III holoenzyme, the enzyme responsible for the replication of the E.coli chromosome (for review, see McHenry, 1988), probably affect the selection of the correct dNTP substrates. The most potent known mutator (mutation frequencies increased $1 0 ^ { 4 } .$ to $1 0 ^ { 5 }$ foldis E.coli mutD5 (Degnen and Cox, 1974; Cox, 1976). Its defect has been localized to the dnaQ gene, encoding the proofreading $3 ^ { \prime }  5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease (e-subunit) of polIII holoenzyme (Echols et al. , 1983; Maruyama et al., 1983; Scheuermann et al., 1983; DiFrancesco et al. , 1984; Scheuermann and Echols, 1984; Cox and Horner, 1986). The mutH, mutL, mutS, mutU and dam mutators all affect postreplicative methyl-directed mismatch repair (Radman and Wagner, 1986; Claverys and Lacks, 1986; Modrich, 1987).

On the basis of spontaneous mutation rates in these mutator mutants, one can attempt to estimate the contribution of each error-avoidance or error-correction mechanism to overall DNA replication fidelity. In E.coli, this fidelity is about $1 0 ^ { - 1 0 } - \dot { 1 } 0 ^ { - 9 }$ errors per replicated nucleotide (Drake. 1969). However, a difficulty is encountered when combining the estimated base-insertion fidelity $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ (for review, see Loeb and Kunkel, 1982) with the estimated factors for proofreading $( 1 0 ^ { 4 } - 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ , implied by the mutD5 mutator effectand mismatch repair $( 1 0 ^ { 2 } - \mathbf { \bar { 1 0 } } ^ { 3 }$ , implied from the mutHLS mutator effect) (Radman and Wagner, 1986; Schaaper and Dunn, $1 9 8 7 a )$ . When all factors are combined, too high a fidelity $( 1 0 ^ { - 1 1 } - 1 0 ^ { - 1 3 } )$ is predicted.

A clue to resolving this paradox came from observations that mutD cells, in addition to being defective in proofreading, were also defective in postreplicative mismatch repair (Radman et al., 1981; Schaaper, 1988, 1989; Damagnez et al, 1989). Thus, only part of the $1 0 ^ { 4 } .$ -to $1 0 ^ { 5 }$ -fold mutator effect of mutD might represent the effect of proofreading, consistent with (indirect) measurements and theoretical considerations suggesting an upper limit to the contribution of proofreading to fidelity of about 200-fold (Fersht et al. , 1982).

In this report, we address the question of how a structural defect in proofreading exonuclease can lead to a defect in mismatch repair, a process that is spatially and temporally removed from DNA polymerization. Our data show that the mismatch-repair defect in mutD cells is transient and reversible, suggesting that it results when mismatch repair is temporarily overwhelmed by a high level of DNA replication errors.

# Results

# Assay of mismatch repair

To test the hypothesis that mutD cells might suffer from saturation of the mismatch-repair system, we directly measured their mismatch-repair capacity by transfecting them with heteroduplex $\mathsf { M } 1 3 \mathsf { m p } 2$ DNA and analyzing the resulting infective centers. Two types of heteroduplexes were used, one containing a T·G (transition) mismatch and the other containing a T· T (transversion) mismatch at the same site (Figure 1). (Transitions are well recognized by the MutH,L,S,U system, transversions less efficiently.) The mismatches are located in the lacZα gene of $\mathbf { M } 1 3 \mathbf { m p } 2$ , the viral (plus) strand carrying a TAA ochre codon which abolishes $\pmb { \alpha }$ -complementation (producing a white plaque), and the complementary (minus) strand carrying (the complement of) a CAA or AAA codon, either of which restores $\pmb { \alpha }$ -complementation (producing a blue plaque). Thus, the progeny of the two strands can be distinguished by visually inspecting the plaques that they produce. If no repair of the heteroduplex takes place prior to replication, a 'mixed' (blue/white) plaque will result containing both types of phage. Repair of the mismatch prior to replication will yield a pure plaque, either blue or white depending on which strand is repaired. The heteroduplexes are hemi-methylated (adenine methylation at GATC sites) and, as a consequence, the methyl-directed mutHLSU-mismatch repair system will preferentially repair the non-methylated (i.e. complementary) strand, producing an excess of white plaques (see below).

# Mismatch repair in mutD mutator at different growth phases

Our first test for whether the mismatch-repair deficiency of mutD strains results from overloading the mismatch-repair system was to measure mismatch-repair at different stages of a culture's growth curve. If the repair deficiency results from saturating one or more components of the mismatchrepair system, the effect might be most pronounced in rapidly growing cells. In slowly dividing or resting cells, the deficiency may be reduced or even abolished. We converted mutD, wild-type and mutL cells in rich medium into competent cells at early log phase $( 0 . 6 - 1 . 0 \times 1 0 ^ { 8 }$ cells/ml; doubling time $2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n } )$ and at late log phase $( 0 . 7 - 1 . 1 \ \times$ 10° cells/ml; doubling time \~55 min).

Transfection of the $\mathbf { T } { \cdot } \mathbf { G }$ and $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { T }$ heteroduplexes revealed the following (Table I). In early log phase, the wild-type strain repairs the $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { G }$ transition mismatch efficiently and the ${ \bf T } \cdot { \bf T }$ transversion mismatch poorly, as evidenced by low $( 1 . 7 \% )$ and high $( 3 3 . 6 \% )$ percentages of mixed infective centers, respectively. This result is consistent with the established specificity of the MutHLS repair system (Kramer et al., 1984; Choy and Fowler, 1985; Dohet et al., 1985; Su et al. , 1988), as is the preferential repair towards the white-plaque phenotype $( 8 8 . 5 \%$ for the $\bar { \mathbf { T } } { \cdot } \mathbf { G }$ mispair). In contrast, the mutD and mutL strains (the latter known to be deficient in mismatch repair) display high percentages $( 4 1 . 7 - 5 6 . 1 \% )$ of mixed plaques for both mismatches. It is clear that under these conditions of rapid growth, the mutD strain is severely compromised in its capacity for mismatch repair.

In late log phase, however, the mutD strain appears to have regained full mismatch-repair capacity. This is most clearly seen with the $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { G }$ mismatch, the percentage of mixed bursts being reduced to $9 . 2 \%$ which is very close to that of the wild-type strain $( 7 . 6 \% )$ under the same conditions. This difference between early and late log phase cells reflects a difference in mismatch repair rather than, for example, strand loss or asymmetry of M13 replication, because no change is observed with the mutL strain, and the effect is observed for the well repairable $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { G }$ mismatch but much less so for the poorly repairable $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { T }$ transversion mismatch. This experiment suggests that the mismatch-repair deficiency of mutD cells in rich medium is reversed upon progression of the cells from fast growth to slow growth, supportive of the hypothesis of saturated mismatch repair.

# Cessation of DNA replication in a mutDdnaA(Ts) strain restores mismatch repair

When cells enter late log phase, aspects of physiology apart from the rate of DNA replication change. To address the possibility that such physiological changes, and not cessation of DNA replicatoin per se cause restoration of mismatch repair directed by MutHLSU, we examined the effects of a replication block in early log phase. We measured mismatch repair in a mutDdnaA(Ts) strain, which allows DNA replication to be halted even in cells in early log phase by shifting to a nonpermissive temperature. A shift to from $3 2 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ to $4 2 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ will prevent the initiation of DNA replication but allow continued DNA synthesis at existing replication forks, such that after $4 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ DNA replication has ceased (von Meyenburg and Hansen, 1987).

This experiment, using the $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { G }$ heteroduplex, revealed (Table II) that temperature shift by itself has no effect on the ability of either the wild-type or a dnaA(Ts) strain to perform mismatch repair $( \sim 1 \%$ mixed plaques). (For reasons unknown, the W3110 and AB1157 backgrounds are more efficient in mismatch repair than the KA796 background used in the experiments of Table I and the rest of the paper.) At the permissive temperature, the mutD and mutDdnaA(Ts) strains are substantially impaired, producing $1 7 . 4 \%$ mixed bursts. At the nonpermissive temperature, however, the mutDdnaA(Ts) strain has fully recovered its capacity for mismatch repair (only $1 . 1 \%$ mixed plaques). A smaller effect is also observed for the mutD strain itself. This effect has been observed a number of times and may reflect the presence of other temperature-sensitive mutations that are likely to accumulate in strong mutators. At any rate, the difference between the mutD $( 6 . 9 \% )$ and the mutDdnaA $( 1 . 1 \% )$ data for mixed burst is highly significant $( P <$ 0.0005 using a $x ^ { 2 }$ test).

The results of this experiment demonstrate the restoration of mismatch repair in mutD strains. They imply that stopping DNA replication is sufficient to produce this effect, and so support the mismatch-repair saturation model. We thus hypothesize that one or more components of the MutHLS system are present in limiting amounts and become depleted in actively replicating mutD cells. If so, providing extra amounts of these gene products is expected to cure the deficiency.

# Effect of multicopy plasmids bearing the mutH+ , mutL \*, mutS+ or uvrD+ genes on the mutD mismatch-repair deficiency

The mutH, mutL and mutS genes have been cloned from Salmonella typhimurium and shown to complement the corresponding deficiencies in E.coli (Pang et al., 1984, 1985). The E.coli uvrD gene (also called mutU) encodes DNA helicase II and has been cloned (Taucher-Scholz and Hoffmann-Berling, 1983). To test the hypothesis that the proteins of one or more of these genes might be limiting, we introduced plasmid pBR322-based clones of each into the mutD5 strain and measured mismatch repair. In two representative experiments (Table III), plasmids carrying either the mutH\* or the mutL+ gene restored mismatch repair in the mutD strain, as indicated by the reduction in the percentage of mixed plaques to the wild-type level. To confirm the identity of the transforming plasmids, we re-isolated plasmid DNAs from these transformants, re-introduced each into strains NR3939 (mutH101), NR3940 (mutL101) and NR3996 (mutS101), and tested the mutator activity of the transformants. These experiments (data not shown) confirmed the mutH\*, mutL\* or $m u t S ^ { + }$ identity of each of the plasmids. Ampicillinsensitive segregants were obtained from the mutD clone bearing the plasmid containing mutL+, and tested for mismatch repair. These segregants were as deficient as the initial mutD strain (Table III). Thus, mismatch repair can be restored even in rapidly dividing mutD cells by providing MutL or MutH functions.

# Discussion

The possibility that mismatch repair can be saturated has beer suggested by a number of observations. In Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumococccal transformation, apparent loss of mismatch repair (detected by the efficiency of transformation with a defined marker) occurred when cotransforming with DNA of the same species but of different origin (Guild and Schoemaker, 1974; Tiraby et al. , 1975). No such effect was observed with 'carrier' DNA of the same organism or with heterologous DNA of Bacillus subtilis. In E.coli, the shapes of mutation-induction curves for mutagens whose mutagenic intermediates are subject to mismatch repair (e.g. 5-bromouracil, 2-aminopurine, 9-aminoacridine and some ellipticines) also suggest that saturation of mismatch repair may occur (Rydberg, 1977; Skopek and Hutchinson, 1984; René et al., 1988). An apparent defect in mismatch repair of phage lambda heteroduplex DNA was observed in E.coli mutD5 in a preliminary study by Radman et al. (1981), who suggested that this deficiency might be due to saturation of mismatch repair by excessive DNA replication errors. Analysis of the mutational specificity of mutD5 in minimal medium (in which the mutator effect is 102- to 103-fold) and in rich medium (104- to 105-fold mutator effect) further suggested a mismatch-repair deficiency in rich medium: transversion mutations predominate in minimal medium, but transitions predominate in rich medium (Schaaper, 1988). This result is consistent with a loss of mismatch repair in rich medium because mismatch repair shows a net preference of correction of transition errors (G· T and A · C) over transversion errors purine $\cdot$ purine and pyrimidine $\cdot$ pyrimidine mispairs)

(Radman and Wagner, 1986; Claverys and Lacks, 1986; Modrich, 1987), and the loss of this repair system will specifically increase the contribution of transition mutations. Finally, recent experiments with heteroduplex DNA of phages M13 (Schaaper, 1988, 1989) and lambda (Damagnez et al. , 1989) directly demonstrated that mutD5 is defective in mismatch repair when grown in rich medium, but not in minimum medium (Schaaper, 1988).

The present work elucidates the mechanism by which mutD5 strains become deficient in mismatch repair. Since the mutD5 mutation affects the proofreading $3 ^ { \prime } \to 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease activity of the e subunit of the Pol III holoenzyme (Scheuermann and Echols, 1984), the simplest hypothesis is that this exonuclease is required for methyldirected mismatch repair. This hypothesis is supported by the demonstrated requirement for Pol III holoenzyme in mismatch repair (Au et al., 1988). Alternatively, the proofreading deficiency in rich medium may create a high level of replication errors, overwhelming mismatch repair and provoking an error catastrophy.

Our data, describing the restorability of mismatch repair in mutD cells, are difficult to reconcile with the first hypothesis, but strongly support the hypothesis of mismatchrepair saturation. Cessation of DNA replication in mutD cells, either by transition of the cells into stationary phase or by preventing actively dividing cells from initiating new rounds of replication, restores mismatch repair. This fits one tenet of the saturation model, namely, that the active generation of DNA replication errors (because of the proofreading defect) disables the repair system. Second, even in rapidly dividing mutD cells, mismatch repair can be restored by multicopy plasmids carrying mutH + or mutL+ genes. This also fits the saturation model because disabling mismatch repair may occur by depleting of one (or more) component(s) of the MutHLS system.

The observation that plasmids containing either the mutH+ or the mutL + gene can restore mismatch correction is intriguing. In the simplest model, one would expect restoration to require the addition of only one specific component or, perhaps, the simultaneous addition of two (or more) jointly rate-limiting components. Direct substitution of the mutH\* for the mutL+ gene product or vice versa is highly unlikely since single mutations in either mutH or mutL genes produce complete defects in mismatch repair (Radman and Wagner, 1986; Modrich, 1987). We noted that in about one-half of our experiments, the plasmid containing mutL† proved more effective than the plasmid containing mutH+ in reducing mutD mutator activity (e.g. Table IV, experiment 1), a result paralleled by a slightly more efficient restoration of mismatch repair in the direct measurements (Table III, experiment 1). This suggests to us that depletion and/or restoration of mismatch repair may occur at more than one level or by more than one mechanism, which may differentially involve the MutH and MutL functions.

To obtain more insights into the mechanism(s) underlying this phenomenon, it will be necessary to study the regulation and expression of the MutH,L,S,U system. For instance, positive control of the mutH gene by the MutL protein, or of the mutL gene by the MutH protein, might account for one of our observations. It will also be important to test whether the MutH and MutL proteins may be at least partially interchangeable, as can be postulated within models involving protein polymerization along the DNA to achieve signal transduction from the mismatch to the GATC site (Modrich, 1987). Finally, it will be interesting to determine whether some of the Mut proteins are used only once and then discarded, rather than used catalytically.

Whatever the molecular mechanism for saturating mismatch repair, our analysis elucidates the mechanism of the most potent genetic mutator effect by providing evidence that a high error rate at one step of DNA synthesis can overwhelm the capacity of a subsequent error-correction system, causing a dramatic increase in spontaneous mutagenesis.

# Materials and methods

# Bacterial strains and plasmids

E.coli strains KA796 (ara, thi. Δprolac), NR9163 (ara, thi, Δprolac, mutL::Tn10) and NR9066 (ara, thi, ∆prolac, trpE, zaf13::Tn10-mutD5) have been described (Schaaper, 1988). Strain W3110 $( \mathsf { F } ^ { - } , s u ^ { - } )$ was from M.Meselson's collection. Its dnaA46(Ts) derivative was constructed by P1-mediated transduction using the tetracycline resistance provided by the adjacent tnaA: $\mathrm { T n } I O$ marker (obtained from R.D'Ari, University of Paris), followed by testing for the Ts-property. Strain KD1087 (thr, leu, arg, his, trpAB, tonB, spcA, mutD5) was obtained from E.C.Cox (Princeton University). Its dnaA46 derivative was constructed as described above for W3110. Plasmids pGW1899, pGW1842, and pGW1811 containing, respectively, the mutH, mutL and mutS genes of S.tryphimurium (Pang et al. , 1985), were obtained from G.Walker (Massachusetts Institute of Technology). Plasmid pGT26, carrying the E. coli uvrD gene (TaucherScholz and Hoffmann-Berling, 1983), was obtained from S.Matson (University of North Carolina). These plasmids were introduced into strain NR9066 (mutD5) by transforming calcium-treated cells and selecting for ampicillin-resistant colonies on minimal-medium plates with $1 0 0 \ \mu \mathrm { g / m l }$ ampicillin. Strains NR3939 (mutH101), NR3940 (mutL101) and NR3996 (murS101) have been described (Schaaper and Dunn, 1987a). Strain CSH50 (ara, thi, strA, Δprolac, F'prolacZΔM15), used for $\pmb { \alpha } \cdot$ -complementation by phage M13mp2 and its derivatives, has been described (Kunkel, 1984).

# Media

LB media were as described (Miller, 1972). Minimal media contained 1XVB salts (Vogel and Bonner, 1956), $0 . 2 \%$ glucose, $\pmb { 5 \mu \mathrm { g } } / \mathrm { m l }$ thiamine and, where required, $5 0 ~ \mu \mathbf { g } / \mathbf { m } ]$ amino acids. Solid media contained, in addition, $1 . 5 \%$ agar. SOB broth, used to grow cells prior to their conversion to competency was as described by Hanahan (1983).

# M13mp2 heteroduplex DNA

Bacteriophage M13mp2(T90) (Kunkel, 1984) contains a $\mathtt { G } \mathtt { T }$ base substitution at position 90 of the lacZα gene (Kunkel, 1984) and is unable to perform $\pmb { \alpha }$ -complementation because of the resulting ochre codon. Phages $\mathbf { m } \mathbf { p } 2 ( \mathbf { C } 9 0 )$ and $\mathfrak { m p } 2 ( { \mathsf { A } } 9 0 )$ , containing a C or A at position 90, respectively, are pseudo wild-type revertants having regained the ability to perform α- complementation (Schaaper and Dunn, 1987b). Single-stranded DNA of phage $\scriptstyle { \mathfrak { m p 2 } } ( { \mathrm { T } } 9 0 )$ was prepared from virions grown on host CSH50 $( d a m ^ { + } )$ M13 RF1 DNA of the two revertant phages was prepared from phage-infected cultures of strain NR3745 (dam\~) (Glickman, 1979) by the method of Birmboim (1983), followed by further purification by CsCl/ethidium bromide ultracentrifugation. The RFI DNAs were linearized with Avall, which cuts M13mp2 only once, to yield RFII DNAs. Heteroduplexes containing either a $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { G }$ or $\mathbf { T } \cdot \mathbf { T }$ mismatch at position 90 were then produced by denaturation/renaturation of mixtures of $\mathbf { m } \mathbf { p } 2 ( \mathbf { T } \mathbf { 9 } 0 )$ single-stranded DNA with either mp2(C90) or mp2(A90) RFI DNA (10: 1 excess of single-stranded DNA), using the procedure of Kunkel and Soni (1988). The excess of single-stranded DNA was extracted with benzoylated-naphtholyated-DEAE cellulose (Gamper er al., 1980).

# Heteroduplex transfections

Competent cells were prepared by the method of Hanahan (1983) using SOB as growth medium. For transfections, a $\mathbf { 0 . 5 \mathrm { - } m l }$ aliquot of competent cells was incubated with $\sim 1 0 ~ \mathrm { n g }$ of heteroduplex DNA for $2 0 \mathrm { \ m i n }$ at $\textstyle \mathbf { 0 } ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ briefly heat-shocked $\mathfrak { s o s }$ at $4 2 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } _ { i }$ and returned to ice. Multiple 2-, 10-, and $5 0 \textmu \textmd { l }$ aliquots were then plated on minimal-medium plates in soft-agar layers containing $\mathbf { 0 . 5 \ m g }$ IPTG (isopropyl- $\cdot \beta \cdot$ -D-thiogalactoside), $1 . 0 \ : \mathrm { m g } { \bf \bar { X } } \cdot $ gal (5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indoly)- $\boldsymbol { \beta }$ D-galactopyranoside) and $0 . 2 \mathrm { \ m l }$ of a saturated culture of the $\pmb { \alpha }$ -complementation host CSH50. After incubation overnight at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ , plaques were scored as either pure white, pure blue or mixed white/blue.

# Mutagenesis experiments

In order to maintain low background mutation frequencies, all strains were kept on minimal-medium plates (containing ${ \bf 5 0 } \mu { \bf g } / \bf m l$ ampicillin for plasmidcontaining derivatives). For mutagenesis experiments, six to ten colonies for each strain were lightly touched by sterile toothpicks which were dropped in 1 ml LB broth (containing $5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { g / m l }$ ampicillin for plasmid-containing strains). After growth ovemight at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ in a shaking waterbath, the cultures were diluted in $0 . 9 \%$ NaCl and aliquots were plated on LB plates to determine the total cell yield, and on LB plates containing rifampicin $( 1 0 0 \mu \mathrm { g / m l } )$ or nalidixic acid $( 4 0 \mu \mathrm { g } / \mathrm { m l } )$ to determine the yield of antibiotic-resistant cells.
