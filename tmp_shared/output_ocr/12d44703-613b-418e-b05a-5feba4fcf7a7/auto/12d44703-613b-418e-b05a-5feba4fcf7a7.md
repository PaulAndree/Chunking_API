# INDUCTION OF A STABLE, HERITABLE EPIGENETIC CHANGEBY MUTAGENIC CARCINOGENS: A NEW TEST SYSTEM

Z. TOMAN, C. DAMBLY & M. RADMAN

Department of Molecular Biology, Free University of Brussels, Rhode-St-Genese, Belgium

# GENERAL INTRODUCTION

There is little doubt that cancer is a 'chromosomal disease', involving a stable change in the expression of the genetic material of a somatic cell (reviews: Cairns, 1978; German, 1974). Main support stems from observations of the stability and cellular heritability of the malignant character, i.e., the clonal nature of tumours, specific chromosomal markers in many malignancies, chromosomal markers and chromosomal instabilities in human cancer-prone syndromes (review: German, 1974) and specific chromosomal involvement in the suppression and expression of the malignant character (Rabinowitz & Sachs, 1970; Stanbridge, 1976).

Little is known, however, about the nature of the relevant chromosomal change; there are two groups of theories that have been put forward: 'genetic' and 'epigenetic' theories. 'Genetic' theories postulate one or more specific somatic mutations (changes in DNA nucleotide sequence) as the cause of cancer, whereas the 'epigenetic' theories postulate specific changes in the control of gene activity, with no change in nucleotide sequence (e.g., chemical modification of the DNA sequences involved in gene control, changes in the feedback loops of complex gene control, etc.). It is likely, on the other hand, that carcinogenesis requires the coincidence of both genetic and epigenetic changes. For example, carcinogenic initiation is probably due to a stable, heritable chromosomal change (perhaps a mutation) which remains unexpressed; carcinogenic promotion appears to be an epigenetic process, with eventual genetic consecuences (Kinsella & Radman, 1978), which transforms initiated cells into malignant cells. Initiators and solitary carcinogens are mutagenic, while the best-known tumour promoter, 12-0-tetradecanoyl-phorbol-13-acetate (TPA), is not

(review: Weinstein et al., 1979). For these reasons, a good shortterm carcinogenicity test should measure both genetic and epigenetic events which correlate with the initiating and promoting activities of carcinogenic agents.

Every respectable theory of cancer should be able to account for the excellent correlation between the capacity of an agent to induce DNA lesions and its carcinogenic potency (review: Cairns, 1978) and for the correlation between deficiency in DNA repair and susceptibility to cancer induction by DNA-damaging agents, as in the case of xeroderma pigmentosum (Cleaver, 1968). The ease with which somatic mutation theories can account for these correlations is their principal contribution to epigenetic theories. However, Pugh & Hollidayl have postulated recently an epigenetic mechanism of carcinogenesis, based on enzymatic modification of DNA, which is compatible with DNA damage, thus correlating with carcinogenesis. We present here the first experimental evidence for DNA damage - induced stable, heritable, epigenetic change - in a genetically and molecularly well-defined system of gene control by feedback loops.

# SPECIFIC INTRODUCTION

DNA lesions caused by ultra-violet (Uv) irradiation and many powerful chemical mutagens/carcinogens provoke both genetic (mutations, recombinations) and epigenetic (e.g., induction of lysogenic bacteria) effects in bacteria (reviews: Devoret, 1978; Witkin, 1976). Moreover, the mutagenic effect of these agents is under the same epigenetic control as the induction of prophage λ in lysogenic bacteria (SOS induction) (Defais et al., 197l; George et al., 1974; Radman, 1974; Witkin, 1974). The λ induction test is therefore a valuable test for a major class of DNA-damaging agents (Moreau et al., 1976). The complex circuits of intertwined negative and positive gene controls in bacteriophage λ have been studied in detail both genetically and biochemically (Echols, 1979; review: Hershey, 197l).

Each bacteriophage λ that infects its host Escherichia coli has the choice of two mutually exclusive developmental pathways: the lytic pathway and the lysogenic pathway. The 'decision' and the maintenance of the given developmental pathway are achieved by a specific repressor: the product of the cI gene in the case of lysogeny, and the product of the cro gene in the case of lytic development. Since these repressors mutually repress each other's synthesis, it suffices to inactivate one repressor to assure the expression of the other. Important molecular details of this intricate gene control have recently been elucidated (review: Echols, 1979; Johnson et al., 1978; Takeda, 1979). It is therefore conceivable that a prophage λ that is integrated into the bacterial chromosome and rendered harmless to its host (e.g., by

mutations that prevent its autonomous replication, excision from the host DNA and other host-killing effects) could exist in two different stable states (Eisen et al., 1970): in either (A) the immune state in which only the cI gene is active, cro and all other phage genes being repressed; or (B) the nonimmune state, initially triggered by inactivation of the cI repressor and consequently maintained by the cro repressor. In this state, various other $\lambda$ genes (except the cI) are expressed.

This knowledge stems from the studies of Eisen et al. (1970) with a mutant thermosensitive cI repressor (active at $3 2 ^ { \circ }$ and inactive at $4 2 ^ { \circ } .$ ); in this case, the transition (A) B was provoked by transient exposure to $4 2 ^ { \circ }$ .

It is known that the cI repressor is inactivated by SOS induction, a cellular process induced as a consequence of the presence of unrepairec DNA lesions; among other effects, Sos induction results in the proteolytic cleavage of the cI repressor (Roberts & Roberts, 1975). Therefore, we assumed that, with the wild-type cI repressor, state (A) could be transformed into a stable and heritable state (B), as a consequence of DNA lesions induced by mutagenic/carcinogenic agents. This prediction proved to be correct.

# INTERPRETATION OF EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

The results obtained with our new strain are in full agreement with the current theory of direct and indirect mutagenic pathways (Radman et al., 1977, 1979) and provide its most elegant support. EMS is a direct mutagen, causing mispairing mutagenic lesions (probably $O ^ { 6 }$ -ethylguanine), which neither cause SoS induction nor require SoS induction for mutation fixation. Therefore, EMS caused no appreciable epigenetic effect and mutated the recA+ cIind and recA- $\mathcal { C } T ^ { \dag }$ strains equally efficiently $( \leq 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ ) (results not shown). At low doses, MNG acts similarly to EMS, as a recA-independent direct mutagen; however, at high doses, it also causes the arrest of DNA synthesis and prophage induction (Kondo et al., 1970), and its mutagenic effects become amplified by Sos induction. MNNG is thus mutagenic in recA+ cIind- and recA- $c I ^ { + }$ strains but also causes epigenetic events in the EMT-1 $\mathit { r e c A } ^ { + } \mathit { c l } ^ { + }$ strain (Fig. 3). UV irradiation-induced pyrimidine dimers are typical 'non-pairing' DNA lesions, which cause SOS induction by inhibiting DNA synthesis and require SOS induction for the mutation fixation process. Therefore, UV irradiation was very efficient in inducing epigenetic events as well as mutational events in the recA+ $c I ^ { + }$ strain (Figs 2 & 4). However, no detectable mutagenesis was observed in the recA" derivative (Fig. 4, legend).

# DISCUSSION: A NEW BACTERIAL TEST SYSTEM FOR ALL MUTAGENIC AND DNA-DAMAGING AGENTS

Both the Salmonella-Ames test (mutatest) and the λ prophage induction test (inductest) for mutagens and carcinogens suffer from the fact that they produce a certain number of false negatives: the mutatest will not detect mutagens whose mutational specificity does not allow the reversion of the two initial his" mutations (McCann & Ames, 1976), and the inductest does not detect mutagens/carcinogens that do not induce $\lambda$ prophage, e.g., EMS (Moreau et al., 1976).

Our EMT-1, EMT-2 and EMT-3 strains provide a forward mutation assay, $c . \tau ^ { + } \nsim c . \tau ^ { - }$ , and the most sensitive and quantitative assay for Sos inducti The latter is true because the epigenetic switch (A) $\twoheadrightarrow$ BFig.1)is triggered by Sos induction and is then inherited by the daughter cells. This allows measurement of the signal (red:white colony ratio) over a $\mathtt { 1 0 ^ { 6 } }$ -fold range, which can be determined accurately, irrespective of the precision of dilutions or of pipetting, since the red:white colony counting is done on single plates. The manipulations involved in the test are considerably simpler than those in the mutatest and inductest: overnight growth is sufficient, and bacteria can be treated either in liquid or on plates. A qualitative spot-test is also simple. Unlike any other test, this one also gives information about the mutational pathway of the compound, which might be important in indicating whether it is a weak carcinogen but an efficient carcinogenic initiator, or simultaneously a strong initiator and promoter, i.e., a strong carcinogen. This is because only Sos-inducing mutagens are also efficient recombinogens, and both events might be required to accomplish carcinogenesis. This test system is now being developed further (recAts, env and uvrA derivatives) and validated. It has been given the name 'epigenetic mutatest'.

The expression of the $\smash { \vec { E } _ { t } ^ { \prime } }$ . coli gal operon was set under the direct negative control of phage λ repressor by a fusion between the gal operon and the active cI cro segment of phage λ genome. This system can exist in two stable but reversible epigenetic states: (A) the immune cI† cro $g \alpha \mathcal { l } ^ { - }$ state (white colonies on McConkey gal plates) and (B) the nonimmune cl cro+ gal+ state (red colonies). Transcription of this gal operon depends upon activation of the $\mathrm { P _ { \mathrm { R } } }$ promoter; hence requiring the removal of $\lambda$ immunity repressor $( c I )$ from the $^ { \circ } \mathtt { R }$ operator. Short exposure of this E. coli strain to radiation or chemical mutagens/carcinogens leads to a stable, inherited loss of the $c I$ repressor (due to the take-over by the cro repressor, repressing the cI gene) and subsequent constitutive expression of the gal operon. This switch, from the (A) state to the state, depends upon $\mathcal { E }$ . coli recA+ and λ cro+ genes and is reversible upon supply of λcI repressor; this is consistent with the fact that cI and cro proteins are mutual repressors, i.e., only one of them can be expressed at a given time.

This $\mathcal { E }$ . coli strain provides the easiest, most accurate and sensitive assay for all mutagenic and sos-inducing agents.
