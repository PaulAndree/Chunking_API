# GATC sequences, DNA nicks and the MutHI function in Escherichia coli mismatch repair

# Françoise Längle-Rouault, Geneviève Maenhaut-Michel and Miroslav Radman'

Département de Biologie Moléculaire. Université Libre de Bruxelles. 1640 Rhode-St-Genèse, Belgium and 'Laboratoire de Mutagenèse. Institut Jacques Monod, CNRS, Université Paris VII, Tour 43-2. Place Jussieu. 75251 Paris Cedex 05, France

Communicated by M.Radman

Circular heteroduplex DNAs of bacteriophage $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { l } 7 4$ have been constructed carrying either a G:T $( E a m ^ { + } / E a m 3 )$ or a G:A (Bam+ /Bam16) mismatch and containing either two, one or no GATC sequences. Mismatches were efficiently repaired in wild-type Escherichia coli transfected with $\phi \mathbf { X 1 7 4 }$ heteroduplexes only when two unmethylated GATC sequences were present in $\phi \mathbf { X 1 7 4 }$ DNA. The requirements for GATC sequences in substrate DNA and for the $E$ coli MutH function in $\pmb { E }$ coli mismatch repair can be alleviated by the presence of a persistent nick (transfection with nicked heteroduplex DNA in ligase temperature-sensitive mutant at $4 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } )$ : A persistent nick in the GATC sequence is as effective in stimulating mutL- and mutS-dependent mismatch repair as a nick distant from the GATC sequence and from the mismatch. These observations suggest that the MutH protein participates in methyl-directed mismatch repair by recognizing unmethylated DNA GATC sequences and/or stimulating the nicking of unmethylated strands.

Key words: $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ phage/heteroduplex DNA/mutagenesis/gene conversion

# Introduction

Methyl-directed mismatch repair in Escherichia coli appears to remove mispaired and unpaired bases from newly synthesized DNA strands, thus correcting replication errors (review, Radman and Wagner, 1986). The discrimination between the parental and the newly synthesized strand is based on the transient undermethylation of GATC sequences in nascent strands (Radman el al., 1980; Pukkila et al., 1983). Full methylation of the heteroduplex DNA strongly inhibits methyl-directed mismatch repair (Lu et al., 1983; Pukkila et al., 1983; Wagner et al., 1984). The methylation on the N6 position of the adenine residue in the GATC sequences is carried out by the product of the dam gene, with a delay relative to DNA synthesis (Marinus, 1976; Geier and Modrich, 1979; Lyons and Schendel, 1984).

Methyl-directed mismatch repair requires the mutH, mutL, mutS and mutU gene products (Nevers and Spatz, 1975; Rydberg, 1978; Glickman and Radman, 1980; Bauer et al., 1981). In addition to these Mut functions, mismatch repair reaction in vitro requires ATP and the single-strand-DNA-binding SSB protein (Lu et al., 1984). MutL and mutS, but not mutH and mutU, gene products are apparently involved also in the very short patch repair system (Lieb, 1985; Jones et al., 1987).

The products of some of the mut genes have been identified (Lu et al., 1984; Pang et al.. 1985), but the mechanistic involvement of these Mut proteins in mismatch repair remains largely obscure. The mutU (uvrE, uvrD) gene product was identified as the helicase II (Maples and Kushner, 1982; Hickson et al., 1983; Kumura and Sekiguchi, 1984). More recently it has been demonstrated that the $E$ coli helicase II protein translocates unidirectionally in a $3 ^ { \prime } - 5 ^ { \prime }$ direction along single-stranded DNA (Matson. 1986). The purified MutS protein binds specifically to DNA regions containing a single base-pair mismatch (Su and Modrich, 1986). A mismatch independent nicking of unmethylated GATC sequences in duplex DNA has been observed using purified MutH protein (Welsh et al., 1986). It was previously suggested that the mutH gene product may be involved in DNA-strand discrimination during mismatch repair (Kramer et al., 1984).

The methyl-directed mismatch repair in vivo requires the presence of GATC sequences in the heteroduplex DNA substrate for efficient mismatch repair activity (Längle-Rouault et al.. 1986). This efficient methyl-directed mismatch repair system of $E$ coli may be a relatively recent evolutionary acquisition of bacterial strains: the dam-methylation of GATC sequences is restricted to a few closely related enterobacteria (Barbeyron et al., 1984). Mismatch repair has been demonstrated in organisms other than $E$ coli (review, Claverys and Lacks, 1986). If it is a ubiquitous process for removing replication errors exclusively from newly synthesized strands, other means for strand discrimination must exist. It has been proposed that, in Streptococcus pneumoniae, the recognition of the donor DNA strand by the hex system depends on single-strand breaks or discontinuities (Guild and Shoemaker, 1976; Claverys et al., 1980: Lacks et al., 1982).

The methyl-directed mismatch repair system in $E$ coli acts by excision —resynthesis sometimes over a few thousand nucleotides (Wagner and Meselson, 1976), suggesting that a step creating a single-strand break is likely to be involved. Such nicks could occur at or near the mismatch, at the GATC sequence(s) or at both sites. This paper shows that the nick in heteroduplex DNA can substitute for the GATC sequences and the MutH function.

# Results and Discussion

# Mismatch repair in 1 GATC' heteroduplex DNA

When mismatch repair does not operate and when both DNA strands undergo replication, heteroduplex DNA molecules segregate genetically, yielding mixed progeny phages carrying the genotype of either of the two parental strands. It is known that $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ replication is asymmetric and that the complementary strand largely governs the rolling-circle replication (review, Kornberg. 1980). Thus the fraction of mixed progeny obtained after transfection with $\phi \mathrm { X } 1 7 4$ heteroduplex DNA reflects the proportion of heteroduplexes that have not been repaired and both strands of which have replicated to yield viable progeny.

We have previously used $\phi { \Chi } 1 7 4$ phage derivatives with short intercistronic inserts. one of which contains two GATC sequences and the other none $[ \phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ DNA is naturally devoid of GATC sequences (Sanger et al., 1977)], to test the role of GATC sequences in mismatch-repair. It appeared that the presence of GATC sequences is required for efficient mismatch repair (Längle-Rouault et al., 1986).

# Nick-stimulated mismatch repair

The excision-resynthesis mode of mismatch repair implies a single-strand incision step. It was suggested that nicks may in general strand-direct mismatch repair and that the only role of the GATC sequence is to provide nicks by a GATC-specific endonuclease (Manarelli et al., 1985). Therefore we have further analysed the potential role of the mutH gene product, and the influence of nicks on efficiency and direction of mismatch repair in $E$ coli. $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ heteroduplex DNA molecules, used in our studies, carry a nick in the complementary strand at the site where the parental RFI duplex was linearized (see Materials and methods). If this nick is not sealed by the $E _ { i }$ coli ligase upon transfection before the mismatch repair occurs, it could interact with mismatch repair as we have previously suggested (LängleRouault et al., 1986).

# Nick-directed mismatch repair

In the absence of mismatch repair, i.e. when heteroduplex DNA molecules give rise to mixed progeny, the two parental genotypes are roughly equally represented among the phage progeny in individual infective centres (data not shown). This suggests that, when both viral and complementary strands are replicated in the absence of, or prior to mismatch repair, both heteroduplex strands yield progeny with equal probabilities. However, most infective centres derived from heteroduplex $\phi { \Chi } 1 7 4$ DNA are not mixed. Even in the case of mutL and mutS bacteria a maximum of $5 0 \%$ mixed infective centres is observed, suggesting that only one strand replicates and yields viable progeny in over $5 0 \%$ of transfected cells. Alternatively, a mismatch repair system not requiring the mutL, mutS gene products may operate in $E .$ coli.

When heteroduplex DNA gives rise to a pure progeny, the predominance of one of the two parental genotypes reflects a bias in the mismatch repair and/or a bias in the replication of one of the two parental strands. In mutS and mutL bacteria, where no methyl-directed mismatch repair occurs, the fraction of pure progeny shows a strong bias in favour of the am marker carried by the parental complementary strand (see Table V, lines 3 and This result is in agreement with the asymmetric (rolling-circle) replication model for $\phi { \bf X } 1 7 4 $ DNA, in which the complementary strand is the template strand (see Kornberg, 1980).

We have shown (Längle-Rouault et al., 1986), that the methylation of GATC sequences in '2 GATC' $\phi X 1 7 4$ heteroduplex DNA directs mismatch repair on the unmethylated strand as was shown with λ heteroduplex DNA (Radman et al., 1980; Pukkila et al., 1983) and with M13 heteroduplex DNA (Kramer et al., 1984). When mismatch repair occurs in GATC-unmethylated DNA, one expects no particular bias in mismatch repair. Indeed, an equal proportion of both parental genotypes Bam+ and Bam16 was observed among the pure progeny of unmethylated '2 GATC' heteroduplex DNA in mut+ lig + bacteria (see Table V, lines 5 and 9).

In mutH ligts strains at permissive temperature where a residual mismatch repair activity is observed, the fact that the strong bias in favour of the genotype carried by the methylated strand in '2 GATC' hemimethylated heteroduplexes is largely reduced (Table V, cf. lines 11 and 7) indicates that the MutH protein is implicated in methyl direction of mismatch repair.

The evidence for the direction of mismatch repair to the nicked strand was obtained under the conditions of nick-stimulated repair in $\phi \mathrm { X } 1 7 4 $ DNA, i.e. in ligts mutant transfected at $4 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (Table V). The observation that nearly all mixed infective centres lost in mut\* ligts bacteria at $4 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ (relative to $3 2 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ ,i.e. $2 2 \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 8$ $= 1 4 \%$ appear in the pure $E a m ^ { + }$ class (i.e. $5 2 \textrm { -- } 4 1 = 1 1 \%$ is indicative of repair on the nicked complementary strand.

The nick-stimulated mismatch repair can occur on the nicked strand even if it is methylated as demonstrated by the decrease of the genotype carried by the nicked methylated strand (Bam16) in favour of the genotype carried by the intact unmethylated strand $( B a m ^ { + } )$ (Table V, lines 7 and 8). Moreover in a mutH ligts strain a bias in favour of the genotype of the intact strand is also observed for both unmethylated and hemimethylated 'GATC' heteroduplex DNAs at nonpermissive temperature (Table V, lines 10 and 12). This suggests that a single persistent nick in a small circular DNA can direct mismatch repair even in mutH-deficient strains.

# Further Discussion and Conclusions

Our results suggest that: (i) the standard methyl-directed mutHdependent mismatch repair requires unmethylated GATC sequences; (ii) the requirement for GATC sequences and for the mutH function in mismatch repair can be complemented by persistent nicks in DNA; and (iii) mismatch repair is directed on the nicked strand in nick-stimulated repair, and on the strand carrying the unmethylated GATC sequences in methyl-directed repair. Therefore it is likely that the involvement of the MutH protein in mismatch repair of $\phi { \bf { X } } 1 7 4 $ heteroduplex DNA is a mismatch-stimulated nicking of the strand carrying unmethylated GATC sequences. Thus, mismatch repair of $\phi { \Chi } 1 7 4$ heteroduplex DNA can be stimulated and directed either by GATC sequences using the mutH gene product or by single-strand breaks, which alleviate the requirement for the mutH function.

To account for results and work reviewed in this paper, we propose the following model for the methyl-directed mismatch repair of $\phi { \Chi } 1 7 4$ heteroduplex DNA in $E .$ coli (Figure 2). The MutL and MutS proteins may recognize and locally open up the site of the mismatch allowing the MutU protein (which is DNA helicase II) to enter the DNA and unwind it bidirectionally. MutH protein(s) may recognize non-methylated GATC sequences flanking a mismatched base-pair and introduce single-strand breaks at these GATC sequences [probably immediately $5 ^ { \prime }$ to the dam (GATC) sequence (Welsh et al., 1986)], allowing the displacement of the single-strand DNA fragment carrying the mismatched bases. The resulting gap is filled up by the DNA polymerase using the intact strand as the template. The nick is then sealed by the $E$ coli ligase.

Mismatch-stimulated breakage at GATC sequences could occur before, coincidently, or after the melting of unmethylated GATC sequences. Alternatively, the MutH-stimulated nicking of GATC sequences could occur independently of other Mutproteins e.g. if the MutH substrates are single-stranded unmethylated GATC sequences (i.e. MutH protein would formally act as a single-strand specific, GATC-specific and methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme). This latter hypothesis is intriguing because it suggests that (i) there may be a coevolution of the Mut-endonuclease and Dam-methylase functions and (ii) the mutH function may be responsible for a very strong counterselection of GATC sequences in single-stranded DNA phages $\langle \phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ , G4 and M13) and in fully unmethylated doublestranded phages (T7) (McClelland, 1985).
