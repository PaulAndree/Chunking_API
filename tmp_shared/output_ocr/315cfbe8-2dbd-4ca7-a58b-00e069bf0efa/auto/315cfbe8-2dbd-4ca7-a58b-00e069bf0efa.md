Summary. A single base pair mismatch (G:T or A:C) in the CYC1 gene of the integrative plasmid pAB218 stimulates up to a five-fold integration into the yeast chromosome. Analysis of chromosomal sites of plasmid integration suggests that the mismatch-stimulated integration is not targeted as would be expected if crossovers, localised in the region of the mismatch, were a necessary step in mismatch repair. Instead, the observed mismatch-stimulated plasmid integration could be due to potentially recombinogenic structures formed during mismatch repair, such as single-stranded gaps or denatured DNA regions extending around the plasmid molecule.

To test for the possible recombinogenic effect of mismatches, we have transformed Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells with an integrative plasmid containing a single base pair mismatch (either A:C or G:T) in the $C Y C { 1 }$ gene. Recombinational repair of the mismatch was expected to result in increased frequency of plasmid integration. We show that a single base-pair mismatch indeed stimulates plasmid integration in yeast. However, contrary to the prediction of mismatch repair via the double-strand break model, we find that the stimulation of recombination is not confined to the vicinity of the mismatch. We discuss the possible relevance of our results to the mechanism of mismatch repair in yeast.

# Introduction

Mismatches, i.e., mispaired or nonpaired bases, can arise in vivo via three routes: (1) in the course of recombination between homologous but not identical DNA sequences, (2) as a consequence of errors in DNA replication, or (3) by the spontaneous deamination of 5-methylcytosine creating a T:G mismatch. Three mechanisms for the general mismatch repair process have been proposed: (a) methyl-directed, (b) nick-directed, (c) recombinational repair, involving a double-strand break (dsb) as an intermediate (for reviews see: Claverys and Lacks 1986; Radman and Wagner 1986; Modrich 1987; Meselson 1988; Lahue and Modrich 1988; Radman 1988). Yeast and bacterial strains and plasmids. Plasmid pAB218 has the BamHI-HindIII fragment in the yeast integrative plasmid YIp5 (Sherer and Davis 1979) replaced by a $2 . 5 \mathrm { k b }$ yeast DNA fragment containing the CYC1 gene. Plasmid pAB219 is a derivative of pAB218 that has a single base pair transition A:T to G:C in the CYC1 gene (cyc1-34) (Fig. 1). Plasmids were extracted without amplification from $\mathcal { E }$ coli GM119 (dam-3, dcm-6, metB1, galK2, galT22, lacY1, thi-1, tonA31, tsx78, mtl-1, supE11). Yeast strains used were diploids D352 (a/α ade5/ADE5 his7-2/HIS7 leu2-3, 112/ leu2-3, 112 trp1-289/trp1-283 lys1-1/LYS1 ura3-52/ura3-52) and D31 (a/α trp5/TRP5 lys2/LYS2 ura3-1/ura3−1).

Construction of mismatch containing plasmid DNA. Plasmids pAB218 and pAB219 were linearized with restriction enzymes, one of them with ClaI and the other with $P { \nu } u \mathrm { I I }$ (see Fig. 1). The enzymes were heat-inactivated or phenol-extracted, the DNA precipitated and dissolved in a small volume of TE buffer ( $\mathrm { ~ \textit ~ { ~ 1 0 ~ m M } ~ }$ Tris-HCl, pH 8.5, $1 \mathrm { m M }$ EDTA). The two linearized plasmids were mixed together and denatured by heating for $1 5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $6 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in $7 0 \%$ formamide, 300 mM NaCl, $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ Tris-HCl, $\mathrm { p H } 8 . 5$ , 1 mM EDTA at a final DNA concentration of $1 0 { - } 2 0 \mu \mathrm { g / m l }$ The solution was allowed to cool slowly (at least for $4 \mathrm { { h } }$ ) to room temperature resulting in the formation of about $50 \%$ circular, mismatch-containing (heteroduplex), molecules. After precipitation with 0.6 volumes of isopropanol, these were ligated with $E$ coli DNA ligase at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and separated by centrifugation in $\mathrm { C s C l } _ { j }$ ethidium bromide density gradients. The homoduplex plasmid (with no mismatch) that was used as a control in transformation experiments was prepared using the same procedure.

# Results and discussion

Transformation of yeast by integrative plasmids is due to homologous recombination between a plasmid molecule and the yeast chromosome. The transformation efficiency can be increased by up to a 1000-fold by the presence of a dsb in the yeast sequence of the plasmid (Orr-Weaver et al. 1981, 1983), illustrating the fact that integration of plasmids can be used to assay for the presence of recombinogenic structures in DNA. We have used this system to test for a possible recombinogenic effect of single base-pair mismatches, by comparing the integration efficiencies of closed circular heteroduplex and homoduplex plasmids. The two plasmids differ only in a single base causing a single mismatched base pair in the CYC1 region of the heteroduplex (see Materials and methods). The same amount of each plasmid was used to transform yeast protoplasts and, in all cases, the heteroduplex transformation was more efficient (2 to 5-fold) than the homoduplex transformation. Figure 2 shows the results obtained with two different diploids. As a control experiment, these two plasmids were linearized by KpnI (182 bp from the site of the mismatch, see Fig. 1). The expected stimulation by the dsb was found (about 200-fold, data not shown), and the transformation efficiencies of the hetero- and homoduplex plasmid were not significantly different (Fig. 3A). Thus, the higher yield of transformants observed with circular heteroduplex DNA is due to the presence of the mismatch and not to different amounts of plasmid DNA. Using replicative mismatchcontaining plasmids, Kramer et al. (1988) have shown that at least $60 - 7 0 \%$ of heteroduplex molecules can be repaired in yeast before replication. Both mismatches used in this study (A:C and G:T) are very efficiently repaired in bacteria (Dohet et al. 1985), in yeast (White et al. 1985; Bishop and Kolodner 1986) and in mammalian cells (Brown and Jiricny 1988). It is thus likely that the observed mismatch-stimulated plasmid integration is a consequence of the mismatch repair process, rather than being directly caused by the DNA mismatch structure.

If repair of mismatches involves formation of dsbs and their subsequent repair by homologous recombination, as proposed by Hastings (1984) and Wagner et al. (1984), we would expect integration of the plasmid bearing a mismatch in the $C Y C t$ gene to be specifically targeted to its chromosomal homologue and not to the ura3 gene. Southern blotting was used to distinguish between plasmid integration at ura3 and CYC1 (see Materials and methods). One hundred and forty four heteroduplex and 80 homoduplex-mediated transformants were analysed from the pool with a weighted mean mismatch-stimulated integration factor of 3.7 (Table 1). Among heteroduplex-mediated transformants, the proportion of integration events in ura3 (10/144) was not significantly different from that observed with control, homoduplex, plasmid (7/80). Thus, mismatches on the plasmid appear to have stimulated integration in ura3 as well as in CYC1 chromosomal genes.

It should also be noticed that the mismatch-dependent stimulation of plasmid integration is relatively weak compared to that produced by enzymatically introduced plasmid dsbs. This could be explained by efficient recombinational repair between cotransformed plasmid molecules. Such a repair process could eventually generate dimeric molecules giving rise to multiple tandem integrations (Orr-Weaver et al. 1981) but these events were rare and no more frequent with heteroduplex than with homoduplex plasmids (Table 1). Moreover, control experiments with replicative plasmids have shown that, under our experimental conditions (data not shown), only about $20 \%$ of cells are transformed with two or more plasmid molecules.

We also looked for a possible killing effect of the mismatch by cutting the heteroduplex plasmid in the URA3 gene (ApaI) before transformation. If a second dsb, related to the mismatch, occurs before integration into the ura3 chromosomal gene, the plasmid should be inactivated and the transformation efficiency should be lower than that of the linearized homoduplex plasmid. Such an effect was not observed (Fig. 3 B).

Although none of the above arguments constitute a direct disproof of the formation of dsbs during mismatch repair, they encourage the view that the mismatch-stimulated integration is due to some other structure, perhaps less recombinogenic than dsbs, formed during mismatch repair. Single-stranded integration plasmids are known to transform yeast about 5-fold more efficiently than the corresponding double-stranded plasmids (Simon and Moore 1987). Single-stranded gaps, or denatured DNA regions, appearing during mismatch repair, could thus be responsible for the enhanced plasmid integration. The observation that the recombinogenic effect does not seem to be localized to a mismatch-containing region, suggests a "spreading" of the recombinogenic effect around the circular molecule. This could occur, for example, by binding of the mismatch-recognizing proteins and their polymerization along with strand-exchange proteins around the circular molecule, or by the entry of a helicase at the site of the mismatch leading to the spread of "waves" of denaturation bubbles around the molecule. A mismatch-dependent melting of DNA was proposed to participate in mismatch repair in both E. coli and Streptococcus pneumoniae (Radman 1988) and may represent a common feature of the mismatch repair process operating in unrelated organisms.
