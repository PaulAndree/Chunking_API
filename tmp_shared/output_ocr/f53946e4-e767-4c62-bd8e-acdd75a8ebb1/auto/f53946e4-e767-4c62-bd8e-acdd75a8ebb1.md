# SOS mutator effect in $E .$ coli mutants deficient in mismatch correction

# P.Caillet-Fauquet\*, G.Maenhaut-Michel and M.Radman

Laboratoire de Biophysique et de Radiobiologie, Département de Biologie Moléculaire, Universite libre de Bruxelles, B-1640 Rhode-St-Genese, Belgium

\*To whom reprint requests should be sent Communicated by M.Radman

We have used bacteriophage λ to characterize the mutator effect of the SOS response induced by u.v. irradiation of Escherichia coli. Mutagenesis of unirradiated phages grown in irradiated or unirradiated bacteria was detected by measuring forward mutagenesis in the immunity genes or reversion mutagenesis of an amber codon in the $R$ gene. Relative to the wild-type, the SOS mutator effect was higher in $E$ . coli mismatch correction-deficient mutants (mutH, mutL and mutS) and lower in an adenine methylation-deficient mutant (dam3). We conclude that a large proportion of SOS-induced 'untargeted' mutations are removed by the methyl-directed mismatch correction system, which acts on newly synthesized DNA strands. The lower SOS mutator effect observed in $E .$ . coli dam mutants may be due to a selective killing of mismatch-bearing chromosomes resulting from undirected mismatch repair. The SOS mutator effect on undamaged $\lambda$ DNA, induced by u.v. irradiation of the host, appears to result from decreased fidelity of DNA synthesis.

Key words: bacteriophage λ/DNA methylation/replicational errors/mismatch-stimulated killing/untargeted mutagenesis

# Introduction

Mutagenesis induced by u.v. irradiation and by many chemicals depends upon transient induction of a cellular mutagenic activity, the SOS mutator activity (Radman, 1974), which permits fixation of mutations on damaged DNA (targeted mutagenesis, see Witkin and Wermundsen, 1978). It was assumed that the same SOS mutator effect generates mutations in the course of replication of undamaged DNA (Caillet-Fauquet et al., 1977) (untargeted mutagenesis, see Witkin and Wermundsen, 1978). Targeted and untargeted mutagenesis have been shown genetically using bacteriophage genomes as probes for cellular mutagenic capacity (Weigle, 1953; Jacob, 1954; Devoret, 1965; Ichikawa-Ryo and Kondo, 1975). Further evidence for the existence of targeted and untargeted mutagenesis was obtained from nucleotide sequence analysis of u.v.-induced mutations in Escherichia coli (Coulondre et al., 1978; Brandenburger et al., 1981; Leclerc and Istock, 1982). On the basis of the current knowledge about the mechanisms of avoidance and correction of mutations (Bourguignon-Van Horen et al., 1982), it was suggested that untargeted mutagenesis may result either from a decreased fidelity of the replication machinery (CailletFauquet et al., 1977) or from a decreased efficiency of postreplicative mismatch correction (Caillet-Fauquet and Maenhaut-Michel. 1982).

The mismatch correction system presumably acts via the excision-resynthesis mode of DNA repair, using the parental strand as template. It recognizes the newly synthesized DNA strand as substrate because of its transient undermethylation (Radman et al., 1980). E. coli dam mutants (Marinus and Morris, 1973), lacking adenine methylation in ${ \pmb 5 } ^ { \prime }$ GATC sequences (Lacks and Greenberg, 1977), are deficient in strand discrimination but proficient in mismatch repair (undirected), whereas mutH, mutL, mutS and mut U (other symbols for the latter gene are uvrE, uvrD and recL) mutants are deficient in mismatch repair but proficient in DNA methylation (reviewed in Bourguignon-Van Horen et al., 1982; Radman et al., 1981). To determine whether the SOS mutator effect is a result of decreased replication fidelity, unirradiated bacteriophage λ was used as the probe for u.v.-induced mutagenesis in $E$ ,coli mut and dam mutants. Our results suggest that the SOS mutator effect acts by a decrease in replicative fidelity.

# Results

# The experimental system

Two different mutation systems were used to measure the u.v.-inducible cellular mutator effect in $E .$ coli:(i) forward mutagenesis in the immunity control region of bacteriophage $\lambda$ (consisting of the cl, cII and cIII loci and resulting in a clear plaque phenotype) and (ii) reversion mutagenesis of an amber codon in the λ R gene. The forward mutation system permits detection of all classes of mutations (substitutions, frameshift, deletions, insertions and inversions), whereas the amber reversion system is specific for a single class of mutations, base substitutions, which may be largely undetected (phenotypically neutral) in the forward mutation system. It can be inferred from the work of Lieb (1981) and Skopek and Hutchinson (1982) that a neutral substitution mutation may become a phenotypic mutation when combined with a preexisting ts mutation. Therefore it was expected that the temperature-sensitive cI857 mutation would increase the detection of base substition mutations causing clear plaque phenotype at $3 2 \textdegree$ . Clear, turbid and 'mottled' (mixed plaques containing both $c$ and $c ^ { + }$ phages) plaques can be distinguished morphologically in the $c ^ { + } {  } c$ mutation system (Figure 1). The frequency of mixed $( c / c ^ { + } )$ plaques was underestimated due to the limiting detection of plaques containing only a small fraction of the alternative phenotype. Clear plaque mutants detected in this study are cl, cll and cIII mutants which occurred in roughly equal proportions, as estimated by complementation tests (not shown).

# Discussion

Mutations induced by treatments which interfere with DNA replication (e.g., DNA damaging agents), but which occur in the course of replication of undamaged DNA, have been called untargeted mutations. Inducible mutagenic activity generating untargeted mutations and depending upon the $r e c A ^ { + }$ gene will be referred to as the SOS mutator effect. Current knowledge of the mechanisms of base substitution and frameshift mutagenesis indicates that a mutator effect could result from: (i) increased errors by the DNA replication machinery copying intact templates, (ii) decreased efficiency of the post-replicative mismatch correction, or (iii) chemical modifications of the DNA resulting in miscoding DNA lesions (for reviews, see Bourguignon-Van Horen et al., 1982; Radman et al., 1981). Replication errors generate mismatched base pairs which can be corrected by excision of the mismatched base from the newly synthesized strand only before completion of DNA methylation. When not corrected such mismatch-bearing DNAs, or heteroduplexes, segregate wild-type and mutant genotypes yielding mixed clones.

We have studied the SOS mutator effect induced by u.v. irradiation of $\pmb { E }$ coli using unirradiated phage λ as the probe. The fact that nearly all SOS-induced clear mutants occur in mottled (mixed, $c / c ^ { + }$ ) plaques (Figure 2A) indicates that such mutants are generated in the course of successive replication rounds (Ichikawa-Ryo and Kondo, 1975; Quillardet and Devoret, 1982) and supports the idea that replication errors are the source of the SOS mutator effect. However, the same increase in infective centers with mottled plaque morphology could be obtained if SOS induction caused an increase in the number of $\lambda$ DNA replication rounds in the.absence of any mutator effect. The fact that neither an increase in the burst size nor a significant delay in cell lysis was observed in u.v.- irradiated bacteria (Caillet-Fauquet and Defais, 1977b) argues against this possibility. On the contrary, a decrease in λ burst size was observed, particularly at high u.v. doses (Jacob, 1954; Caillet-Fauquet and Defais, 1977b) which, if it reflects a decrease in replication rounds, may explain the sharp decrease in mutation frequency at high doses (Figure 2A).

E. coli mut mutants deficient in mismatch repair and a dam mutant deficient in the adenine methylation responsible for the strand specificity of mismatch repair, have been used to investigate the mechanism of the SOS mutator effect. The finding that some mut mutants exhibit a higher u.v.-induced increase in $\lambda$ mutagenesis than the wild-type strain (Figure 2A; Figure 3) suggests that the induced mutations are largely subject to mismatch correction and hence arise in the newly synthesized strands. Since all induced λ mutagenesis in mut + and mut $E$ , coli is recA dependent (Figures 2 and 3), i.e., SOS induced, it appears that inactivation of mismatch repair by mut mutation allows more complete expression of the SOS mutator effect. Since mut mutations exhibit different effects on the two mutation sites, it is unlikely that the observed effects of mut mutations result from an increased efficiency of SOS induction.

The small effect of mut mutations on phage $\lambda$ spontaneous mutation frequency (as compared with $E .$ coli mutagenesis) may reflect a poor efficiency of the mismatch correction system, e.g., due to a limited availability of free enzymes or to an unfavorable DNA methylation pattern or kinetics (Dreiseikelman et al., 1979). Alternatively λ replication machinery may generate a mutation spectrum different from that of $E .$ coli (i.e., a high proportion of mismatches which are not subject to correction in wild-type E. coli). Non-corrected mismatches have been characterised in experiments with phage λ DNA heteroduplexes (C.Dohet, R.Wagner and M.Radman, in preparation).

The observation that only mutH and mutL mutants showed increased $c ^ { + } \to c$ mutagenesis and only mutS and mutH mutants showed increased $R a m 2 2 l  R ^ { + }$ mutagenesis may indicate a specificity in the recognition of different mismatches by the components of the mismatch repair system. Although mut mutants share some common phenotypes, mut-specific effects have been found with different mutagenic agents and with different mismatched heteroduplexes (Jones and Wagner, 1982; Bebenek and Janion, 1983; Fox and Meselson, 1983). We assume therefore that the clear-plaque mutants may arise (via mismatched base pairs) by base changes different from the $R ^ { + }$ revertants. In such a case one may observe differential effects of the mut mutations on different mutation sites.

We interpret the lack of induced $\lambda$ mutagenesis in the $E .$ . coli dam mutant (Figures 2C and 3) as a consequence of the killing of unmethylated mismatch-bearing λ DNA by the mismatch repair enzymes rather than the absence of SOS mutator activity (as in recA mutants) for the following reasons: (i) the induced $c$ and $R ^ { + } \lambda$ mutants can be partially recovered in E. coli dam mutH and dam mutS double mutants (Figures 2C and 3); (i) the recovery of mutants is accompanied by an increase in viable phage (Figure 4), and (iii) the killing of $\lambda$ phages in irradiated dam mutants occurs in the dose range in which the SOS mutator effect is highest (Figure 4). Analogous to these results is the observation that $E _ { \mathrm { { \ell } } }$ ,coli cells are sensitized to u.v. light by the dam3 mutation and that this sensitivity is alleviated by an additional mutH or mutS mutation (Figure 5). Therefore, it appears that mismatchstimulated killing may occur also in the bacterial dam chromosome presumably at sites of SOS-induced mismatches, which, if not corrected, would yield untargeted mutations.

If mismatch-stimulated killing in dam- mutants is the consequence of an active undirected mismatch repair, then the u.v.-induced effect is not likely to result from an inhibition of the mismatch repair system (either by SOS induction or by a direct competition of damaged host DNA for the mismatch repair enzymes). Inhibition of mismatch repair should decrease mismatch-stimulated killing in dam mutants resulting in an increased (rather than decreased) mutation frequency. Mismatch-stimulated killing in dam- mutants, postulated for base analog- and alkylation-induced mismatches (Glickman and Radman, 1980; Jones and Wagner, 1981; Bebenek and Janion, 1983) has been demonstrated also for single base pair mismatches G/T and A/C in unmethylated heteroduplex $\lambda$ DNA (P.Doutriaux, unpublished experiments).

Elimination of untargeted mutations in phage λ DNA by mismatch correction, which does not significantly affect the targeted mutations in λ DNA (Maenhaut-Michel, CailletFauquet and Radman, unpublished), may be relevant to the pattern of bacterial mutagenesis. Mismatch repair-deficient mut mutants have a strong spontaneous mutator effect on the bacterial chromosome but a very weak effect on phage λ, suggesting preferential mismatch correction of the bacterial chromosome. Therefore, it is conceivable that the predominance of targeted mutations in the bacterial u.v. mutagenesis (Witkin and Wermundsen, 1978; Foster et al., 1982; Haseltine, 1983; Miller, 1982) may be the consequence of an efficient mismatch correction of untargeted mutations as well as of the small number of DNA replication rounds during the SOS mutator effect (Witkin and Wermundsen, 1978). Insufficient mismatch repair and/or generation of mismatches not recognised by the mismatch repair system may have contributed to the significant proportion of untargeted mutagenesis in irradiated $\lambda$ phage.

We conclude that: (i) SOS mutator effect generates untargeted mutations in the newly synthesized strands, presumably as the consequence of decreased replicative fidelity, (ii) post-replicative mismatch correction decreases u.v.-induced untargeted mutagenesis and (ii) mismatch-stimulated killing may be responsible for the failure to recover induced phage mutants from dam bacteria.

# Methods

Media, u.v. irradiation, phage assay and measure of λ clear mutation frequency were described by Defais et al. (197l), except that $E$ coli C600 was used as indicator strain. This indicator strain and the particular nature of the AB1157 genetic background account for the difference between the results presented in this paper and in the previous work (Defais et al., 1971). We decided not to use the hyperlysogenizing strain N0483 (Ichikawa-Ryo and

Kondo, 1975) for detection of λ clear mutants because it may lead to false results due to mixed lawn (unpublished results). This appears in particular when the $\pmb { \lambda } \pmb { c }$ mutation frequency is compared in irradiated and unirradiated hosts, because the proportion of the regular test-strain and the No483 indicator strain necessarily varies in the lawn (M.Defais, personal communication and our unpublished observations).
