# Large non-homology in heteroduplex DNA is processed differently than single base pair mismatches

C. Dohet, S. Didi, R. Wagner and M. Radman Instiut Jacus Monod, C.R.S.Universi ParisVII, Tour 3, 2Place usu,F-75251 Pari Cedex 05,F

Summary. Unmethylated DNA heteroduplexes with a large single stranded loop in one strand have been prepared from separated strands of DNA from two different strains of bacteriophage $\lambda$ ,one of which has a $\sim 8 0 0$ base pair IS1 insertion in the cI gene. The results of transfections with these heteroduplexes into wild-type and mismatch repair deficient bacteria indicate that such large non-homologies are not repaired by the Escherichia coli mismatch repair system. However, the results do suggest that some process can act to repair such large non-homologies in heteroduplex DNA. Transfections of a series of recombination and excision repair deficient mutants suggest that known excision or recombination repair systems of $E _ { \mathrm { { \ell } } }$ coli are not responsible for the repair. Repair of large non-homologies may play a role in gene conversion involving large insertion or deletion mutations.

The Escherichia coli mismatch repair system is believed to repair replication errors by removing mispaired and unpaired bases from newly synthesized DNA (Radman et. al. 1980; Glickman and Radman 1980; Pukkila et al. 1983; Wagner et al. 1984; Dohet et al. 1985; review, Radman and Wagner 1986). The basis of strand discrimination in the E. coli system is adenine methylation of GATC sequences, i.e. DNA regions with fully methylated GATC sequences are refractory to repair (Pukkila et al. 1983; Wagner et al. 1984). Thus, the primary site of mismatch repair during E. coli DNA replication is believed to be the transiently undermethylated region of newly synthesized DNA immediately behind the replication fork. Repair is thought to occur via excision and resynthesis (Wagner and Meselson 1976; Lu et al. 1983).

The system which we have used for the study of $E$ coli mismatch repair involves annealing separated strands of bacteriophage λ DNA from phages of two different genotypes to form heteroduplex DNA in vitro. These heteroduplexes are introduced by calcium-mediated transfection (Mandel and Higa 1970) into $E ,$ coli cells at low multiplicity and the progeny phages derived from single heteroduplexes are recovered from infective centres and their genotypes determined (Dohet et al. 1985; Wagner et al. 1984). Mixed infective centres, i.e. those yielding phages of both input genotypes, reflect the absence of both mismatch repair and strand loss. In experiments using heteroduplexes with single base pair mismatches (Table 1 and Wagner et al. 1984; Dohet et al. 1985) or single unpaired bases (Dohet et al. 1986), a small fraction of mixed infective centres (e.g., $1 \% { - } 1 5 \%$ for well-repaired mismatches) has been shown to reflect the occurrence of mismatch repair.

Mismatch repair is also believed to operate on mismatched heteroduplexes formed during the processes of genetic recombination and gene conversion (reviews, Radding 1978; Radman and Wagner 1984). It has been shown that large non-homologies can be included in regions of heteroduplex formed by two recombining DNA molecules (Lichten and Fox 1984) and there is some evidence suggesting that such structures may not be substrates for mismatch

Heteroduplexes with a large ( $\sim 8 0 0$ base pair) loop were prepared by annealing separated strands of two different λ DNAs, one of which contains an IS1 insertion in the cI gene (Skopek and Hutchinson 1982). When these heteroduplexes are introduced into wild type $E .$ coli by transfection, the fraction of mixed infective centres recovered is much larger than that found for well repaired single base pair mismatches (Table 1). However, the fraction is somewhat smaller than that found for single base pair mismatches when the transfections are done in mutL bacteria, which are deficient in mismatch repair (Table 1). Taken alone, therefore, the finding of an intermediate frequency of mixed infective centres from transfections of wild-type bacteria suggests that the mismatch repair system can operate, although poorly, on heteroduplexes with large non-homologous regions. However, nearly identical results are obtained when the transfections are done in mismatch repair deficient (mutL) (Rydberg 1978; Glickman and Radman 1980; Radman et al. 1980; Pukkila et al. 1983; Lu et al. 1983; Wagner et al. 1984; Dohet et al. 1985) bacteria (Table 1). Experiments with three other mismatch repair deficient mutants, mutS, mutH and mutU (uvrE) gave results similar to those of mutL transfections (Table 3), suggesting that heteroduplexes with large non-homologous regions may be completely refractory to repair by the $E .$ coli mismatch repair system.

If the finding of nearly equal sized fractions of mixed infective centres from transfections in wild type and mutL bacteria indicates that the mismatch repair system is not operating on these heteroduplexes, then the question still remains as to why the fraction of mixed infective centres is lower than that found for heteroduplexes with single base pair mismatches under conditions of reduced mismatch repair (mutL). It is unlikely that the heteroduplex preparations are badly contaminated with homoduplexes, because the results are reproducible with heteroduplexes from different strand preparations (e.g. Tables 1 and 2) and, as in previous work, self-annealing of individual strand preparations reveals virtually no contamination of R strands by $\mathbf { L }$ strands and no unusual amount of contamination of $\mathbf { L }$ strands by R strands (Wagner and Meselson 1976; Dohet et al. 1985). The results could be accounted for by the occurrence of increased strand loss in transfections with heteroduplexes having large unpaired loops. Alternatively, there may be some mutL $( H , S , U )$ independent repair process in $E$ coli which can repair such heteroduplexes.

The results indicate that both $o$ alleles can be recovered from some pure turbid $( c ^ { + } )$ or pure clear $( c I )$ infective centres and about $9 0 \%$ of the mixed centres $( c ^ { + } / c I )$ are also mixed at $o$ (Table 2). Therefore, unless the infective centres mixed at $o$ are the result of frequent, and heteroduplex-dependent, recombination between daughter molecules followed by the loss of (or the failure to recover) one genotype, strand loss is not the only source of pure infective centres from transfections with heteroduplexes having large unpaired loops. No conclusions can be drawn from these data with regard to the nature of the repair system acting on the heteroduplexes except that it is not identical to the $E$ coli repair system that acts on single base mismatches. (However, it cannot be excluded that the mismatch repair system may have some residual activity in all four $E$ coli mut mutants that is capable of recognizing and repairing large non-homologous regions in heteroduplex DNA). Transfections of a uvrA mutant and three rec mutants (recA, recBC and recF) with heteroduplexes with the singlestranded IS1 loop suggest that known excision or recombination repair systems of $E$ coli are not responsible for the repair of this large non-homology (Table 3). The relative fractions of pure turbid (loop excision) and pure clear infective centres are different for the two different heteroduplexes (Tables 13). The data do not suggest a source for this difference. It may be that repair, or strand loss, is different for the different heteroduplexes, perhaps because transcription interferes with repair in one of the heteroduplexes more than in the other.

Heteroduplex regions with large non-homologies have been shown to occur in the course of bacteriophage $\lambda$ recombination (Lichten and Fox 1984); the results reported here suggest that repair of such heteroduplexes may allow the insertion or deletion of large non-homologies and thus contribute to the formation of recombinant genotypes.
