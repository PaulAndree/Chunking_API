Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium belong to 2 different bacterial genera that have emerged from a common ancestor some 150 millions yr ago [1]. Since that time their genomes have evolved by accumulation of mutations such that their divergence at the DNA level is $\approx 2 0 \%$ , mostly (19/20) localized at the third base of the codons [2]. The fact that DNA has evolved but not proteins encoded by that DNA is in agreement with the theory of neutral evolution developed by Kimura [3] and explains why, in the laboratory, it is possible to substitute most, if not all, of vital gene functions between these 2 bacteria. Yet, the cross between these 2 bacteria is sterile, ie there is no significant genetic exchange. In a classical conjugation experiment, the intergeneric recombination frequency is $\approx 1 0 – 8$ per Hfr donor compared to a $\mathbf { 1 0 ^ { - 1 } }$ frequency in intraspecific recombination [4]. The cause of the sterility of these crosses has recently become clear. The identity of a species appears to be ultimately defined by the base sequence of its DNA. Closely related species cannot interbreed because recombination is impaired by their DNA sequence differences. On the molecular level, this is because the key step in recombination involves the pairing of complementary strands of DNA from each parent, ie the formation of a hybrid DNA joint. If the parental sequences are different, this hybrid DNA will be a heteroduplex bearing mismatches at the sites of sequence difference. A surprisingly small degree of sequence divergence is sufficient to hinder recombination: as little as one difference in 10 bases already has a large effect [5, 6]. Efficient recombination in vivo requires a nearly perfect homology. The fidelity of recombination depends on the precise formation of a hybrid joint which is a key structure for aligning parental sequences in genetic recombination. About 30 bp of perfect homology are needed to initiate recombination, ie a MEPS (minimum efficient processing segment) of perfect homology [6]. But after initiation, heteroduplex extension by the RecABCD system of $E$ coli can proceed even across large heterologies [7]. The fidelity of recombination is not guaranteed by the RecA protein because heteroduplex DNA formation catalysed in vitro by the $E$ coli RecA protein tolerates up to 30% mismatches [8]. The results described here indicate that the control of recombination fidelity, ie the stringency of the sequence identity necessary for recombination, is assured by the mismatch repair enzymes as we have previously shown [9], and that interspecific recombination between $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli and $s$ typhimurium occurs via the RecABCD pathway.

# Results

# RecA dependence of interspecific recombination

Recombination following Hfr-mediated conjugation is the 'natural' means for genetic exchange in $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli whereby chromosomes pair and exchange homologous fragments to produce recombinant chromosomes. This complex process is promoted by a series of recombination enzymes. The recombinational pathways have been initially defined using Hfr -mediated conjugation. Conjugational recombination requires RecA protein and follows the RecBCD pathway in wild type $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli [15]. To show that the recombination fidelity is controlled by the mismatch repair enzymes, we previously studied interspecific conjugational recombination between $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli and S typhimurium. To test whether such intergeneric recombination proceeds via homologous recombination pathways, we checked the RecA dependence of this 'relaxed' recombination permitted by the inactivation of one of the mismatch repair (Mut) functions. The results are shown in table I. Inactivating one Mut protein increases the recombination frequency, at least 103- fold for MutL or MutS and 102- fold for MutH. These intergeneric recombination events are RecAdependent, as illustrated by the absence of such recombination in a recA mut background and therefore operationally represent homologous recombination events.

# Implication of the RecBCD pathway

The implication of the RecBCD pathway in interspecific recombination has been examined in comparison to the known role of this pathway in intraspecific recombination [15]. Homospecific and heterospecific crosses using a similar Hfr and identical Salmonella recipients either mut+, mutS or mutL were made in a $r e c \hat { C } ^ { + }$ or recC background. The results are shown in table II. In homospecific crosses, inactivating either the MutL or the MutS protein does not change the frequency of recombination (in contrast to heterospecific crosses). The recC mutation decreases recombination in homospecific crosses from $\mathbf { 1 0 ^ { 5 } }$ for murt to 200-fold for mutL. For heterospecific crosses, a decrease is also observed and varies from 2-fold for mut+ to ${ \bf 1 0 3 }$ -fold for mutS. Thus most of the interspecific recombination which takes place when either the MutS or the MutL protein is inactive depends on an active RecC protein and therefore proceeds via the RecBCD pathway. Thus, the major pathway for conjugational recombination in wild type $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli is also the major pathway for conjugational interspecific recombination $\mathbf { \delta E }$ coli-S typhimurium).

# Discussion

We have presented experiments indicating that interspecific recombination between $E$ coli and S typhimurium is a RecA dependent process and thus corresponds to a bona fide homologous recombination. The inactivation of one of the Mut functions $^ { ( \mathbf { L } , \pmb { S } }$ or H) allows a laxier recombination between the 2 different DNAs. With an inactivated MutL or MutS protein, which corresponds to the most 'permissive' condition for recombination between divergent sequences, there is roughly only a 10-fold decrease in recombination frequency which is due to the inter-species difference in the DNA sequences; anterior steps such as mating and DNA transfer already reducing the efficacy by 10-fold. When similar experiments were done in our laboratory with a Salmonella Hfr donor and Escherichia $\check { F ^ { - } }$ recipients proficient in restriction, the frequency of recombination in a mutS or mutL background was of the same order of magnitude, ie $\approx 1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ per Hfr (B Mahé, personal communication), as in the reverse crosses with Salmonella recipients deficient in the 2 major restriction systems (table I). This suggests that the restriction of foreign DNA is not the major barrier to interspecific mating and recombination in conjugation. Editing of recombination by the Mut proteins appears as the principal barrier to inter-species recombination. It has been observed that restriction is alleviated by SOS induction [16]. As interspecific recombination could induce the SOS system through its molecular intermediates, eg by the attempt to recombine diverged DNA followed by abortion of the mismatched heteroduplex DNA by the Mut HLS system, we are currently testing whether intergeneric mating results in SOs induction. Sommer et al have shown that conjugation of an $E$ coli Hfr with an $E$ coli Frecipient already partially induces SOs (personal communication). Dimpfl "and Echols showed that partially homologous sequences present in $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli rhsA and rhsB, probably $< \dot { 1 0 \% }$ divergent, recombine to give duplications with a higher frequency (a 10-fold increase) in a recA730 strain constitutively induced for SOS [17]. The formation of these duplications seemed to depend on the RecF pathway in which several genes are known to be induced in the SOS response [15]. The interspecific recombination we observed in mut backgrounds depends mainly on the RecBCD pathway and may thus be different from the SOS induced duplications from the above study. In a collaborative study with Dr Echols' laboratory, it was shown that the frequency of the same duplications increases in mut background (unpublished results) and the recombination then seems to depend on the RecBCD pathway (H Echols, personal communication) which would be consistent with results of conjugation experiments. The relation between SOS and intergeneric recombination is not yet clear. The fact that the intergeneric recombination in mut bacteria is mostly RecBCD-dependent, like the 'regular' homospecific conjugational recombination, indicates that a similar recombination process is taking place but with a more relaxed specificity, ie less stringently depending on the sequence identity of the two DNAs. This is in agreement with the effects of localised sequence divergence on recombination between mouse $\mathbf { \dot { V } _ { H } }$ sequences via the RecBCD pathway observed by Shen and Huang [18].
