# UV-Reactivation of Bacteriophage λ in Excision Repair-Deficient Hosts: Independence of Red Functions and Attachment Regions

The survival of ultraviolet (UV)-irradiated phage λ is strongly increased when the host cell is also UV irradiated prior to infection. This phenomenon is known as UV. reactivation $( I )$ . An enhanced efficiency of host-cell reactivation of the irradiated phage genome after UV irradiation of the host cell was one of the simplest working hypothesis advanced to account for UV-reactivation (2). Host-cell reactivation (HCR) is the repair of the phage genome which results from the excision of pyrimidine dimers; this repair process is controlled by the bacterial genes uvrA, uvrB, and uvrC (review, Ref. 3).

The above hypothesis implies that UV. reactivation should not be obtained in excision-deficient mutants as some authors found $( 4 , ~ 5 )$ . In contrast, other authors claimed that a slight UV-reactivation could be obtained in the absence of excision repair $\left( \mathcal { Q } , \ \theta { - } \vartheta \right)$ . This latter result was then attributed to some "residual HCR" (8, 9).

This paper reports data which show without ambiguities that for relatively low UV doses to $\lambda$ phage and to the host cell, three known types of excision-deficient mutants of $E$ coli K12 exhibit UV-reactivation of $\lambda$ phage. Moreover, UV-reactivation can be several times more efficient than in the wildtype bacteria at a given UV dose to the host cell.

In agreement with the hypothesis put forward by Kneser et al. (6) these results indicate that the mechanism of UV-reactivation does not necessarily involve hostcell reactivation.

Three mutants defective in excision repair show UV-reactivation with a maximal effect between 50 and 100 ergs $\mathrm { m m } ^ { - 2 }$ to the host cell (Fig. 1). This effect depends on low doses given to both phage and bacteria $( I \bar { \delta } )$ . Underestimation of such a requirement might be the reason why some authors failed to detect UV-reactivation with excision-deficient bacteria. The inability of recA mutants to show UV-reactivation of $\lambda$ phage (Fig. 1) was previously reported $( 4 , 8 )$ .

Independence of UV-reactivation of λ phage on the presence of attachment regions in host and phage chromosomes. Weigle (16) and Garen and Zinder (17) suggested that UV-reactivation might be due to recombination between homologous parts of the UVirradiated host and phage chromosomes. If this were so, one would expect that the presence or absence of a large piece of bacterial chromosome, extending from galT to uurB, would affect the extent of UV-reactivation. Furthermore, one might assume that the absence of att regions in the bacterial chromosome $\mathrm { ( B B ^ { \prime } ) }$ and in the λ chromosome (PP) should render difficult the contact between the bacterial and phage DNA and therefore influence the extent of UV-reactivation.

In order to test these hypotheses two combinations of $E$ coli and $\lambda$ mutants were used. These were (a) $E$ coli KMBL516, which carries the large deletion described earlier, and phage $\lambda b \mathcal { Q }$ which lacks at least one-half of the $\lambda$ att region (attachment system: --; $- \mathbf { P ^ { \prime } } )$ (Refs. 18, 19); (b) $E$ coli KMBL516 derivatives no. 6 and no. 10 possessing the attB region and phage $\lambda ^ { + }$ (attachment system: $\mathbf { B } \bar { \mathbf { B } } ^ { \prime } ; \mathbf { P P ^ { \prime } } )$ .

Figure 2 shows that in both cases the extent of UV-reactivation was the same. Apparently, UV-reactivation does not involve the presence of either att regions or the chromosomal region in $E .$ coli K12 extending from galT to uvrB.

Increased UV resistance of $\lambda b \mathcal { Q }$ when compared with $\lambda ^ { + }$ (Fig. 2) is probably due to the smaller size of the $\lambda b \mathcal { Q }$ DNA.

In conclusion, three known excision-deficient $E$ coli mutants show UV-reactivation of $\lambda$ phage. This phenomenon depends neither on the red function (Fig. 1) nor on the attachment regions in $\mathcal { E }$ coli and λ DNAs (Fig. 2). Probably, UV-reactivation does not involve excision repair of pyrimidine dimers in phage DNA at least for low UV doses delivered to the phage and to the host cell. Biochemical results recently published by Boyle and Setlow (20) lead to the same conclusion.
