# Complementary Action of Recombination and Excision in the Repair of Ultraviolet Irradiation Damage to DNA

MirosLav Radman†, Lorenzo Cordone‡, Draga Krsmanovic-Simic And Maurice Errera

Laboratoire de Biophysique et Radiobiologie Faculté des Sciences Université libre de Bruxelles, Belgium (Received 24 March 1969, and in revised form 26 November 1969)

The roles of the bacterial rec and uvr and of the λ red functions for the survival of ultraviolet-irradiated λ phage have been studied.

If only a small number of pyrimidine dimers is present in the λ DNA, repair is preferentially performed by recombination (rec and red functions); excision repair (uvr functions) being efficient only for extensive damage. This is probably related to the ultraviolet-induced inhibition of DNA replication.

A temporary inhibition of post-irradiation DNA replication was obtained using a $\lambda ,$ cistron O, thermosensitive mutant. It was thereby demonstrated that excision repair is efficient on non-replicating and recombination repair on replicating DNA.

It is suggested that recombination and excision are complementary mechanisms for repairing ultraviolet irradiation damage to DNA, excision taking place before DNA replication and recombination repair after and/or during replication. Regulation of DNA replication is therefore an important factor for the efficient repair of DNA.

# 1. Introduction

Two classes of bacterial mutations are known to increase the sensitivity of bacteria and infecting viruses to ultraviolet irradiation and some other mutagenic agents.

The uvr mutants are unable to excise (Boyce & Howard-Flanders, 1964; Setlow & Carrier, 1964) u.v.-irradiation induced pyrimidine dimers (Beukers & Berends, 1960; Wacker, Delweg & Weinblum, 1960). Three uvr genes have been identified (Howard-Flanders, Boyce & Theriot, 1966; van de Putte, van Sluis, van Dillewijn & Rörsch, 1965).

A second class, the rec mutants (Clark & Margulies, 1965; Howard-Flanders & Theriot, 1966; van de Putte, Zwenk & Rörsch, 1966) are both radiation sensitive and unable to perform genetic recombination. Escherichia coli mutated in the recA gene degrade their own DNA after u.v. irradiation (Howard-Flanders & Boyce, 1966) and show broken survival curves (Clark & Margulies, 1965; Howard-Flanders & Theriot, 1966; van de Putte et al., I966). At low u.v. doses recA- bacteria are extremely sensitive, but at higher doses the slope of the survival curve becomes very similar to the wild-type curve which indicates that the uor functions operate relatively more efficiently at high doses.

Excision and recombination appear as independent repair systems: a single mutation in the rec or uvr locus does not affect the functions of the other gene: uvr - bacteria and bacteriophage infecting this strain recombine normally, and with an increased frequency after u.v. irradiation (Jacob & Wollman, 1955; Baker & Haynes, 1967; Howard-Flanders, Boyce, Simson & Theriot, 1962). On the other hand, rec- bacteria can excise pyrimidine dimers from their DNA (Clark, Chamberlin, Boyce & HowardFlanders, 1966).

In the present work, the respective roles of genetic recombination and of excision for repairing u.v.-damaged phage λ DNA have been studied. The lethal damage to λ DNA at the u.v. doses used in these experiments was shown to be almost exclusively due to pyrimidine dimers (Krsmanovié-Simié, manuscript in preparation). The analysis of survival curves shows that recombination repair (controlled by the host rec and the λ red functions) is efficient only for small amounts of u.v. damage, whereas the excision repair mechanism starts to be efficient for highly damaged DNA.

Blocking DNA replication of an u.v.-irradiated O cistron thermosensitive mutant of $\lambda ,$ causes exclusively an increase of excision repair; conversely the replication of DNA favours recombination repair.

These results lead to the conclusion that the repair of DNA by the uvr system (excision) takes place before DNA replication, and that its repair by the rec system (recombination) takes place, as suggested by Rupp & Howard-Flanders (1968), after and/or during replication.

# 4. Discussion

Excision repair of u.v.-induced pyrimidine dimers is thought to occur through the following steps: recognition of the lesion and incision, excision and broadening of the gap, gap-filling repair replication and covalent joining by the polynucleotide ligase (see for a review, Howard-Flanders, 1968). An implication of this model is that unfinished excision repair should be affected by semi-conservative DNA replication which would lead to lethal discontinuous double helices.

On the other hand, if the replication of a continuous DNA strand still containing pyrimidine dimers occurs, then, according to Rupp & Howard-Flanders (1968), a gap will appear in the daughter strand opposite each dimer. Post-replicative excision of such dimers is also expected to cause lethal double-strand breaks. It is therefore reasonable to suppose that excision of pyrimidine dimers can lead to efficient repair only before the first post-irradiation DNA replication.

Recombination can occur only in the presence of two homologous DNA duplexes, i.e. in the case of single DNA molecules, only after and/or during DNA replication, between the two newly-synthesized daughter double helices.

The results presented in this paper do suggest that efficient excision repair occurs only before the first post-irradiation DNA replication, recombination repair taking place after and/or during DNA replication. In this sense excision and recombination repair are complementary mechanisms. This is found to be the case when one enquires into the number of pyrimidine dimers necessary to reduce survival of $\lambda$ phage to $1 / \theta ,$ when assayed on the various bacterial strains (Table l). Two dimers are sufficient to inactivate phage in the red-/rec-uvr" system. This is very similar to the finding for $\pmb { \cal { R } }$ , coli deficient in both excision and recombination (Howard-Flanders, Theriot $\pmb { \delta }$ Stedeford, 1969) where 1·3 dimers are found to be lethal.

If recombination and excision were independent and uncorrelated repair processes, then the wild-type $( r e d ^ { + } / r e c ^ { + } u v r ^ { + } )$ system would support up to $\mathbf { 4 } + \mathbf { 7 } { \cdot } \mathbf { 2 } = \mathbf { 1 } \mathbf { 1 } { \cdot } \mathbf { 2 }$ pyrimidine dimers in $\lambda$ DNA. However, it repairs about 38 dimers (see Table l) suggesting that excision and recombination are complementary and in addition co-operative repair systems. A similar relationship between excision and recombination repair was found in $\pmb { \mathscr { L } }$ coli (Howard-Flanders, 1968).

Results in Table 2 clearly show that a blook in DNA replication enhances the efficiency of excision repair; on the contrary, replication favours recombination repair. Identical results have been obtained with $\pmb { \mathscr { L } }$ , coli mutants thermosensitive for DNA replication: excision only repairs with high efficiency u.v.-damaged E. coli DNA before the first replication (Radman & Errera, 1970). These results support suggestions that various recovery phenomena such as liquid holding (see Harm, 1966), starvation of bacteria and stationary phase of growth (Howard-Flanders, 1968; Horii & Suzuki, 1968) could simply be due to the efficient excision repair during a temporary inhibition of DNA synthesis.

We would like to suggest that the complementarity or co-operativeness of excision and replication repair may simply be a consequence of the optimal timing for DNA replication. One of the major effects of u.v. irradiation in vivo is the inhibition of DNA synthesis (for review see Setlow, 1968). At low doses, replication will not be inhibited and therefore the sister exchange between daughter molecules will be possible (see scheme below). This scheme implicates the existence of a critical dose, above which DNA replication is inhibited by u.v. irradiation for a sufficiently long time to allow excision repair to take place. Pyrimidine clusters in the promoter regions (Szybalski, Kubinski & Sheldrick, 1966) could contribute to this control : following u.v. irradiation these will contain more dimers than other regions on the chromosome, and would be expected to inhibit DNA transcription and replication allowing efficient excision repair.

Genetic recombination induced by u.v. irradiation (Jacob & Wollman, 1955) seems to be a consequence of recombination repair. Baker & Haynes (1967) studied u.v.-induced recombination among $\lambda$ phage in the same host bacteria used in the present experiments. They found a linear increase in recombination frequency for low u.v. doses to $\pmb { \lambda }$ both in uvr+ and uvr- bacteria, but only in uvr+ bacteria did the recombination frequency remain constant beyond a critical dose, above which one expects that excision starts eliminating pyrimidine dimers (Fig. 1).

The recombination frequency curves obtained by Baker & Haynes (1967) and the λ survival curves (Fig. 1) are in agreement with the scheme suggested above. The u.v.-induced recombination among $\lambda$ phage occurred also in recA- hosts (Baker & Haynes, 1967), i.e. it can be promoted by $\lambda$ red functions, expressed only after λ DNA replication (M. Yarmolinsky and P. Brachet, personal communications).

In conclusion, the control of replication and transcription of u.v.-irradiated DNA is expected to be an essential factor for the optimal repair by excision and recombination.
