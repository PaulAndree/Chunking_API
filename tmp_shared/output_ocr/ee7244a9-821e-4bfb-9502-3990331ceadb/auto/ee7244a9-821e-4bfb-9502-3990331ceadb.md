# DNA Repair Systems and Bacterial Evolution

M. RADMAN, F. TADDEI, AND I. MATIC INSERM E9916, Faculté de Médécine, Necker Enfants Malades, Université René Descartes, 75015 Paris, France

Evolution, the fundamental strategy of life, is an interplay of genetic variation and phenotypic selection. Longterm survival of natural populations relies on their capacity to adapt to unpredictable environmental changes via genetic variability generated by mutation and recombination. Random mutation produces new heritable alleles (vertically transmitted variability), whereas genetic recombination produces new combinations of preexisting alleles (horizontally acquired variability). This property of genetic recombination improves the effectiveness of mutagenesis because it allows for association of multiple adaptive mutations, as well as for their separation from much more frequent deleterious mutations.

Until recently, it was generally assumed that the mutation rates are the lowest possible given the cost of antimutator mechanisms. The dogma of molecular and evolutionary biology was that mutation is an unavoidable stochastic event due to the limits of the precision of biological processes-a trade-off between the ideal and the real. Evolutionary biologists have considered that the biodiversity is immense, in particular for bacterial populations, and that adaptive evolution works through selection upon preexisting variability. The process of mutagenesis was not supposed to be affected by the selective pressures. This may be so when considering global biodiversity, but selection usually acts locally on limited size populations and there the diversity is often limited, in particular for populations having experienced a recent selection (bottleneck) (Arjan et al. 1999). The genetic "arms race" between parasites and host defense systems is particularly revealing. Viruses and bacteria mutate extensively in an attempt to generate rare variants that can escape the host's immune system. In turn, the immune system mutates to create and try antibodies that recognize these new variants. Clearly, these and similar adaptive processes (e.g., the somatic evolution of tumors) are limited by the supply of mutations.

When adaptation is limited by the available genetic diversity, genetically unstable bacterial populations are expected to adapt, at least short term, more rapidly than nonmutators because of increased supply of mutations (Taddei et al. 1997). Two classes of genes are known to accelerate mutation and/or recombination rates in bacterial populations: stress-inducible wild-type genes, usually part of the SOS regulon, and genes whose functional loss, or down-regulation, increases the rate of genetic variability (mutator and/or hyper-rec mutants). The coexistence of these two evolution-driving strategies, one acting at the level of individual cells and the other at the population level (Radman et al. 2000), is discussed here, as well as the molecular mechanisms involved.

# BENEFITS OF GENETIC MUTATORS

Odd as it may appear, bacteria sometimes find their evolutionary success through a biochemical failure in DNA replication or repair processes, a genetic defect that helps them not only to adapt, but also to become adaptable. Such mutants have lost some specific function; this loss results in increased rates of mutation and/or recombination in the entire genome.

It is well known that most newly arising mutations either have no effect or are harmful, whereas only rare specific mutations are favorable during adaptation under particular selective conditions. The ratio of deleterious to favorable mutations in random mutagenesis may be as high as four to five orders of magnitude (Taddei et al. 1997). It therefore came as a surprise that high mutation rates are favored during adaptation despite the high cost incurred by the generation of numerous deleterious mutations (Sniegowski et al. 1997; Taddei et al. 1997). It is, however, not surprising that when adaptation is achieved, low mutation rates are favored (Tröbner and Piechocki 1984). Computer simulations (Taddei et al. 1997; Tenaillon et al. 1999, 2000) and experiments (Chao and Cox 1983; Tröbner and Piechocki 1984; Sniegowski et al. 1997) are consistent with this paradigm.

Because it is possible to select, in the laboratory, bacterial mutants with increased fidelity of protein or DNA biosynthesis (Fijalkowska et al. 1993 and references therein), it is clear that there was no durable selective pressure in nature for maximal fidelity. Apparently, selection for genetic fidelity is balanced by selection for efficiency and robustness. All approaches hint to trade-offs between the cost and benefit of mutations in the course of adaptive evolution. High mutation rates are costly, but when this is the only way to buy survival, then such cost is paid: Successive selections for specific adaptive mutations will consistently lead to second-order selection of mutator mutants (Mao et al. 1997; Taddei et al. 1997; Tenaillon et al. 1999). However, despite their short-term success, the cost of mutators is such (see below) that they are most likely the evolutionary dead ends (Funchain et al. 2000), and the only long-term beneficiaries of mutator activity may be the genes/alleles (or operons) created by them and then transferred to genetically stable cells (Tenaillon et al. 2000).

# SECOND-ORDER SELECTION FOR INCREASED MUTATION AND RECOMBINATION RATES

Theory and experiments show that successive selections for any particular mutations, or new alleles, will also indirectly select for high mutation or recombination rates (Mao et al. 1997; Taddei et al. 1997). This phenomenon is named second-order selection. How does the secondorder selection work? The fraction of spontaneously arising mutator mutations in a fresh bacterial laboratory culture is approximately $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ (Boe et al. 2000). Strong mutators, such as the mismatch repair-deficient mutators, increase the mutation rate by 1001000-fold (Glickman and Radman 1980) and the interspecies recombination rate by about 1000-fold (Rayssiguier et al. 1989). A million-fold enrichment for mutators, making them the predominant population, will occur after three successive selections for specific mutations (Mao et al. 1997). A single selection for an Escherichia-Salmonella interspecies recombination event enriched for the mismatch repair-deficient mutators up to $1 0 \%$ and one or two further recombination events coselected up to $100 \%$ the mismatch repair-deficient mutators (P. Francino, unpubl.; J.H. Miller et al., this volume). Apparently, the selection for mutation or recombination events second-order-selects for mutator or hyper-rec mutants. This can be regarded as a short-term evolution of the capacity to evolve: By selecting for particular genetic events, one coselects for the general mechanism that produces such events, hence the second-order selection. Metaphorically, one could say that, in terms of adaptive evolution, bacteria have now "learned how to learn," but, mechanistically, mutators increase in frequency because of their genomic association (hitch-hiking) with favorable mutations generated by the mutator activity. Therefore, sex and recombination should lower the probability of mutator fixation (Tenaillon et al. 2000).

For natural populations, one can thus expect that, depending on the way the new selected traits have been acquired (vertically, by mutation, or horizontally, by recombination), mutator and/or hyper-rec mutants could be locally greatly enriched for. For example, new metabolic operons, or antibiotic resistance genes, can be acquired horizontally, but new metabolic specificities and some antibiotic resistance alleles can be generated by point mutations. Indeed, natural populations of bacteria bear a much higher frequency of genetic mutators than one would expect from the rate of their emergence and the decreased fitness of individual mutator cells (see below).

# COST OF GENETIC MUTATORS

In vitro (Chao and Cox 1983; Mao et al. 1997; Sniegowski et al. 1997) and in vivo (A. Giraud et al., unpubl.) experiments and computer simulations (Taddei et al. 1997; Tenaillon et al. 1999, 2000) all suggest that bacteria are better off being mutators while adapting and nonmutators when adapted. Funchain et al. (2000) have shown that some mutator clones can go to extinction following successive bottlenecks. In terms of supply of mutations, the effect of increasing by 100-fold the mutation rate is equivalent to a 100-fold increase in population size (the amount of new mutations will be equivalent). But, unlike mutators, the adapted wild type does not bear the cost of the ongoing deleterious mutation load. Therefore, considering the "economy" of adaptation, bacterial populations would be better off being mutators while adapting and nonmutators when adapted. There is now compelling evidence that bacteria are endowed with such inducible mutator capacity (for reviews, see Radman 1999; Radman et al. 2000).

# THE PRIME ROLE OF ALL DNA REPAIR SYSTEMS

Aside from heredity and evolution, i.e., the fine balance between the maintenance of the memory of evolution (DNA sequence) and its variability necessary for adaptive evolution, the DNA repair systems' prime role is the maintenance of the physical integrity of genomic DNA strands. Neither the dividing nor the nondividing cells can function with DNA strand discontinuities, or chemical blocks, because neither replication nor transcription can fully operate. Presumably, the subject of this Symposium—biological responses to DNA damage—is so important for life because of this fundamental problem. Cells require the signaling systems to monitor faithfully any DNA strand discontinuity or nonreadable DNA damage. The first such cellular response to DNA damage was discovered in bacteria, the SOS response, which shares all of the principal hallmarks of the eukaryotic responses to DNA damage, i.e., the arrest of DNA replication and cell division and induction or enhancement of DNA repair activities until the DNA strands become clean and continuous. The SOS response will be discussed further below.

How is the stability of DNA sequence maintained? A panoply of proteins has evolved whose action, at many levels and through diverse strategies, results in the observed stability of genetic information (Friedberg et al. 1995). The double-stranded structure is essential in allowing DNA protection and repair proteins to maintain the original nucleotide sequence despite frequent chemical insults to the fine DNA structure and the intrinsic imperfection of DNA replication machinery.

The basic strategies to conserve the DNA sequence (Fig. 1) involve: (1) The maintenance of the chemical purity of ingredients for DNA replication, i.e., the repair of DNA templates and the enzymatic chemical purification and equilibration of dNTP pools; (2) the high-fidelity DNA copy machine, i.e., efficient selection of correct dNTPs by DNA polymerase and the immediate removal of the mistake by the proofreading exonuclease; and (3) the quality control of new strands, i.e., the postreplicative mismatch repair of the new strands to match the sequence of the template. The latter system lowers the frequency of spontaneous mutations by 1001000-fold.

# MISMATCH REPAIR AND GENETIC STABILITY: EDITING OF DNA REPLICATION AND RECOMBINATION

It is remarkable that the ubiquitous mismatch repair system edits not only DNA replication, but also homologous recombination, which itself is one of the most versatile DNA repair mechanisms for establishing DNA strand continuity (Friedberg et al. 1995). Mechanistically, this double editing is fairly well understood. All mismatch repair systems appear to use the transient strand discontinuity (nick) to trigger and direct the repair event. In a small fraction of bacteria (those endowed with the Dam methylase for methylating adenine residues in the $5 ^ { \prime } \mathrm { G A T C }$ sequence), such a nick is assured by the MutH protein, which cuts the unmethylated (newly synthesized) strand at the GATC sequence proximal to the mismatch. The nicking occurs only when the MutS protein has detected the mismatch and attracted the MutL partner (for review, see Modrich and Lahue 1996). The helicase II in $E _ { \ast }$ coli peels off the strand from the free ends, and the peeled off strand is degraded by exonucleases. It may be that all newly synthesized DNA is discontinuous in vivo (see Varlet et al. 1996) and that the ligase activity gives the impression of the leading/lagging strand asymmetry. Perhaps only the MutH utilizing bacteria can allow themselves to have a hyperactive DNA ligase. Alternatively, the direct interaction of mismatch repair proteins with the DNA polymerase complex may allow them to distinguish between the template and the copy. The strand direction is one of the least understood details of mismatch repair for most of the bacterial species and all of the eukaryotic species.

The mismatch-stimulated (as detected by MutS and MutL binding) localized helicase-catalyzed melting of DNA may account for the editing of both DNA replication and recombination: In replication, the mismatched Okazaki fragment would be aborted, and in recombination, the mismatched invading strand would be aborted (Fig. 2) (Rayssiguier et al. 1989; Stambuk and Radman 1998). Hence, when MutS or MutL are not functional, the mutation rate goes up, and recombination between similar sequences can take place (for review, see Radman et al. 1995; Modrich and Lahue 1996). Thus, we speak of the mutator phenotype and the relaxed genetic barrier between the species' (plus the chromosomal rearrangements) phenotypes. The latter phenotype allows for efficient horizontal transfer of genes from diverged related species, which is often referred to as the hyper-rec phenotype, which, however, does not apply to recombination between genetically identical partners (Vulic et al. 1997).

# MUTATION RATES IN NATURE

Mutation rates in microbes cultivated in the laboratory are generally very low (Drake et al. 1998), but in natural populations of $E$ coli and Salmonella enterica, strains with high mutation rates (mutators) often exceed a frequency of $1 \%$ (Treffers et al. 1954; Jyssum 1960; Gross and Siegel 1981; Tröbner and Piechocki 1984; LeClerc et al. 1996; Matic et al. 1997). The fraction of strong mutators among Pseudomonas isolated from the lung of cystic fibrosis patients is about $2 0 \%$ (Oliver et al. 2000).

The strand is degraded in the direction of the Holliday junction, thus separating the two parental molecules. Hence, mismatch repair prevents recombination between similar sequences but does not affect recombination between identical sequences.

Most of the strong mutator mutants that have emerged in nature and in the laboratory are mismatch repair-deficient mutants. There does not appear to be any special reason for their abundance compared with other mutator mutants: The large target size of the four mismatch repair genes, in particular the mutS, can account for their abundance (Bregeon et al. 1999). Other potential mutator genes are either small (e.g., mutT) or essential for the viability (e.g., genes encoding replicative DNA polymerase). However, the long-term robustness of mutator cells is compromised by the continuous production of deleterious mutations (Funchain et al. 2000). It is likely that the high fraction of mutators reflects a recent, or ongoing, selective pressure. In fine, most mutator populations may well go to extinction, but their selected favorable alleles may survive in other genomes following a horizontal transfer. Furthermore, mutator bacteria may be able to regain a lower mutation rate once adaptation has been achieved (Denamur et al. 2000).

# THE SOS REPAIR 30 YEARS LATER

In November 1970, one of us (M.R.) had written and privately circulated a memo among the leading experts in the field of DNA repair and mutagenesis proposing that E. coli possesses an inducible DNA repair system. This hypothetical repair, which we call SOS repair, is manifested only following damage to DNA and requires de novo protein synthesis. Mutagenesis by ultraviolet light is observed only under conditions of functional SOS repair: We therefore suspect that this is a mutation-prone repair" (Radman 1974). In fact, the initial term was "SOS replication" to imply that it is a translesion DNA replication rather than a mechanism for removal of DNA damage (for an historical account, see Friedberg 1997). This memo was a step forward from an earlier paper (Defais et al. 1971) which showed that bacteriophage $\gimel$ UV-induced mutagenesis, repair, and lysogenic induction are apparently coexpressed. To this list, the memo has further added the already published inhibition of cell division (filamentous growth or filamentation) and prophage induction (Witkin 1967), and (as the key ingredient) cellular mutagenesis, in a scenario of a coregulated global cellular response to DNA damage. To honor Weigle's discovery of enhanced reactivation of UV-irradiated bacteriophage $\gimel$ in UV-irradiated host cells (which he called UV-reactivation [Weigle 1953])—a condition sine qua non to obtain phage mutagenesis—the phenomenon was renamed Wreactivation. In addition to the notion of coordinated regulation of metabolically unrelated genes, already present in Witkin's paper, the iconoclast aspect of the SOS hypothesis (there was no response to the memo for 2 years) was the notion that bacterial mutagenesis is not an unavoidable stochastic event, but a well-regulated, genetically programmed process. The apparent smell of Lamarckism (bacteria mutate when needed!) was apparently scary at that time. Much later came the excitement, and controversy, of the Cairns et al. (1988) paper claiming the gene-directed inducible mutagenesis that is independent of SOS (Cairns and Foster 1991). Neither claim, except the inducibility, resisted the experimental tests. Today, thanks to the discovery of two SOS-induced mutagenic DNA polymerases and the studies of the effect of mutator alleles on the fitness of bacterial populations, it appears that SOS mutagenesis is a device to render Darwinian selection more efficient. Eventually, the mutagenic SOS response to bacterial starvation (or aging) on plates, involving also the metabolic cAMP regulon, was discovered (Taddei et al. 1995), and the role of SOS in adaptive mutagenesis was established (McKenzie et al. 2000). Hence, much of the inducible adaptive mutagenesis is an SOS phenomenon. The idea of "inducible evolution" (Radman 1980; Echols 1981) is gaining experimental support.

# TRANSLESION SYNTHESIS: A MECHANISM FOR SURVIVAL AND EVOLUTION

Damages, or lesions, in DNA templates that are noninstructive for base pairing interrupt the DNA replication process by blocking the elongation of nascent chains. Translesion synthesis (TLS) is the DNA-copying process that can overcome such blockage and allow the completion of DNA replication despite the presence of lesions. TLS was proposed to produce "targeted" mutations, i.e., mutations occurring at the site of DNA damage (for reviw, see Friedberg et al. 199). Initially, it was proposed that TLS is an error-prone DNA synthesis as a key component of the stress-inducible cellular SOS response in bacteria (Radman 1974). TLS was first demonstrated in vivo, by a physical analysis, as an SOS-induced process that allows completion of the complementary strand synthesis on UV-irradiated single-stranded $\Phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phage DNA following infection of irradiated $E$ coli host cells (Caillet-Fauquet et al. 1977). This is the condition sine qua non to obtain UV-induced mutagenesis of this singlestranded phage. Later on, DNA sequencing of UV-induced mutations and construction of DNA with a single defined UV lesion provided further evidence that the UVinduced mutations indeed occur opposite lesions, such as pyrimidine dimers (for review, Friedberg et al. 1995). However, even after the identification oinducible genes, and their products, which are specifically required for induced mutagenesis, the mechanism of the mutagenic TLS in bacteria remained elusive until 1999. Early attempts (19751978) by one of us (M.R.) with S. Spadari, G. Villani, and S. Boiteux to detect in vitro bacterial and mammalian radiation-induced error-prone DNA polymerases activities were unsuccessful and left us only the observation that the AMV reverse transcriptase is capable of copying across UV lesions in $\Phi { \bf X } 1 7 4$ DNA that normally block $E$ coli DNA polymerases I and III. The latter blockage was accompanied by the unproductive turnover of dNTPs to dNMPs by successive DNA polymerase and proofreading exonuclease activities—a process we have called polymerase idling (Villani et al. 1978). Thus, at that time, we concluded that the TLS required at least the absence or inhibition of the $3 ^ { \prime }$ to $5 ^ { \prime }$ proofreading exonuclease (Villani et al. 1978).

# TRANSLESION SYNTHESIS: UNUSUAL DNAPOLYMERASES ACT AS DNA "BAND-AIDS"CAPABLE OF AVOIDING AND CREATINGMUTATIONS

Isolation from bacteria and eukaryotes of numerous members of a superfamily of new mutagenic DNA polymerases, performing TLS in the test tube, was such a surprise that in year 1999 more reviews were published than were original papers. However, the SOS hypothesis that guided this research was there for nearly three decades, and the pioneering work of C. Lawrence and colleagues on yeast TLS polymerases Rev1 and Rev3/7 (DNA polymerase $\zeta$ has been published since 1996 (Nelson et al. 1996). The discoveries that the properties of E. coli DNA polymerases V (UmuD'C) and IV (DinB) can account adequately for genetic experiments was of fundamental importance: The former is responsible for mutations targeted to the lesion and the latter is responsible for "untargeted" mutations (for reviews, see Radman 1999; Friedberg et al. 2000). Bacteriophage $\lambda$ clear plaque mutation assays allowed a morphological distinction of targeted and untargeted mutations (Caillet-Fauquet et al. 1984). DNA polymerases IV and $\mathrm { v }$ add nucleotides one by one in an error-prone fashion: pol V produces base substitution and frameshift mutations opposite the damaged site, and pol IV produces predominantly frameshift mutations explained by its capacity to extend synthesis on unpaired termini (Wagner et al. 1999). Thus, it appeared as if pol V allowed for a mutation-generating TLS, whereas pol IV allowed for a general transient mutator effect, as deduced from the observation that only the UV-induced untargeted mutations are subject to mismatch repair (Caillet-Fauquet et al. 1984), suggesting that the template bears no lesion (or an unstable lesion or structure). The former activity helps survival and both appear to increase genetic variability (for review, see Radman 1999).

A real surprise came from research on eukaryotes. Two observations defined a puzzling paradox: One was the finding that the yeast dinB/UmuDC homolog Rad30 and its human homolog Rad30A (mutated in human disease predisposing to UV-induced skin cancer xeroderma pigmentosum variant [XPV] and called DNA polymerase η) are capable of copying correctly the UV-induced cyclobutane thymine dimers, through addition of two adenines, while being more error-prone than any other known DNA or RNA polymerases (see Gerlach et al., this volume). How can a DNA polymerase be "error-free" when copying a damaged site (amounting to a memory of the sequence before it was damaged by UV light) and error-prone when copying normal DNA? Yet, the data in vivo and in vitro are perfectly clear: The XPV cells are hypermutable by UV, and pol- $\boldsymbol { \cdot } \boldsymbol { \eta }$ copies thymine dimers with adenines (Wang et al. 1991; for review, see Friedberg et al. 2000).

The following is a scenario for the evolution of TLS DNA polymerases: Whenever a nonreplicable lesion in

DNA template is detected and the right nucleotide terminally added to the primer opposite the lesion, the molecule becomes replicable by regular DNA polymerases and the original sequence is restored. Such cells would survive not only because their DNA becomes replicable, but also because the cost of TLS (usually a targeted mutation) will not have to be paid by mutation as seems to be the case of TLS with bacterial and some other eukaryotic DNA mutases. Unlike mutagenic TLS, any nonmutagenic TLS is a true DNA repair process that restores, in the copy strand, the damaged template sequence. Following such TLS, the damage in the template can be repaired by standard base or nucleotide excision repair systems restoring the original sequence also in the template strand. Most recent results show that each of the three E. coli SOS-induced DNA polymerases (II, IV, and V) can be either mutagenic or antimutagenic depending on the nature ofDNA lesion (Napolitano et al. 2000). Yet, all SOS-induced mutageneses depend on these DNA polymerases. The question therefore arises: Is induced mutagenesis the result of a confused, inappropriate, recruitment of SOS polymerases to different DNA lesions or is there a programmed "misuse" of these polymerases to increase the mutation rate under these selective conditions (another case of second-order selection)?

# TLS POLYMERASES AND MUTAGENESIS IN REPLICATION AND RECOMBINATION

The basic mechanism for all unusual prokaryotic and eukaryotic DNA polymerases may be similar: The polymerase may initially just bind to the free $3 ^ { \prime }$ ends (singlestranded overhangs), like an inactive terminal transferase, in either replication or recombination intermediates. In recombination, such free single-stranded ends can be annealed to the complementary template by the homologous recombination proteins (strand invasion step in recombination), such as the RecA and its eukaryotic homologs. Thus, created hybrid DNA would be an immediate primer/template for the synthesis by the $3 ^ { \prime }$ -OHbound TLS polymerase which would act in its errorprone mode because the template is not damaged! The DNA strand-break-triggered, recombination-dependent and pol- $\boldsymbol { \zeta }$ -dependent mutagenesis targeted to homologous sequences was already described in yeast by Strathern and colleagues (Strathern et al. 1995; Holbeck and Strathern 1997) and postulated previously as the mechanism of bacterial adaptive mutagenesis (Harris et al. 1994; for review, see Foster 1999).

The attractive feature of this model is that it can account for the mutagenic and antimutagenic activity of TLS polymerases in replication and for the exclusively mutagenic activity in recombination. In replication, the free $3 ^ { \prime }$ -OH end is generated in the newly synthesized strand abandoned by the replicase because of the blocking lesion in the template which now can be copied by the TLS polymerase in the error-free fashion (by the appropriate TLS polymerase) or in the error-prone fashion (by the inappropriate TLS polymerase). Thus, it may be that the immunoglobulin gene hypermutation is generated by the unusual polymerases and targeted to the V gene by DNA breakage without involvement of other DNA lesions? There is a great suspense in awaiting the results of mouse gene knockout experiments to know whether the unusual DNA polymerases that are expressed in lymphoid tissues ( $\mu$ and $\lambda$ ;Aoufouchi et al. 2000; Dominguez et al. 2000) are responsible for somatic hypermutation in the immune system.

Clearly, limiting the activity of error-prone polymerases to the troubled sites in DNA becomes a major issue. It was shown that bacterial pol V activity is limited to the RecA nucleofilament, which is itself diagnostic of nonreplicable DNA (for review, see Radman 1999). The slow, step-by-step distributive copying mechanism of all TLS polymerases may itself assure that only the troubled sites in DNA are their substrates: As soon as the normal base pairs are created, regular faithful and processive replicative polymerases would take over the replication process. Alternatively, the break-directed mutagenic DNA synthesis in recombination would be localized to the repair tract.

# EVOLUTIONARY ORIGIN OF TLS DNA POLYMERASES: A SPECULATION

Two models come to mind to account for the most surprising property of these unusual DNA polymerases, which is the correct copying of a particular DNA lesion but an erroneous copying of intact DNA. These polymerases could be specific DNA-lesion-recognizing proteins that have coevolved a specific template-dependent deoxynucleotidyl terminal transferase activity inserting the bases that are the complement of the undamaged template bases. Alternatively, they could be DNA polymerases with a flexible active site capable of copying the B and non-B DNA structures, albeit inefficiently. In either case, they could correctly copy the specific DNA modification but erroneously copy the normal DNA. For instance, pol $\cdot \boldsymbol { \mathrm { n } }$ would avoid the mutagenic effect of TT dimers by AA incorporation, pol-l may be suppressing the mutagenic effect of the deaminated cytosine (and 5- methyl-cytosine) by preferentially incorporating G opposite all three pyrimidines (Zhang et al. 2000), whereas Revl may be suppressing the mutagenic effect of depurination (G loss) by its deoxycytidyl transferase activity, etc.

The coevolution of a specific transferase activity with specific damage recognition (directly or via another protein partner) would account for the multitude of UmuD'C/DinB-like DNA polymerases in eukaryotes, because each major lesion may require its own "error-free" polymerase. For instance, oxidative damage to purines and pyrimidines would presumably require other specific DNA polymerases. The observation of very high expression of most "error-prone" polymerases in testes (spermatocytes) (see Gerlach et al. [this volume] and references therein) could now cease to be a paradox: These polymerases could be a sort of DNA "Band-Aid," patching gaps opposite lesions before the male germ line DNA is packaged into sperm that is devoid of repair enzymes. Such a process would restore the continuity of DNA strands, in particular when the TLS is followed by the repair of bypassed DNA damage. In any case, "error-free" TLS polymerases define a conceptually new, replicative, DNA repair system for damaged single-stranded DNA. The existence of DNA polymerases with a strong basebias (e.g., pol- $\boldsymbol { \eta }$ and pol $\mathbf { \nabla } \cdot \mathbf { \kappa }$ for making AT-rich DNA and pol-1 and Rev1 for making GC-rich DNA) offers a hypothesis for the origin of species-specific genomic base composition and codon bias if species (or even chromosomal regions in eukaryotes, namely, isochores; Bernardi 2000) differed in the respective mutagenic activities of unusual polymerases. TLS polymerases might be fastevolving enzymes. and there may be a wide-ranging polymorphism of respective activities and specificities of TLS polymerases that could reflect the dominant ecological and/or metabolic "niche" experienced in the evolutionary past of each of the species or even strains. Alternatively, or in addition, some or all unusal DNA polymerases may act as real mutases in meiosis (Gerlach et al., this volume).

# CONCLUSIONS: COMPARING DIFFERENT EVOLUTION-ACCELERATING STRATEGIES

Given this interpretation of different strategies for accelerated adaptive evolution, it is puzzling that rather strong genetic mutators are found among natural isolates of enterobacteria collected from all over the world and from most diverse environments (LeClerc et al. 1996; Matic et al. 1997). Why have bacteria been tricked into the costly strategy of second-order selection of strong genetic mutators when inducible mutator activity is the alternative? It may be, however, that the "smart" systems of inducible mutators and hypermutable genes are not efficient enough for adaptive evolution. For instance, hypermutable genes with microsatellite runs are useful only for adaptation to a single specific and recurrent selective pressure, e.g., the immune system (Field et al. 1999). Although the inducible mutator systems act on the entire genome, they will not be producing diversity during the periods of "easy life," but will do so during nonlethal selection. Therefore, when a lethal selective pressure hits an isolated small population, the inducible mutators may not have the opportunity to produce adaptive mutations because all bacteria would be instantly killed. Strong genetic mutators are particularly favored when adaptation requires several genomic mutations, which may be the case for most adaptations to complex environmental changes (Ninio 1991).

In anthropomorphic terms, we may consider genetic mutators as genetic altruists: they pay a high price (due to accumulation of deleterious mutations) to provide the population with mutations/alleles that can save life under new and most diverse selective pressures. Figure 3 shows the coexistence of two mutational strategies among natural isolates of Escherichia and Shigella species: Strains with a strong inducible mutator capacity seem to accumulate fewer constitutive mutators than strains with weak inducible mutator activity. Ongoing research in our laboratory, both modeling and experimental, already shows some compelling evidence that genes which have evolved in the mutationally damaged genomes of strong mutators can be rescued by nonmutators, via sex and recombination (Tenaillon et al. 2000). Thus, the mutator subpopulations may arise and live as ephemeral genetic engineers which are themselves evolutionary dead ends, but which are often the source of new adaptive alleles that are transmitted horizontally to the nonmutators. Alternatively, mutator bacteria that have generated adaptation without having the time to accumulate deleterious mutations may restore lower mutation rate by suppressor mutations, reversion, or horizontal transfer of functional antimutator genes (Denamur et al. 2000).

Indeed, over evolutionary times, horizontal gene transfer has repeatedly allowed transition from mismatch repair-deficient to mismatch repair-proficient status (Denamur et al. 2000). This is evidenced by the incongruencies between phylogenies of these genes and the housekeeping genes (Denamur et al. 2000). Furthermore, there is about $9 0 \%$ correlation between the sequence mosaicisms of the different mismatch repair genes and their hyper-recombination phenotypes.

As the hyper-rec phenotype experienced by mismatch repair-deficient bacteria is not limited to their own loci, they may also have generated a high degree of genomic mosaicism. Thus, the global mosaic structure of enterobacterial genomes (Milkman and McCane 1995) may have resulted from the described evolutionary scenarios, and the periods of mismatch repair deficiency may have been the periods of the most intense genetic engineering of bacterial genomes in the course of their adaptive evolution.
