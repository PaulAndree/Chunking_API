Istituto di Fisica dellUniversilà and Grappi Nazionali Struttura della Matcria of theConsiglio Nazionale delle Ricerche, Palermo, Italy; and Laboratoire de Biophysique et Racicbiologie, Université Libre de Bruxelles, Belgium

Accepted January 7, 1970

Experimental results are reported, showing that one of the important factors in the UV-induced increase of recombination is the A-T content of the DNA regions involved. A model explaining the results is suggested and discussed.

It is well known that ultraviolet irradiation of phage increases the absolute frequency of recombination (Jacob and Wollman, 1955). It has been suggested (Baker and Haynes, 1967) that this increase is due to UV-induced pyrimidine dimers in the parental phage DNA (Setlow, 1964). These are mostly thymine dimers or thyminecytosine dimers, where the efficiency of dimers formation is: $\widehat { \mathrm { T T } } > \widehat { \mathrm { C T } } > \widehat { \mathrm { C C } }$ for Escherichia coli (Setlow and Carrier, 1966) and $\widehat { \mathrm { T T } } = \widehat { \mathrm { C T } }$ for phage $\lambda$ (KrsmanovieSimic, 1969) for UV doses in the biological range, $\widehat { \mathrm { C C } }$ being an absolute minority. As only adjacent pyrimidines can dimerize, the above increase in recombination should be expected to be higher in the A-T rich regions of DNA, than in the A-T poor regions.

We wish to report on experiments undertaken with an aim to checking this hypothesis. If a genetic cross is performed in w hich two counterselectionable markers $a$ and $b$ are placed on opposite sides of a third morphological marker $c _ { \scriptscriptstyle \mathrm { i } }$ ,the ratio $\scriptstyle ( \mathbf { c } ^ { + } / \mathbf { c } ) _ { \mathrm { r e c ~ a } ^ { + } \mathrm { b } ^ { + } }$ is a measure of the relative distance of markers $\pmb { a }$ and $b$ from c. Moreover, if the region of DNA included between $^ { a }$ and $c$ is richer in A-T than the region between c and $b$ , this ratio should decrease upon UV irradiation (See fig. 1, a).

In order to pursue this idea, we have exploited the fact that the region located between the N and CII cistrons is on the average much richer in A-T content than the CII-R region in λ phage DNA. This follows from the fact (Inman, 1967; Skalka et al., 1968; Jaffé and Henry, 1969) that one of the zones of most effective thermal denaturation in phage $\lambda$ DNA (after the $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { b } _ { 2 } }$ region)is that centered at about $1 3 . 7 ~ \mu$ ,starting from its left end (Inman, 1967). This zone is probably located between the end of the N cistron and the beginning of the O cistron (Hogness, 1966; Davis and Davidson, 1968). It is well known (Inman, 1967) that the most effective denaturation zones are also the richest in A-T, and this seems to be the case for the region of our choice (Hogness, 1966; Davis and Davidson, 1968). Conversely any zone starting from the CII gene toward the right is on the average relatively poor in A-T.

# CONCLUSIONS

The A-T rich regions are expected to yield more UV-induced dimers than other regions do. The present results show that the UVinduced relative increase in genetic recombination in A-T rich N-CII region is higher than in the G-C richer CII-P region of $\lambda$ phage DNA. We suggest, therefore, that the increase in recombination frequency caused by UV irradiation is higher in A-T rich regions than in others because of the higher yield of pyrimidine dimers.

Jacob and Wollman (1955) have shown that photoreactivation, which monomerizes pyrimidine dimers (Wulff and Rupert, 1962), leads to the decrease of the UV effect on recombination frequency. Excision repair of pyrimidine dimers leads to the same consequences (Baker and Haynes, 1967). On the basis of the present results we therefore suggest that pyrimidine dimers are recombination-promoting sites.

As for the question whether it is the presence of dimers or the presence of sites from which they have been removed that stimulates recombination, we suggest that the enhancement in recombination may be mostly promoted by the unpaired bases due to the presence of dimers (Marmur et al., 1961; Pearson and Johns, 1966) but perhaps also to their removal during the excision repair. It seems also reasonable to assume that more generally recombination takes place at those sites in which the original symmetry of the DNA base pairs has been perturbed, e.g., by thermal fluctuation processes.
