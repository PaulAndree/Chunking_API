Z. Toman, C. Dambly-Chaudière, L. Tenenbaum and M. Radman†

Département de Biologie Moléculaire Université libre de Bruxelles rue des Chevaux, 67   
B-1640 Rhode St Genèse, Belgium

(Received 4 January 1985, and in revised form 31 May 1985)

In order to compare the genetic and epigenetic effects of genotoxic agents, we have constructed Escherichia coli K12 strains that allow the detection of mutagenesis, SOS induction (epigenetic effect) and genetic recombination in the same genetic background. The epigenetic effect was detected in a similar way to any genetic alteration, i.e. by counting altered clones (colonies), using a gene fusion system that responds to a temporary epigenetic effect by a stable, heritable switch.

The gene fusion consists of the E. coli gal operon and a partially deleted prophage lambda, resulting in the gal operon coming under the control of the cI and cro genes. It allows the detection of SOS induction and forward mutagenesis in the cI gene. Even a temporary inactivation of the CI repressor in this particular system leads to a stable epigenetic switch transmitted to the cellular progeny, which can be detected as $\mathrm { G a l ^ { + } }$ (red) colonies. The genetic (mutational inactivation of gene cI) and epigenetic (proteolytic inactivation of the product of gene cI) mechanisms leading to gal expression can be distinguished.

Genetic recombination between two heteroallelic lacZ genes, one located in the bacterial chromosome, the other on an $\mathbf { F ^ { \prime } }$ lac plasmid, can be detected as $\mathrm { L a c } ^ { + }$ colonies.

Radiation and several chemical mutagens show very different capacities in generating mutants, inductants and recombinants; therefore, a dose range of any physical or chemical agent generates a set of relative values for the generation of mutants, inductants and recombinants that are characteristic of the agent.

# 1. Introduction

Genotoxic effects, defined as cell-inherited alterations, may originate from either genetic or epigenetic alterations. Genetic alterations are alterations in the nucleotide sequence, due to either point mutations or to rearrangements of the genomic nucleotide sequence. The term epigenetic alteration is used here to refer to changes in gene function or expression that are not caused by alterations in the nucleotide sequence. Such alterations can be stable and heritable (cell-to-cell inheritance), as in some cellular differentiation processes.

Genetic effects can be induced by different mechanisms, depending on the agent. Direct mutagenesis results from miscopying of miscoding DNA lesions (for a review, see Radman et al., 1977). Indirect mutagenesis results from an error-prone replication activity (Caillet-Fauquet et al., 1984) that is induced by the presence of non-coding DNA alterations (Radman, 1974; Caillet-Fauquet et al., 1977).

There is evidence from studies of Escherichia coli (for reviews, see Witkin, 1976; Walker, 1984), yeast (Lawrence & Christensen, 1982; Fabre & Roman, 1977) and mammalian cells (for a review, see Radman, 1980) that many of the genetic effects of DNA-damaging agents (i.e. indirect mutation and recombination) result from the activity of a cellular enzymatic machinery that is under an epigenetic control. The best-studied such system is the $\pmb { \cal E }$ . coli

SOS system. The key elements of the SOS control system are two proteins: LexA and RecA (for reviews, see Witkin, 1976; Walker, 1984). The LexA protein is a cellular repressor of over a dozen "SOS" genes, including the recA gene. The RecA protein is a DNA recombinase which, in the presence of damaged DNA, can also trigger a specific protease activity that cleaves the LexA repressor leading to the activation of several SOS functions.

A well-analysed epigenetic system is that of bacteriophage λ, which synthesizes two mutually exclusive repressors, the cI and cro gene products (Eisen et al., 1970). Bacterial strains carrying an appropriate λ-defective prophage can therefore exist in two stable states. In the immune state, the CI repressor is present and therefore the cro gene is shut off; in the non-immune state, the Cro repressor is present and therefore the cI gene is shut off. An immune bacterial strain can switch to the nonimmune state by inactivation of the cI gene (genetic change) or by cleavage of the CI repressor by the RecA-dependent protease (epigenetic change).

On the basis of this property of bacteriophage λ, we have developed an $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ .coli K12 system that provides a measure of the ability of DNA-damaging agents to induce genetic effects (mutations in the cI gene) or epigenetic effects (inactivation of the CI repressor). In order to visualize the immune and the non-immune bacteria, we have used a strain in which the bacterial gal genes are under the control of the CI repressor (Dambly-Chaudière et al., 1983; see Fig. 1). In this system, inactivation of the CI repressor results in a switch from $\mathbf { G a l ^ { - } }$ (immune) to $\mathrm { G } \dot { { \bf a } } \mathrm { l } ^ { + }$ (non-immune state: see Fig. 2).

In addition, we have developed in the same strain a system that allows detection of a third effect, homologous recombination. This strain contains two copies of the lacZ gene, each carrying a distinct mutation, which can produce Lac+ recombinants.

The strains can be used to detect and characterize genotoxic effects of individual mutagenic/carcinogenic agents and to search for a correlation between mutagenic, recombinogenic or SOS-inducing potency with the carcinogenic potency. We have tested a small number of chemicals and sources of radiation over a range of doses and found that the resulting response is characteristic of each agent. Preliminary accounts of this work have been reported at several symposia (Toman et al., 1980; Dambly-Chaudière et al., 1981).

# 4. Results and Discussion

# (a) System for detection of genetic and epigenetic effects

In $\boldsymbol { E }$ . coli ${  { I D I } ( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } ) }$ (MT1), both the induction of mutations in the cI gene (genetic change) and the induction of proteolytic cleavage of the CI protein (epigenetic change) can be scored (see Fig. 2). Fully red colonies arising from mutation can be distinguished from red colonies with white margins arising from the epigenetic switch. Indeed, the "epigenetic" $\mathrm { G a l ^ { + } }$ state is much less stable than the mutant $\mathrm { G a l ^ { + } }$ state (see the legend to Fig. 2). (White margins probably arise because the level of galactokinase is lower in epigenetic $\mathrm { G a l ^ { + } }$ bacteria than in CI mutant $\mathrm { G a l ^ { + } }$ bacteria, not shown: see the legend to Fig. 2.) However, this distinction is possible only when the ratio of red to white colonies is high. Indeed, when this ratio is low (i.e. when red colonies must be counted on plates with many white colonies), the two types of red colonies present the same morphology and the distinction between them needs an additional streaking. We therefore constructed the $1 \mathrm { D } 1 ( \mathrm { c I } ^ { + } ~ i n d ^ { - } )$ (MT5) strain, in which the $\mathrm { I n d ^ { - } }$ repressor is not susceptible to proteolytic cleavage by the recAdependent protease activity and which, consequently, does not undergo the epigenetic switch. SOS-independent mutations can be detected by the use of the $1 \mathrm { D } 1 ( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } ) r e c { A } ^ { - }$ (MT2) derivative, since the $\mathrm { R e c A } ^ { + }$ function is necessary for both the epigenetic switch and SOS-dependent mutagenesis. Thus, three bacterial strains, $1 \mathrm { D } 1 ( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } )$ (MT1), 1D $\ : \vert ( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } \ : i n d ^ { - } ) \ :$ (MT5) and 1I $) \mathbf { l } ( \mathbf { c } \mathbf { I } ^ { + } ) r e c A ^ { - }$ (MT2) can be used to distinguish mutational and epigenetic responses.

The efficiency of a compound or treatment in inducing the transition from the immune to the non-immune state is measured by the ratio of red $( \mathrm { G a l } ^ { + } )$ over total colonies. u.v. and gamma radiation induce the appearance of red $( \mathrm { G a l ^ { + } } )$ colonies about $1 0 ^ { 2 }$ to $1 0 ^ { 3 }$ times more efficiently in the MT1 $( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } )$ derivative (epigenetic effect and cI mutation) than in the MT5 $( \bar { { \mathbf { c } } } \bar { \mathbf { I } } ^ { \mp } i n d ^ { - } )$ derivative (cI mutation only) (Fig. 4(a)). In the MT2 $( r e c A ^ { - } )$ derivative, there is no induction by u.v. or gamma radiation of cI mutations (not shown). Therefore, u.v. and gamma radiation appear to be epigenetic inducers and SOS-dependent mutagens. The epigenetic response is two to three orders of magnitude higher than the mutagenic response. This is expected, since the "target" for the induction of the epigenetic effect is probably the entire chromosome, whereas mutations are measured in a single gene.

Induction of $\mathrm { G a l ^ { + } }$ colonies by mitomycin C is four orders of magnitude higher in MT1 $\bar { ( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } ) }$ than in MT5 $\{ c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } \mathrm { \Delta } i n d ^ { - } \}$ (Fig. 4(b)). In the MT2 recA strain, there is no induction of cI mutants by mitomycin C. Thus mitomycin C appears as a epigenetic inducer and a totally SOS-dependent mutagen. Its epigenetic effect is four orders of magnitude higher than its mutagenic effect; therefore, mitomycin C effects qualitatively resemble those of u.v.

In contrast, MNNG gives similar responses in

MT1 $( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } )$ and MT5 $\{ c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } : i n d ^ { - } \}$ derivatives. MNNG also induces mutants in the MT2 $( r e c A ^ { - } )$ strain (Fig. 4(c)). We conclude that MNNG induces the epigenetic effect about ten times less efficiently than the mutagenic effect, and that at least part of its mutagenic activity is SOS-independent.

EMS induces red $( \mathrm { G a l } ^ { + } )$ colonies in MT1 $( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } )$ somewhat more efficiently than in MT5 $( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } \ i n d ^ { - } )$ The percentage of red colonies resulting from epigenetic switches was determined for each tested dose by streaking the red colonies on MacConkeygal plates (about 30 colonies were streaked for each dose). At the dose giving the maximal effect $( 1 0 ^ { 2 } ~ \mathrm { m } \mathbf { u } )$ , about $3 0 \%$ of the red colonies arise from epigenetic switches. EMS mutagenesis appears essentially recA independent (Fig.' 4(d)). (The difference observed between mutagenic effects in MT1 $( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } )$ and MT5 $( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } i n d ^ { - } )$ strains is reproducible and not yet explained.) At high concentrations, EMS induces the epigenetic response with similar efficiency as the mutagenic response. Its mutagenic activity is largely SOS independent. Thus EMS shows qualitatively the same effects as MNNG. Note that the frequency of the epigenetic effect of the two alkylating agents is about three orders of magnitude lower than for radiation, whereas their mutagenic effect is one order of magnitude higher than for radiation. Whereas the mutagenesis is efficiently induced at non-toxic doses of EMS and MNNG, the epigenetic effect occurs only at toxic doses. A quantitative analysis of these responses will be published elsewhere.

# (b) The recombination system

Stimulation of homologous recombination between two heteroallelic copies of lacZ gene can be measured using the strain MT6, which is $1 \mathbf { D } \mathbf { I } ( c \mathbf { I } ^ { + } )$ lacZ813ochre $\mathbf { F ^ { \prime } }$ lacZM15. Figure 5 shows that mitomycin C, u.v. and gamma rays are detected as recombinogens. MNNG (and EMS, not shown) appear to be inactive in stimulating recombination in the $r e c ^ { + }$ strain. To determine if the failure of MNNG and EMS to induce recombination is due to the low sensitivity of the test system resulting from the high level $( 1 0 ^ { - 2 } )$ of spontaneously arising recombinants, a $r e c B ^ { - }$ derivative was constructed. The ratio of $\mathrm { L a c ^ { + } }$ to total colonies in the $r e c B ^ { - }$ derivative is $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ and the recombinogenic effect of MNNG can be detected readily (not shown).

As expected, u.v., gamma rays and mitomycin C are potent recombinogens in our system, whereas the recombinational effect of MNNG cannot be detected in the rec\* strain but can in a $r e c B ^ { - }$ derivative.

In summary, a set of $E$ .coli K12 strains was constructed that provides a convenient and sensitive system to detect and quantify cellular effects of DNA-damaging agents. In this system, the epigenetic phenomenon (repressor cleavage) as well as two different genetic phenomena (mutation and recombination) can be scored as endpoints of the action of different agents that cause damage to DNA.

This system may be used for the establishment of a genotoxicity profile for any genotoxic agent, which may facilitate the evaluation of its potential carcinogenic activity. A quantitative analysis of the potency of every agent to induce genetic (mutants, recombinants) and apigenetic (inductants) alterations among viable cells may facilitate comparisons of potencies for the generation of mutants, inductants and recombinants with the carcinogenic potency, as was attempted with the Salmonella Ames test (Meselson & Russel, 1977). A possible advantage of this system may be its capacity to detect thrcc different events (the generation of mutants, inductants and recombinants) that are induced by carcinogens and to assign pathways of these events. Furthermore, this test system with multiple endpoints is suitable for a quantitative analysis similar to that proposed by Haynes $\&$ Eckaerdt (1980), by which we measure the total capacity of any agent to produce stable alterations (genetic or epigenetic) in surviving cells. Following exposure of a cell population to DNA-damaging agents, there will be generally three groups of cells: unaffected, killed, and affected but alive. Only affected but alive cells are carriers and transmitters of genotoxic effects. Furthermore, expressing dose in dimensionless units (lethal hits) allows comparison of potencies for any kind of genotoxic treatment (e.g. radiation versus chemicals). In a forthcoming publication, we intend to correlate any of the three parameters (generation of mutants, inductants and recombinants), or any combination thereof, with the carcinogenic potency of radiations and chemicals determined in experimental and epidemiological studies.
