Key words: Mismatch repair, heteroduplex DNA, DNA methylation, strand discrimination.

In vivo mismatched base pairs in DNA arise from replication errors, from 5meC deamination and from recombination that result in the pairing of homologous but nonidentical sequences. An organism may respond to these mispairs and convert them to the normal W-C base pairs or may ignore them and produce nonidentical and mutated progeny, as well as modified transcripts. Since the bases are otherwise normal W-C DNA bases but noncomplementary matched, the cellular mismatch repair (MMR) machinery is faced with the problem how to recognize and specifically eliminate incorrect base. Both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are capable to overcome this problem and efficiently repair mismatched bases and protect genom from abundant mutations. The most of our knowledge about MMR comes from the work with Escherichia coli. In this simple organism at least three MMR systems have been identified. They differ in regard to the strand-discrimination signal and to the strand excision-repair tract size. The long-patch mismatch repair (LPMMR) system is generally known as methyl-directed MutS,L,H pathway. Mismatched bases are recognized by MutS and MutL proteins. After mismatch recognition MutH endonuclease specifically introduces a nick in the vicinity of dGATC sequence only if the adenine is not methylated, as it is the case in the newly synthesized DNA strand. Parental, "old" DNA is modified by the activity of Dam methylase, the product of dam gene, which methylates A exclusively in dGATC sequence. Methylation stays behind the replication about a half of the minute, transiently resulting in hemimethylated states of DNA strands susceptible to MutH dependent incision. Discontinuities provoked by MutH endonuclease in the new strand are substrate for MutU (helicase II) which catalyses strand displacement from dGATC sequence to the mismatched bases. The displaced strand is subsequently degraded by Exo VII/RecJ or Exo I. SSB protein, DNA pol III as well as DNA ligase necessary for resynthesis and for sealing this up to several kilobases long patchwork [14, 28, 34].

About $9 9 \%$ of the spontaneous replication errors are corrected via LPMMR. The same mechanism is very efficient as a control mechanism for recombination fidelity, preventing the recombination of homologous but nonidentical DNA strands [31, 36, 41]. In addition to LPMMR, two pathways for mismatc correction were identified in E. col The first one, very short patch mismatch repair (VSPMMR) corrects T in T:G mispaired bases that occurs in dCCA/TGG sequence. This sequence is a substrate for dcm-encoded methylase which methylates the internal $C '$ at the position 5 in the cytosine ring. 5-meC deaminates spontaneously to T, generating T:G mispair. In VSPMMR, strand-specific mismatch endonuclease is product of vsr gene, located very closely to dcm gene. It recognizes T:G mismatches that occur only in dCT (A/T)GG and dNT(A/T)GG sequences and makes incision $5 '$ of the T. DNA pol I removes the T by its exonucleolytic activity, and then carries out repair synthesis. This pathway is sequenceand mismatch-specific, and depends on MutS,L, Dcm, Vsr, and DNA pol I , with only a few nucleotides long patchwork.

The $E$ coli MMR does not recognize and repair all mismatches with equal efficiency. In general, transition mismatches G:T and C:A are repaired more efficiently than transversion mismatches G:A; C:T; A:A, G:G, T:T and C:C [10, 18]. One of transversion mismatches, G:A, could be repaired by MutY-dependent mismatch pathway. The MutY protein plays an important role as a DNA glycosylase. The G:A mismatches are repaired to G:C base pairs $( 7 0 \% )$ or A:T $( 3 0 \% )$ .This pathway is mismatch-specific, MutS,L,H -independent, DNA pol I-dependent and gives rise to repair patches in the range of 10 to 20 nucleotides [2, 33, 43].

There is a lot of convincing evidence that eukaryotes possess very potent MMR activity, and both types of MMR, longpatch and short-patch have been demonstrated in human cells. LPMMR has a large excision tract (90—170 nucleotides) but little or no sequence specificity [14]. The first step of MMR, the recognition of mismatched bases, has been highly conserved during evolution, and homologous of bacterial MutS and MutL have been identified in many eukaryotes from Saccharomyces cerevisiae to humans. Four genes have been characterized as human homologous of $E$ coli MutS, and three as homologous of MutL [30]. Inactivation of genes encoding these activities results in a large increase of spontaneous mutability and in the sporadic or hereditary predisposition to tumor development, like hereditary human nonpoliposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC) [12, 20].

Mammalian cells have been shown to have capacity to repair specifically G:T mismatch to G:C in a manner very similar to short-patch repair in prokaryotes. By this mechanism, a single T nucleotide in T:G mismatched pairs is removed by thymine-DNA glycosylase; the resulting AP site is subjected to endonucleolytic incision and gap is repaired by the $\mathrm { p o l } \beta$ [3, 46, 47]. It was shown that monkey kidney cells repair different mismatches with different efficiencies and repair bias was influenced by mismatch flanking sequences, in a manner similar to $E$ coli [8, 42]. In addition, it is reported that HeLa cells extracts possess the activity of the recognition and nicking A:G mispairs as in the $E$ coli ${ \mathrm { M u t Y ^ { + } } }$ -dependent pathway [30]. In spite of the homology in mismatch recognition step between prokaryotes and eukaryotes, an analog of the bacterial MutH endonuclease in strand-discrimination step, has not been identified yet in any eukaryotic cell, and chemical nature of this signal is far to be known.

By in vitro experiments it was shown that the MMR can be directed in many systems, such as $E$ coli, Xenopus laevis, Drosophila melangaster and human cells, by a nick or a gap in the vicinity of mismatched bases but the signal for mismatch carrying strand recognition is not yet found [27, 45].

Xenopus laevis egg extracts, earlier shown to possess very efficient MMR activity [6, 44, 45] was used in this work to test the possible role of the DNA methylation as the stranddiscrimination signal. The substrates for this investigations were covalently closed heteroduplexes with either T:G or C:A mismatched base pairs constructed in vitro from two M13 mutants. The first one was well known M13mp18 and the other, $\mathrm { M } 1 3 \mathrm { m p } 1 8 / \mathrm { M P } - 1$ was constructed for this purpose [32]. DNA strands of the heteroduplexes were unmethylated or in vitro methylated in CpG dinucleotide, to get the mismatched bases inside the unmethylated, hemimethylated or fullymethylated DNA strands. Heteroduplexes were treated with a Xenopus laevis egg extracts and the efficiency of MMR and the role of the strand methylation as a putative strand discrimination signal were tested.

Xenopus eggs are totipotent system with many advantages for in vitro experiments. They exhibit low levels of nucleolytic and nick translation activities. Furthermore, they possess recombination-promoting enzymes, ligase and double-strand break repair activities as well as single-stranded DNA replication activity. As the other germ cells, Xenopus eggs are able to perform at least 12 cell duplications following fertilization, without de novo transcription. Since embryogenesis is one of the most critical developmental stages demanding very precise duplication and maintaining of genetic information, it necessarily requests very active repair systems, including MMR.

Methylation/demethylation activity of Xenopus laevis egg extracts. Generally, eukaryotic DNA methyltransferases can catalyze two methylation reactions: (i) de novo methylation of unmethylated C in CpG dinucleotide, and (ii) maintenance methylation, in which the enzyme methylate a C in one strand, using a methylated C in the other strand as a recognition signal [9].

Recently, the activity of the DNA demethylases were found in some experimental systems, indicating that the active process of DNA methylation is a reversible biological signal which regulates gene and chromosome activity [35, 48].

The eukaryotic methylation pattern can be determined by the treatment with Hpall and Mspl endonucleases. The restriction enzymes HpaII and MspI both recognize the sequence dCCGG, but HpalI does not cut if either C is methylated, whereas MspI tolerate the methylation of internal C of the specific sequence but does not cut if the external C is methylated. These traits make Mspl refractory to the eukaryotic type of methylation [29]. In tandem, HpalI and MspI endonucleases make a conventional tool for the determination of the methylation status in eukaryotes. To check the methylation and/ or demethylation activity in our extracts, homoduplexes used in our experiments (unmethylated, hemimethylated and fullymethylated) were incubated with Xenopus egg extracts as it was done in MMR assay. After incubation with egg extracts, they were subjected to the cleavage with either Hpall or MspI endonucleases, and the number and lengths of the fragments were compared. No difference in the number and size of fragments between treated and untreated homoduplex DNAs were found in our experiments (data not shown). Thus our results indicate that unfertilized Xenopus egg extract does not change methylation pattern of unmethylated, hemimethylated and fullymethylated DNA; it carries neither methylation nor demethylation activities. It was necessary to make this control experiments because it is known that once established in the genome of soinatic cells, CpG methylation pattern is very stable and heritable for all the cells of that tissue [13, 38, 40]. In contrast, DNA methylation is very dynamic during early development as well as in certain tumor cells [16], but for the unfertilized Xenopus eggs these data were not known, so far.

It is generally assumed that the DNA methylation generegulatory sequences (like promotors or $5 \cdot$ sequences of the genes) strongly correlates with gene activity - it can inhibit transcription initiation and arrest transcript elongation. Also, it can act as an imprinting signal for chromosomal inactivation and as a suppressor of homologous recombination [11, 17, 19, 22, 23]. DNA methylation has been conserved during evolution probably because it provides unique possibilities for setting up functions of the various type [9]. Some authors [15] proposed the role of the methylation as strand discrimination signal for MMR in certain experimental system, in the manner very similar to that of $E$ coli methyl-directed repair. The absence of the new data in other experimental systems led us to test the role of DNA methylation as a discrimination signal in MMR in Xenopus oocites. The fact that Xenopus egg extract is not able to replicate covalently closed doublestranded DNA [25] offered the opportunity to examine MMR with no interference with heteroduplex DNA replication.

Methylation is detected in all four groups of eukaryotes; protists, plants, fungi and animals with some exceptions like Drosophila melanogaster, yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and nematode Caenorahabditis elegans. Widespread distribution of DNA methylation among eukaryotes indicates that methylation could be an ancestral DNA modification which promotes in or interferes with a variety of cellular functions: gene expression, chromosomal structure, genomic imprinting, homologous recombination, and at least in some cases, it acts as a strand-discrimination repair signal [11, 15, 17, 19, 22, 23]. The best documented is the strong correlation between the methylation and gene silencing; methylation can interfere at the level of transcriptional initiation or elongation.

The consideration that methylation has a suppressing effect on somatic recombination is based on the facts that many cancer cells harbor chromosomal rearrangements that could be explained by the homologous recombination between repeated sequences, and at the same time cancer cells are very often hypomethylated [4, 21]. Even nondividing tissue like human brain has very potent mismatch repair and methylation activities [7]. In such a tissue, errors like those caused by deamination of 5meC should be repaired very efficiently because the neurons are among the longest living cells and must remain functionally intact for decades in human being.

The pleotropic role of DNA methylation in DNA metabolism and structure implicates the necessity for a faithful maintaining of methylation pattern. On the other hand, chemical instability of methylated C and its deamination to yield T can cause not only mutational event in the genom but also the change in methylation profile. Despite the existence of a G:T to G:C mismatch repair system, it is estimated that $3 5 \%$ of point mutations causing human genetic disorders have occurred at CpG dinucleotides, and over $9 0 \%$ of them were transitions from C to $\mathrm { T }$ or corresponding G to A [37].

We postulate that the same chemical marker as methylation, with so many effects on the DNA activity and structure, can be additionally used as a signal for strand discrimination in the repair of mismatched bases.

The results obtained with the model system used in our experiments indicate that the preferential repair of T:G in hemimethylated DNA presumably belongs to the repair activity of T:G - specific glycosidase [3, 46, 47]. Restoration of C:G base pair from either T:G or C:A in spite of strand-methylation, may indicate the preferential mechanism to save C:G base pair in the context of CpG sequence. The activity that we observed may involve more different mechanisms for MMR which do not entail any particular strand selectivity.

We propose that nick shown to conduct repair direction in many prokaryotes and eukaryotes, is not methyl-directed in Xenopus eggs. It seams likely that signal for endonucleolytic incision could be created in many ways and most of them still remain to be discovered.
