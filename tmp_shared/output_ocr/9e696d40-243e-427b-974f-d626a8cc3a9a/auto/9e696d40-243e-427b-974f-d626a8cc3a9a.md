Protein-protein interactions (PPIs) play crucial roles in cellular signaling, transmitting signals from the cell surface to its interior. One of the most important signaling cascades is the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathway. This pathway is initiated by various upstream signaling reactions, including receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) activation, and it controls many biological functions like cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. Once RAS is activated, it binds RAF and relays the signal to downstream proteins. The RAS-binding domain (RBD) in RAF protein plays a crucial role in this process, facilitating the RAS-ERK pathway signaling.

In this study, we explored the effect of oxidative stress induced by UV radiation on the KRAS-RBD interaction. Using the Split Intein-Mediated Protein Ligation (SIMPL) method, we assessed the impact of different UV doses on KRAS-RBD interactions and observed a disruption of this interaction at higher doses. UV-treated samples exhibited high levels of protein carbonylation, as detected by Oxime Blot and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, indicating oxidative damage. The MS results provided detailed insights into specific carbonylation modifications on the KRAS protein.

Our study demonstrates that protein oxidation and carbonylation can disrupt protein-protein interactions, specifically the KRAS/c-RAF interaction. These findings highlight the impact of oxidative stress on signaling pathways, such as those triggered by UV irradiation. A deeper understanding of these molecular changes may aid in developing therapies targeting diseases linked to oxidative stress, including cancer.

# 1. Introduction

Cell communication is strongly dependent on protein-protein interactions (PPIs) [1–3]. They are essential in regulating signaling like the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK pathway, which controls cellular processes such as growth, differentiation, and apoptosis [4]. The key step to activating this pathway involves the binding of RAF (RAF1, BRAF or CRAF) to active RAS through its RAS-binding domain (RBD) [5,6]. The RBD has a high affinity for active RAS, the GTP-bound form [7]. On the other hand, upon binding to KRAS, RAF undergoes activation through a complex and yet not fully characterized process, and subsequently phosphorylates and activates MEK1/2, which further phosphorylates and activates ERK1/2. Activated ERK can be translocated into the nucleus and shows a broad spectrum of substrate specificity including kinases, transcription factors, etc. Their phosphorylation is critical in their activities and functions such as gene expression changes upon primary activation [6].

The KRAS gene is frequently mutated in cancer which results in sustained activation of this pathway and thus operates as oncogenic driver [6,8]. Targeting the RAS signaling pathway, especially its involvement in RAF activation is crucial for cancer therapeutics although direct targeting RAS proteins has been proved difficult [9–11]. However, recent research on the structural and functional aspects of RAF activation has revealed potential new therapeutic targets, suggesting that a combination of inhibitors targeting different points within the RAS signaling network may be necessary for effective treatment [12].

Proteins are major targets of oxidative stress which can be induced inside and outside cells. As basic components of most biological systems, proteins can be damaged up to $5 0 \mathrm { - } 7 5 ~ \%$ by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [13]. UV radiation is one mechanism for the production of ROS and it has been shown to induce protein oxidation in the form of carbonylation [14,15]. UV-induced oxidative damage is complex, involving both direct and indirect pathways. In the indirect pathway, ROS doesn’t interact directly with all amino acids but instead starts a series of reactions that lead to protein damage. Reactive species like hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions can oxidize amino acids and lead to the formation of protein carbonylation. ROS can also trigger lipid peroxidation, creating reactive aldehydes that further damage proteins and impair their function.

One major consequence of ROS-induced protein damage is the formation of protein carbonyls. This modification happens when ROS interacts with the side chains of certain amino acids—particularly lysine (Lys), arginine (Arg), proline (Pro), and threonine (Thr). Carbonylation is a non-enzymatic, and irreversible protein modification that can be introduced by many oxidative pathways [16–18]. The level of carbonylated proteins increases with age, leading to the degradation of proteins as well as changing their structure and disease-related protein dysfunction [19–21]. Thus, it would be interesting to examine how oxidation affects one of the major components in the RAS signaling pathway known as KRAS-RBD interaction; such information may also provide some insight into the involvement between KRAS mutation and cancer development/progression.

In this study, we investigated for the first time how UV-induced oxidation affects KRAS-RBD interaction. We use a multidisciplinary approach, where interaction was examined using SIMPL assay [22], the Oxime blot assay [23] for the detection of carbonylation, immunoprecipitation to further specify oxidation of KRAS and RBD, and mass spectrometry (MS) to identify post-translational modifications. Furthermore, molecular dynamic simulations were used to test how individual oxidation modifications alter the KRAS-RBD interaction.

# 2. Results

# 2.1. UV-induced oxidation significantly disrupts the KRAS-RBD interaction

To investigate the impact of oxidation on the KRAS-RBD protein interaction, we used the Split Intein-Mediated Protein Ligation (SIMPL) method [22,24]. This approach involves fusing bait and prey proteins respectively with intein N-terminal (IN) and C-terminal (IC) fragments from a re-engineered split intein GP41-1. Upon bait-prey binding, the intein is reconstituted, splicing the bait and prey peptides into a single intact protein, which serves as PPI readout detected by standard methods like Western blot. In our design, KRAS is fused at its N-terminus with a FLAG tag and an IC fragment, and the RBD protein is fused at its C-terminus to a V5 tag and an IN fragment (Fig. 1A). HEK293 cells were transfected with vectors expressing either KRAS or RBD, after which cell lysates were subjected to UV radiation at doses of 0.3 and $0 . 6 ~ \mathrm { J / c m ^ { 2 } }$ to induce oxidation. Following exposure to UV radiation, KRAS and RBD lysates were mixed, incubated at $3 0 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ , and then subjected to Western blot analysis as detailed in Fig. 1B. We found a notable disruption in the KRAS-RBD complex formation after the RBD component was subjected to oxidation induced by UV radiation (Fig. 1C). A more pronounced effect was observed when both RBD and KRAS were exposed to a UV dose of $0 . 3 \ : \mathrm { J / c m ^ { 2 } }$ . Following exposure to $0 . 6 \mathrm { J } / \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ UV radiation, there was a further increase in the change observed, particularly shown in Fig. 1C. Moreover, the interaction between KRAS and RBD significantly decreased when both RBD-expressing and KRAS-expressing cell lysates were irradiated with $0 . 6 ~ \mathrm { \ J / c m ^ { 2 } }$ UV radiation.

# 2.2. Protein carbonylation detection and characterization of the KRAS protein

We then investigated the molecular damages of KRAS protein induced by oxidation. At first, total protein carbonylation was analyzed in cell lysates as an indicator of oxidative damage, performed using the Oxime blot method as previously described [23]. Samples were derivatized with aminooxy-biotin and western blotting with streptavidin-HRP detection (Fig. 2A). Briefly, the carbonyl groups react with the biotin-aminooxy probe to form a chemically stable oxime bond, enabling the detection through streptavidin-HRP binding. Higher total carbonylation levels in samples exposed to UV radiation than in the control samples were observed in both RBD and KRAS-expressing cell lysates (Fig. 2B). We next analyzed carbonylation levels of immunoprecipitated KRAS to examine the specific oxidation of KRAS proteins. Our findings revealed a 1.5 fold increase in carbonylation compared to the untreated samples (Fig. 2B).

We focused on examining the consequences of oxidative damage in terms of protein carbonylation, where Lys, Arg, Pro, and Thr are the primary targets of carbonylation due to their susceptibility to oxidative modifications. Using mass spectrometry, we further explored specific modifications associated with protein carbonylation, including arginine to glutamic semialdehyde, lysine to aminoadipic semialdehyde (allysine), methionine to aspartic semialdehyde, and carbonylation of alanine, isoleucine, leucine, valine, and proline residues (Supplementary Table 1). We analyzed six samples, which are triplicates for both control and UV-treated conditions. 2321 proteins were identified, yielding 44,404 peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) overall. KRAS (Uniprot: P01116) was the highest-scoring protein, with 2370 PSMs attributed to it, achieving $8 5 \%$ sequence coverage. Of these PSMs, 1343 belonged to the control samples, while 1027 were from the UV-treated samples. We observed increased carbonylation of specific amino acids in the UV-treated samples, including a higher incidence of Pro to PyroGlu, Arg to GluSA, Met to AspSA, and Lys to Allysine modifications (Fig. 3A). The total number of carbonylation events detected was 72 in the UV-treated samples compared to 33 in the controls. Normalizing these values to account for the difference in PSM counts between the irradiated and control samples, we found an estimated carbonylation ratio of approximately 2.9, indicating a substantial increase in oxidative modifications due to UV exposure. All modification sites in the KRAS gene are presented in Fig. 3B. These analyses provide insights into the molecular alterations resulting from UV-induced oxidation of KRAS and RBD proteins.

# 2.3. Molecular modeling of KRAS and RBD interactions

We evaluated how UV-induced carbonylation on individual residues impacts KRAS/RBD interaction using molecular modeling. We specifically focused on the K42, I46, and R149 residues because these amino acids play key roles in the structural integrity of KRAS and their interaction with the Ras-binding domain. Lysine (K42) and arginine (R149) are both polar and positively charged residues, making them important for forming electrostatic interactions with the RBD and stabilizing the KRAS-RBD binding. Additionally, we chose Isoleucine (I46) to test whether modifications of hydrophobic residues in the core region would impact the KRAS-RBD interaction. Results are summarized in Table 1, which presents the loss of interactions numerically, while Fig. 4 graphically presents these interactions. One can see that oxidative modifications of K42, I46, and R149 (at KRAS) caused a loss of interactions between KRAS and RBD. In total, there are 7 fewer atomic contacts between KRAS and RBD. These include the loss of 3 hydrogen bonds (K42 and R149), 3 polar contacts (K42 and R149), and one nonbonding interaction (R149). This result shows that oxidation changed the side chain properties of the amino acids in question (e.g., K42 and R149 have positively charged $\mathrm { N H 3 + }$ groups in the side chain, which is replaced by negatively charged O-). As far as the I46 residue, oxidation induces a carbonyl group in the side chain, which can happen at CD1, CG1 or CG2 carbon atoms. To understand the difference between different carbonylation sites, we modeled the carbonyl group at all of them. Investigation of the overlap of all interactions shows that different carbonylation sites did not influence contacts of I46 with RBD as these consist of nonbonding interaction, which was conserved upon carbonylation. These results suggest that polar, charged residues, such as arginine and lysine, are more sensitive to the effects of carbonylation.

# 3. Conclusions

Oxidative damage to proteins is known to cause dysfunction, loss of function, and the acquisition of toxic (cell-killing) functions [25]. Using the RAS and RAF protein interaction as a model system, we demonstrate that oxidative damage, as anticipated, disrupts protein-protein interactions. Since irreversible protein oxidation (carbonylation) correlates with lifespan across all tested animal species and with the death of cultured cells (from bacterial to human), it has been proposed as a cause of aging and age-related diseases. We posit that the disturbance of protein-protein interactions by protein damage likely contributes to increasing functional noise in aging cells.

In light of these findings, it’s essential to recognize the broader implications for critical cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and survival [5]. Among these pathways, the KRAS protein plays a pivotal role, particularly in cancer, where mutations in KRAS are found in approximately $2 0 \%$ of all cases, often driving tumor development [9, 10]. Understanding the interactions of KRAS with its protein partners is essential for developing effective cancer therapies, especially considering the "undruggable" nature of the KRAS protein [26].

In this study, we investigated the effects of oxidative stress on the KRAS-RBD interaction, focusing on the susceptibility of specific amino acids in KRAS to oxidative damage. Using UV radiation as an oxidative stressor and the SIMPL method for detecting PPIs, we demonstrated that oxidative damage leads to a dose-dependent disruption of the KRASRBD protein complex. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis revealed a 2.9- fold increase in carbonylation in UV-treated KRAS samples, identifying specific modifications such as the conversion of lysine to aminoadipic semialdehyde, arginine to glutamic semialdehyde, and carbonylation of other residues including alanine, isoleucine, leucine, valine, and proline. We hypothesize that the cumulative effect of these carbonylation events is responsible for the observed loss of the KRASRBD interaction.

This is the first report demonstrating that protein oxidation and carbonylation can negatively impact protein-protein interactions, as evidenced by the disruption of the KRAS/c-RAF interaction. Additionally, our molecular modeling studies indicate that oxidative modifications at key KRAS residues—K42 and R149—disrupt critical hydrogen bonds, polar contacts, and nonbonding interactions with the RBD protein. Previous research has shown that even a single amino acid mutation in Raf-1 (Arg89 to Leu) can impair its interaction with Ras [27]. Moreover, specific mutations at the KRAS/RBD-CRD interface, where KRAS engages with the RAS-binding domain and the membrane-interacting cysteine-rich domain of RAF1, have been associated with a significant reduction in RAF1 activation [28]. Consistent with these findings, our study suggests that oxidative modifications at residues K42 and R149 in KRAS lead to the loss of essential interactions with the RBD protein, further emphasizing the impact of oxidative stress on protein-protein interactions.

Our findings highlight the significance of studying UV irradiation as an environmental stressor that may impair vital signaling pathways. This work not only expands our understanding of the molecular consequences of oxidative stress on proteins but also opens avenues for further research aimed at identifying therapeutic targets to mitigate these effects and preserve cellular integrity. Such insights could be instrumental in developing new strategies to counteract the detrimental impact of oxidative stress in diseases like cancer.

# 4. Methods

# 4.1. Cell transfection

HEK293FT cells were seeded into 6-well plates (500,000 cells/well) and grown in DMEM (Capricorn, $\#$ DMEM-HXA) containing $1 0 ~ \%$ FBS (Capricorn, #FBS-HI-11A) and $1 \ \%$ Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Scientific, $\# 1 5 0 7 0 0 6 3 )$ at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with $5 \%$ $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 }$ until a confluency of ${ \sim } 6 0$ $\%$ was reached. The cells were then transfected with $1 ~ \mu \ g$ of plasmid using polyethyleneimine (PEI) (Sigma, $\# 4 0 8 7 2 7 )$ and incubated for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were lysed using cell lysis buffer $2 0 \mathrm { m M }$ Tris, $1 5 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ NaCl, $2 \ \mathrm { m M }$ EDTA, $0 . 5 ~ \%$ Triton X-100) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Thermo Scientific, #1861279) and placed in a sonication ice bath for $1 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , and then centrifuged for $1 0 \ \mathrm { \ m i n }$ at $1 6 , 0 0 0 \times g$ at $4 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . Samples were quantified using Bradford Reagent (Sigma, #B6916) and adjusted to the same protein concentration using lysis buffer.

# 4.2. Oxidation by UV irradiation

A portion of the cell lysated was treated with different doses of UV using an irradiation chamber BS-02 (Opsytec Dr. Grobel GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany) with a 1:2 ratio of UVC $( 2 5 4 ~ \mathrm { n m } )$ ) and UVB (280–315 nm). During irradiation, the samples were exposed to $\mathrm { O } _ { 2 }$ and kept on ice in a 96-well plate without a cover, while the other part which served as a control, was just incubated on ice.

# 4.3. Split-intein Mediated Protein Ligation (SIMPL) assay

Equal amounts of UV-irradiated and control samples were mixed and incubated on a thermo mixer at $8 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ , $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . Laemmli buffer $4 0 \%$ glycerol, $2 5 0 \mathrm { m M }$ Tris, 280 mM SDS, $5 \%$ 2-mercaptoethanol, $0 . 5 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ Brilliant Blue) was added to the samples, and they were incubated for $5 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $9 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . The samples were then resolved by SDSPAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Cytiva, $\# 1 0 6 0 0 0 1 6$ ) using the Trans-Blot® Turbo™ Transfer system (Bio-Rad) with homemade buffers as described by Jim´enez-Soto1 . The membranes were blocked in $2 \%$ BSA (Roth, $\# 2 8 3 4 . 3 )$ for $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at room temperature on a rocking platform, then incubated overnight at $4 ~ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ on the rocking platform in primary antibodies: rabbit anti-V5 (1:5000) (Cell Signaling, $\# 1 3 2 0 2 )$ for RBD samples and anti-FLAG (1:10,000) (Sigma, #F7425) for FLAG samples. The next morning, the membranes were washed three times for $1 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ on the rocking platform with PBS-T (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM $\mathrm { N a _ { 2 } H P O _ { 4 } }$ , $0 . 0 5 ~ \%$ Tween20), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #111-035-144) for $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ at room temperature on the rocking platform. The washing step was repeated, as in the previous step, and the membranes were incubated with ECL substrate (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #SC-2048) and imaged using X-ray film (Fuji, #RX1824) on a Fuji Medical Film Processor FPM-100A (Fuji Photo FILM Co., Japan).

# 4.4. Carbonyl labeling

An aliquot of irradiated samples and control was set aside for carbonyl labelling after UV irradiation. We used the Oxime blot method with minor changes to detect carbonylation [23]. A stock solution of $1 0 0 \ \mathrm { m M }$ Alkoxyamine-PEG4-Biotin (Thermo Scientific, $\# 2 1 3 6 2$ ) was dissolved in DMSO. Derivatization with the biotin-aminooxy probe was done by incubating samples with $1 0 \mathrm { m M }$ of the probe (diluted in PBS) in the presence of $2 0 ~ \mathrm { m M }$ catalyst $p$ -phenylenediamine (Sigma, $\# \mathrm { P } 6 0 0 1 $ ), prepared fresh. Samples were incubated overnight on a thermo mixer at ${ } ^ { 2 5 } \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at $6 0 0 ~ \mathrm { r p m }$ . Next, Laemmli buffer was added to the samples, and they were heated to $9 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $5 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ . The samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were incubated with $2 \%$ BSA overnight at $4 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ on the rocking platform. The next morning, the membranes were washed three times for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ on the rocking platform with PBS-T and incubated for $^ { 2 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at room temperature on the rocking platform with streptavidin-HRP (1:10,000) (Thermo Scientific, $\# 8 9 1 1$ ). Then the washing step was repeated, as in the previous step, and the membranes were incubated with ECL substrate and imaged using X-ray film.

# 4.5. Immunoprecipitation experiment

Thirty-six $_ { 1 5 \mathrm { c m } }$ dishes were seeded with 10 million HEK293FT cells. The next day, half of the dishes were transfected with FLAG-KRAS. Each dish was transfected with $2 5 \mu \ g$ of DNA and $5 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ Lipofectamine $^ \mathrm { \textregistered }$ 2000 Reagent (ThermoFisher, #11668019) already mixed in OptiMem (ThermoFisher, #31985062), and cells were incubated for $4 8 \mathrm { h }$ at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ with $5 \ \% \mathrm { C O _ { 2 } }$ . Transfected cells were washed twice with PBS and scraped in lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors; duplicate plates were combined and sonicated for 1 min in an ice-cold sonication bath. Lysates were centrifuged at $1 6 , 0 0 0 \times g$ for $1 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at $4 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and quantified using Bradford Reagent. Equivalent amounts of protein $_ { \sim 9 }$ mg) were immunoprecipitated using ANTI-FLAG M2 Affinity Gel (Sigma, $\# \mathsf { A 2 2 2 0 }$ ) and Anti-V5 Agarose Affinity Gel (Sigma, #A7345) following their protocol. Bound proteins were eluted with $0 . 1 ~ \mathrm { ~ M ~ }$ Glycine, $\mathrm { p H } 3 . 5$ , and neutralized using 1 M Tris, $\mathtt { p H } 8$ .

If samples were used for MS analysis, they were frozen and stored at $- 8 0 ~ { } ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ at this point. For further analysis, a portion of the samples was derivatized with the biotin-aminooxy probe following the described protocol for carbonyl labeling, and another portion of the samples was boiled with Laemmli buffer, and the described protocol for Western blotting was followed.

# 5. Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R, where one-way ANOVA was followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction of p-value.

# 5.1. Mass spectrometry experiment

Diluted samples after immunoprecipitation were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at a final concentration of $2 0 \%$ (w/v) on ice for at least $^ { 2 \mathrm { h } }$ . Samples were spun for $3 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ at $^ { 1 6 , 0 0 0 \times g , }$ $4 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } .$ . Pellets were washed 3 times with $8 0 ~ \%$ ethanol, dried under vacuum, and dissolved in denaturing buffer (6 M urea, $2 \textbf { M }$ thiourea in $1 0 \ \mathrm { m M }$ Tris buffer, $\mathrm { p H } 8 . 0 \AA$ ). Denatured samples were reduced with $5 \mathrm { m M }$ DTT for $^ { 1 \mathrm { h } }$ at ${ 2 5 ^ { \circ } } \mathrm { C } ,$ followed by alkylation with $1 6 . 5 \mathrm { m M }$ iodoacetamide for $^ { 1 \mathrm { ~ h ~ } }$ at $2 5 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ in the dark. Samples were diluted with 4 vol of $2 0 \mathrm { m M }$ ammonium bicarbonate and digested with trypsin overnight. The digestion was stopped by adding $1 \ \%$ trifluoroacetic acid, and digested peptides were desalted by solid-phase extraction on Stage Tips.

The peptides in $0 . 2 \%$ formic acid and 0.2 acetonitrile were loaded on an EASY-nLC™ 1200 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a pre-column (i.d. $7 5 \mu \mathrm { m } \times 2 \mathrm { c m } , 3 \mu$ $3 \mu \mathrm { m }$ particles) and an analytical column (EASY-Spray™ column, i.d. $5 0 \mu \mathrm { m } \times 1 5 \mathrm { c m }$ , $2 \mu \mathrm { m }$ particles) packed with C18 stationary phase for reverse-phase chromatography. The peptides were eluted at a flow rate of $2 0 0 ~ \mathrm { { n L / m i n } }$ using a $6 0 { \cdot } \mathrm { { m i n } }$ gradient of acetonitrile in $0 . 1 ~ \%$ formic acid. The nanoLC system was coupled to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated in positive ion mode with data-dependent acquisition. Full ion scan (MS1) was recorded at a resolution of 70,000, followed by ion fragmentation of the 12 most intense parent ions and MS/MS acquisition at a resolution of 17,500 (Top12) with dynamic exclusion for $_ { 1 5 s }$ .

Raw data was processed with Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Sequest HT search engine. Raw data was searched against human protein sequences, retrieved from UniProt, Release 2023_05. The following changes were defined as variable modifications during peptide search: Met oxidation/ $+ 1 5 . 9 9 5$ Da (M), Arg- $> \mathrm { G l u S A } / \mathrm { - } 4 3 . 0 5 3 \mathrm { I }$ a (R), Lys- $>$ Allysine/− 1.032 Da (K), Pro- $>$ pyro-Glu/ $+ 1 3 . 9 7 9$ Da (P), Met- $>$ AspSA/− 32.008 Da (M), Carbonyl/ $+ 1 3 . 9 7 9$ Da (A, I, L, R, V), and Cys alkylation (Carbamidomethylation)/ $+ 5 7 . 0 2 1 $ Da as fixed modification.

# 5.2. Molecular modeling

The initial structure of the KRAS (residues 1–169) in complex with RAF1(RBDCRD) (residues 56–185) crystal form I (PDB: 6XI7, resolution of $1 . 9 5 \mathrm { ~ \AA } )$ [28] was used for analyses. UV-induced oxidation modifications were introduced to the structure of KRAS using Chimera [30] and its Structure Editing tool. Focusing on the oxidized amino acid residues in Table S1, we explored which residues are at the interface with RBD and can influence their interactions. We identified residues: K42, I46, and R149. These residues were altered (lysine to aminoadipic semialdehyde (allysine), carbonylated isoleucine, arginine to glutamic semialdehyde) and their interactions with RBD were analyzed using the Arpeggio web server [31]. Arpeggio web server can be used for calculating interactions within and between proteins and protein, DNA, or small-molecules. These interactions include 15 different classes based on atom type, distance, and angle parameters (such as van der Waals’, ionic, carbonyl, metal, hydrophobic, and halogen bond contacts, and hydrogen bonds and specific atom–aromatic ring and aromatic ring–aromatic ring interactions). We specifically investigated interactions of side chains as backbone atoms remained unchanged.
