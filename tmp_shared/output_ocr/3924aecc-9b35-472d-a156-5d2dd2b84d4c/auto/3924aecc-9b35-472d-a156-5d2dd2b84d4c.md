# Replicational Fidelity: Mechanisms of Mutation Avoidance and Mutation Fixation

M. RADMAN, G. VILLANI, S. BOITEUX, A. R. KiNSELLA, B. W. GLICKMAN,\* AND S. SPADARI† oovibBe B-6RotGe Labo GThevo. Pavia, Italy

Most spontaneous and induced mutations occur as mistakes during DNA synthesis. The low error rate (roughly $1 0 ^ { - 9 } ,$ nucleotide replication in Escherichia coli: for review, see Cox [1976]) is achieved by the cellular enzymatic machinery. For example, prokaryotic DNA polymerases contribute to the fidelity of DNA synthesis through an active base selection and an associated $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease, called proofreading or editing activity, as demonstrated by genetical and biochemical studies of mutator and antimutator mutants affecting DNA polymerase of bacteriophage T4 (Gillin and Nossal 1976; Ko-Yu and Bessman 1976). Mutations in T4 gene 32, coding for a DNA-binding protein, can also affect spontaneous mutation rates by an unknown mechanism (Drake 1973). Of the ten loci which are known mutators in E. coli, two of them (weak) have been shown to code for DNA polymerases I and III; for the other eight loci, which include powerful mutators, neither the gene product nor the mechanism of their action is known $( \mathbf { C o x } \ 1 9 7 6 )$ .

A major source of spontaneous base-pair changes during DNA synthesis is the mispairing base modifications which, due to their good Watson-Crick base pairing, leak through the replication machinery (for review, see Drake and Baltz 1976). Tautomers and isomers of normal DNA bases are thought to be the predominant source of mispairings in spontaneous mutagenesis (Watson and Crick 1953; Topal and Fresco 1976). Other subtle base modifications, such as base analogs (e.g., 2-aminopurine [2AP] and 5-bromouracil [5BrUra]), bisulfite- and hydroxylamine-modified bases, and oxygen-alkylated bases, probably belong to the same class of mutagens, which have been called direct mutagens (Radman et al. 1977). Direct mutagens are relatively weak mutagens and are highly base-specific (Coulondre and Miller 1977).

On the other hand, mutagens which cause nonpairing, usually bulky modifications of DNA, also cause the arrest of DNA synthesis and act as mutagens through an inducible cellular mechanism which is part of a pleiotropic response to such DNA damage, called SOS induction (Defais et al. 1971; Radman 1974; for review, see Witkin 1976). These mutagens, exemplified by UV and ionizing radiations and by many powerful mutagenic carcinogens (e.g., mitomycin C, aflatoxin $\scriptstyle \mathbf { B } _ { 1 }$ , benzo[a]pyrene, etc.), have been called indirect mutagens, because DNA lesions provoked by them are not only the most probable sites of induced mutations, but more importantly, such lesions trigger SOS induction. SOS induction involves activation of a cellular mutator activity in the absence of which there is little or no mutagenesis by nonpairing DNA lesions (for review, see Witkin 1976). This inducible mutator activity causes mutagenesis of both damaged and undamaged DNAs, hence a high potency and a low base specificity of indirect mutagens such as UV irradiation (Coulondre and Miller 1977).

The basic mechanism of UV-induced mutagenesis is best illustrated by the fact that UV-irradiated DNA can act as a mutagenic agent in trans, e.g., when undamaged phage is mutated following infection of an irradiated host cell (Bleichrodt and Verheij 1974; Ichikawa-Ryo and Kondo 1975) or when the transfer of an irradiated episome triggers mutagenesis in an unirradiated recipient cell (George et al. 1974). These effects are not due to the transfer of UV-induced lesions from irradiated to unirradiated DNA, but to the induction of cellular functions common to the induction pathway of prophage $\lambda$ and requiring recA and lexA genes in $E _ {  }$ coli (Radman 1974). A simplified conclusion from the reviewed work is as follows. Indirect mutagens, causing only nonpairing DNA lesions, trigger SOS induction in bacteria and require SOS induction for mutation fixation; direct mutagens, causing only mispairing base modifications, neither require nor induce the SOS system.

In this paper, we discuss several hypotheses about the molecular basis of spontaneous and induced mutagenesis, both in bacteria and in mammalian cells. We also speculate about some general biological implications of our hypotheses. A new approach to the study of replicational fidelity will be described which implicates a postreplicative DNA methylationinstructed repair of mismatched base pairs in the DNA and which may account for the nature of several of the known $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli mutator loci. Furthermore, indirect arguments from in vitro studies will be presented for the involvement of a control of the $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ proofreading exonuclease activity in determination of induced mutation rates both in $E _ {  }$ coli and in mammalian cells.

# RESULTS

# Evidence for the Existence of a Postreplicative Methylation-instructed Excision Repair of Mismatched Bases

Genetic experiments suggested that pneumococcus $h e x ^ { - }$ mutants and $E$ coli $u v r E ^ { - }$ mutants may be deficient in the repair of some mismatched base pairs in the DNA (Tiraby and Fox 1973; Nevers and Spatz 1975); both mutants are also spontaneous mutators. $E _ {  }$ coli dam" mutants are deficient in the generalized adenine methylation of $E _ {  }$ coli DNA (Marinus and Morris 1973) which occurs in the 5'GATC3' sequence (Lacks and Greenberg 1977); dam" mutants are also spontaneous mutators (Marinus and Morris 1975). DNA methylation is a postreplicative process; newly synthesized DNA chains are not methylated. Therefore, Wagner and Meselson (1976) have suggested that DNA methylation is one possible means to discriminate between the parental (template) and the newly synthesized DNA chains. Should a DNA-correction system exist that is devised to eliminate replicational errors (residing in the new, hence undermethylated, DNA chains) by the excision of mismatched bases, methylation might provide for strand discrimination.

M. Radman, R. E. Wagner, and M. Meselson (unpubl., but see Nature 268: 489490 [1977]) have obtained the first experimental evidence in support of the hypothesis of methylation-instructed mismatch correction using $E _ { \cdot }$ coli transfection assays with phage λ heteroduplex DNA with strands differing in the degree of methylation and carrying different genetic markers. Improved experiments of this type were described by Dr. M. Meselson during this symposium and will be published elsewhere by M. Rykowski et al. (in prep.).

Rydberg's (1977) observation that $E _ {  }$ coli uvrE-mutants are hypermutable by 5BrUra implied the possibility that the mismatch correction system might recognize the mutagenic 5BrUra as a mismatch in the double-stranded (DS) DNA. The role of adenine methylation in such mismatch repair was supported by the finding that $d a m ^ { - }$ mutants are hypermutable by 5BrUra and 2AP, moderately hypermutable by ethylmethanesulfonate (EMS), but are normally mutable by UV irradiation (Glickman et al. 1978). More data are summarized in Table 1, showing that only mispairing mutagens are subject to the effect of dam mutations.

Taken together, all these results are consistent with the hypothesis of the (adenine) methylation-instructed DNA repair of mismatched bases, whereby mismatches involving normal DNA bases, base analogs, and some base alkylations can be recognized, excised selectively from the new (undermethylated) DNA chains, and replaced by correct bases during the repair synthesis (see Fig. 3 and Discussion). The direct, physical demonstration that the described effects are mismatch-provoked and not the result of general instability of undermethylated DNA chains is still missing.

# Possible Involvement of Several Mutator Loci in the Base-pair Mismatch Correction

$D a m ^ { - }$ mutants are sensitive to high concentrations of 2AP and 5BrUra (Glickman et al. 1978). In the light of the above hypothesis, this sensitivity could be caused in dam mutants by excision of closely spaced mismatched bases from both new and old DNA strands, leading eventually to double-strand breakage. Such overlapping excision could be a quite frequent event in view of very long excision tracts during the repair of mismatched base pairs in $E$ coli (Wagner and Meselson 1976). If this were true, a deficiency in mismatch repair (e.g., in endonucleolytic cleavage) could restore the resistance of $d a m ^ { - }$ mutants to analogs, while maintaining the mutator character. Therefore, we have searched for second-site, 2AP-resistant revertants of $d a m ^ { - }$ mutants, hoping to find mismatchrepair-deficient mutants which, when separated from the dam" mutation, would themselves be mutators.

# Involvement of Mismatch Correction in Genetic Recombination: Mechanism of High Negative Interference and Gene Conversion

Recombination between well-separated genetic markers is usually reciprocal, even in $E _ { \ast }$ coli (Sarathy and Meselson 1976), and can be considered to be due mainly to crossovers. As the distance between the two genetic markers decreases, there is an increased probability that one or both of them become incorporated into the DNA hybrid segment (molecular heterozygosity): a single mismatch-correction event can now lead to the formation of a genetic recombinant. Selection for DNA molecules with hybrid region in a desired segment (e.g., by preselecting for nearby recombinants in multiple-factor crosses) will reveal an excess of genetic recombinants between unselected markers, presumably because these now occur predominantly by mismatch corrections. This phenomenon is called high negative interference and was extensively studied, on a molecular level, by White and Fox (1974).

Fincham and Holliday (1970) have pointed out that when genetic markers are very close to each other, the excision tract in the correction event at one mismatch site may frequently extend to the other mismatch (cocorrection), hence, -reproducing a parental genome and leading to the loss of recombinants (so-called map expansion phenomenon). Indeed, cocorrection and long excision tracts have been demonstrated in suitable $\lambda$ heteroduplex DNA transfections of $E .$ coli (Wildenberg and Meselson 1975; Wagner and Meselson 1976).

# A Mammalian $\mathbf { 3 ^ { \prime } } \to \mathbf { 5 ^ { \prime } }$ Exonuclease Performs Several Proofreading Functions In Vitro

The proofreading exonuclease activity is defined in biochemical terms as a preferential removal of mismatched $_ { 3 ^ { \prime } }$ -OH terminal bases, thus converting a poor substrate into a good substrate for a high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Brutlag and Kornberg 1972). It can be measured in vitro as a $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease activity or as the DNA-dependent turnover of dNTPs to free dNMPs during DNA synthesis (see Table 4).

Purified mammalian DNA polymerases $\alpha , \beta ,$ and $\gamma$ are devoid of any detectable exonuclease activity (for review, see Weissbach 1977), but polymerase- $\pmb { \alpha }$ -like activity from rabbit bone marrow ("polymerase $\delta ^ { \prime \prime }$ has been shown to contain an associated $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease (Byrnes et al. 1976). However, the nature of the association is not clear.

Purified mammalian DNA polymerases $\alpha , \beta$ , and $\gamma$ can efficiently copy pyrimidine dimers, unlike $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli DNA polymerases I and III (Radman et al. 1977; Villani et al. 1978). Yet, pyrimidine dimers are not copied in mammalian cells in vivo (Lehmann 1972). This discrepancy between in vivo and in vitro DNA synthesis, which was not found for $E .$ coli (Villani et al. 1978), could be accounted for by the existence of a factor having proofreading properties similar to the $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease associated with bacterial DNA polymerases. This idea was supported by the observation that the capacity of DNA polymerase $\pmb { \alpha }$ to copy irradiated DNA increased in the course of its purification (Radman et al. 1978). The increase in the capacity for pyrimidine dimer bypass synthesis occurred concomitantly with a decrease in copying fidelity and a loss of a $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease activity (Radman et al. 1978; S. Spadari et al., in prep.).

# DISCUSSION

# Mechanisms and Genetic Controls of Spontaneous and Induced Mutagenesis

Errors in DNA replication can occur either at the growing point, when the base selection and the proofreading exonuclease fail to prevent stable incorporation of a mispairing base modification (direct mutagenesis), or as a necessarily erroneous copying of the nonpairing DNA lesions, the latter involving an inducible mechanism, SOS repair (indirect mutagenesis).

Figure 3 shows that base analogs and single-basepair mismatches may be recognized by a mismatch repair system acting specifically on the newly synthesized daughter strands. We have argued that the old/new strand discrimination is provided by the transient undermethylation of new DNA chains, as first suggested by Wagner and Meselson (1976). This hypothesis accounts for the mutator character of both mismatch-correction-deficient mutants and of adeninemethylation-deficient mutants (dam); the latter should lack strand discrimination.

Our work indicates that as many as half of the known mutator loci in $E .$ coli (dam, uurE [mutU], mutH, mutL, and mutS) may be involved in the mismatch correction. It is interesting that the mismatch-repair system in pneumococcus appears to recognize preferentially purine-pyrimidine (transitiontype) mismatches, whereas $E .$ coli mutH, mutL, mutS, and uurE mutators produce predominantly transition base changes and frameshifts (Siegel and Vaccaro 1978; Cox 1976), as does the dam mutator activity (B. Glickman, in prep.). Since frameshift and transition mutations constitute the majority of spontaneous mutations even in wild-type $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli $( \mathbf { C o x } 1 9 7 6$ ;Drake and Baltz 1976; Coulondre and Miller 1977), the methylation-instructed mismatch repair appears to be an important error-avoidance system. It is not evident why bulky purine-purine and pyrimidine-pyrimidine (transversion) mismatches would not be efficiently recognized, whereas bulky frameshift mismatches would (see also Leblon 1972), but it is conceivable that the polymerase-associated fidelity mechanisms successfully eliminate transversion-type mispairs. Indeed, the in vitro misincorporation rate by $E _ {  }$ coli DNA polymerase I for a transversion-type incorrect nucleotide is at least one order of magnitude lower than for a transition-type incorrect nucleotide (Loeb et al., this volume).

Nonpairing DNA lesions cause arrest of DNA chain elongation and unless the gap (see Fig. 3) is repaired by homologous recombination, they represent a danger to cell survival. It is fascinating that this condition leads to SOS induction which causes arrest of cellular division, induction of prophages, and several other metabolic effects (for review, see Witkin 1976), including induction of a mutator activity which is required for the fixation of mutations caused by nonpairing lesions (Radman 1974). Figure 3 shows the polymerase idling at a CT dimer, as a turnover of dNTPs to dNMPs by the polymerase-associated $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease (see Table 2) (Villani et al. 1978). We have speculated (Villani et al. 1978) and found preliminary biochemical evidence (Villani 1978) that the SOS-inducible mutator activity may be caused by the suppression of DNA polymerase-III-associated $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease. Genetic experiments have also implicated DNA polymerase III in UV mutagenesis (Bridges et al. 1976).

If this molecular scenario for the mutagenesis by nonpairing DNA lesions is correct for E. coli, then it is likely to apply to mammalian cells as well: induced reactivation of UV-irradiated herpesvirus and SV40 virus, as well as mutagenesis of both intact and UVirradiated herpesvirus, can be triggered by UV irradiation of the host cell and is inhibited by cycloheximide (DasGupta and Summers 1978 and references therein). Such qualitative results have been obtained with phage $\lambda$ in $E$ coli (Defais et al. 1976). These findings and our isolation from calf spleen of a $3 ^ { \prime } {  } 5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease with several proofreading properties (Figs. 1 and 2) support our suspicion that induced mutagenesis in mammalian cells is likely to be a genetically controlled process, like SOS induction, operating perhaps by regulating the activity of the proofreading exonuclease (Radman et al. 1977, 1978).

# Mutagenesis, Recombination, and Cancer: A Speculation

Cairns (1975) has discussed a specific strategy that the epithelial stem cells may have developed to avoid inclusion of the newly arisen, mutant daughter cells into the stem-cell elite. During a lifetime, most cell divisions, and therefore most replicational errors, occur in epithelia, and as much as $9 0 \%$ of all human cancers develop in epithelia. Cairns has speculated, and has reviewed some evidence, that the stem cells may always be able to retain the original DNA template strands and to send off the new DNA chains to the less important daughter cells which do not colonize the organism. This mechanism of mutation avoidance would be efficient only if no recombination is allowed (as pointed out by Cairns) and if no mismatch correction is allowed to occur from the parental strand. The latter event would lead to the irreversible loss of the original genetic information. Our studies and those of others, which suggest that DNA methylation may provide for DNA strand an immunity against mismatch correction, provide a hypothesis for the mechanism of genetic protection of the parental strands in the stem cells. Among other possibilities, the carcinogenicity of ethionine could be explained by its effect as an inhibitor of DNA methylation (Cox and Irwing 1977).

A recombinogenic, but otherwise nonmutagenic, treatment may become mutagenic only in this specific context of stem-cell hierarchy, through recombinational insertions of new, spontaneously mutated, DNA chains into the parental strands. Kinsella and Radman (1978) have found that tumor promoter 12-Otetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (TPA), although nonmutagenic, induces sister-chromatid exchanges and hence could be comutagenic specifically to the stem cells. However, this is not our hypothesis for tumor promotion; instead, we have postulated that a recombinational activity may be inducible by TPA (Kinsella and Radman 1978) and that mitotic recombination (and other aberrant mitotic segregations) may account for the conversion of an initiated cell, heterozygous for recessive mutations, to a homozygous mutant cell in which such mutations become expressed. Complete carcinogens, such as nitrosoguanidine (MNNG), cause both mutagenic and recombinogenic events. Table 6 shows that mitotic chiasmata, seen in the form of quadriradial and triradial chromosomes (see German 1964) and several types of chromosomal aberrations, are inducible by MNNG and that their induction is inhibited by an antipromoting and anticarcinogenic protease inhibitor, antipain. This inhibition argues for activation or inducibility of a recombinational enzymatic activity in mitotic Chinese hamster V79 cells. Since antipain inhibits neither DNA repair nor MNNGinduced mutagenesis (A. R. Kinsella and M. Radman, in prep.), the anticarcinogenic effect of antipain could be accounted for by the suppression of the second step (promotion), if the latter is indeed due to aberrant mitotic segregation visualized in the form of quadriradial and triradial chromosomes and other chromosomal aberrations (Table 6).

What might be the biological advantage of an inducible cellular mutagenic and recombinogenic system? SOS induction may contribute toward adaptability and evolution by contributing toward optimal mutation rates in changing environments. Optimal mutation rates are expected to be low in a population that is well adapted to its environment and high in a population under a high selective pressure such as environment-inflicted damage to their DNA. A transient induction of a powerful mutator activity following unrepaired damage to DNA (or to its replication machinery) appears as an elegant way to increase both the survival of the affected organisms and the fitness of the population by a rapid increase in genetic variability. In noninbred diploid organisms, increased genetic recombination also yields genetic variability and an increased population fitness (see Ayala 1967). Radiation-induced cellular recombinational capacity has been demonstrated in two lower eukaryotes, Ustilago (Holliday 1975) and yeast (Fabre and Roman 1977).
