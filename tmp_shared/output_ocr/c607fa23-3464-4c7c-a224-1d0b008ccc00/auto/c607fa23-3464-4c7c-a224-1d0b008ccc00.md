# Genetic barriers among bacteria Ivan Matic, François Taddei and Miroslav Radman

B iological species are interbreeding roups of rganisms, with each species separated from others by reproductive barriers1. This classical definition of a species, as proposed by Mayr, applies only to organisms that can interbreed. Can bacteria be classified into different species according to this criterion? Genetic exchange is very different in prokaryotes and eukaryotes2. In bacteria, reproduction and genetic exchange are independent functions and so genetic exchange occurs less frequently than in eukaryotes. During genetic exchange in bacteria only a fraction of the genome is transferred and transfer is always unidirectional, from a donor cell to a recipient cell. The size of the DNA fragment transferred depends on the transfer mechanism. Segments of transforming DNA are several kilobases long; transductionmediated recombination can transfer sections of tens to hundreds of kilobases, whereas conjugation can transfer regions of hundreds to thousands of kilobases.

Barriers to chromosomal gene transfer between bacterial species control their genetic isolation. These barriers, such

as different microhabitats, the host ranges of genetic exchange vectors and restriction-modification systems, limit gene exchange, but the major limitation is genomic sequence divergence.

In bacteria, the mobilization of genetic material primarily involves plasmids, many of which encode systems for their own transfer3. Promiscuous genetic exchange gives bacteria a special adaptive advantage: it allows cells to incorporate genes from other taxa or even other phyla that confer, for example, resistance to antibiotics, toxins and metals and the ability to degrade xenobiotic compounds. One consequence is a pool of genes that is accessible to virtually every bacterial species. Functions encoded by extrachromosomal genes are required only occasionally, whereas numerous chromosomal genes are essential for the survival, maintenance and reproduction of the cell. This could explain why the transfer of chromosomal genes is not as promiscuous as the transfer of extrachromosomal DNA.

SOS system stimulates interspecies recombination, while natural selection determines the effective recombination frequencies.

The frequency and significance of recombination in the evolution of bacteria is the subject of intense debate2-6. Comparative analyses of the DNA sequences of different gene segments from different strains yield different phylogenetic relationships, providing evidence for recombination in Escherichia, Salmonella, Neisseria and Streptococcus7-10. The amount of genetic transfer observed varies from species to species. Those bacterial species where recombination appears to be common (Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Neisseria meningitidis and Bacillus subtilis) are naturally transformable5. However, transformability does not necessarily imply that extensive recombination occurs, as Haemophilus influenzae is transformable but shows little evidence of frequent recombination in nature. For species that are not naturally transformable, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhimurium, the rate of recombination between strains in nature is relatively low, but is significant. Recombination, primarily involving short stretches of nucleotide sequence, has been important in shaping the nucleotide sequence of numerous E. coli and S. typhimurium genes7-9. However, it is difficult to estimate the recombination rate and its evolutionary relevance for an entire bacterial genome from data for a single or a few loci.

The transfer of chromosomal genes, particularly between closely related species, usually requires homologous recombination. Compared with recombination within each species, interspecies recombination of chromosomal markers occurs at a sufficiently reduced rate and extent to keep the species genetically distinct11,12. Therefore, the concept of a biological species, as used in eukaryotic systematics, may be applied to bacterial species that can exchange segments of chromosomal DNA4.

# Mechanisms of genetic isolation

The process of speciation can start only when genetic exchange is interrupted between two populations. Thus, the study of genetic isolation between closely related bacterial species might indicate what triggered the process of speciation. One theory considers isolation to be an accidental by-product of genetic divergence. Another regards isolation as a product of natural selection. The two theories for the origin of genetic isolation are not mutually exclusive. The degree of genetic isolation in nature between bacterial species may depend on several factors: differences in their microhabitats, the host ranges of genetic exchange vectors, and restriction-modification systems, as well as DNA sequence divergences and functional incompatibilities. It is not easy to identify functions involved in the initial phases of speciation because other functions contributing to genetic isolation may become fixed after speciation is complete.

Barriers, such as different microhabitats and the host ranges of genetic exchange vectors, seem to slow down DNA transmission rather than to block it. The large overlap in host range among conjugal plasmids allows sequential DNA transmission among almost all bacterial species3. Transformation and transduction may provide routes for gene transfer between conjugationally incompatible groups. The promiscuous transfer of DNA among virtually all eubacteria, yeasts and even plant cells suggests that there is no strong selection against it13. The major limitation for interspecies gene transfer seems to consist of barriers that block the establishment of the acquired genetic information.



# Restriction-modification systems

It is generally believed that restrictionmodification systems protect bacteria from infection by foreign DNA, but that this protection is limited and short lived14. Phages and other infectious foreign DNA can be modified by the restriction-modification systems and will then be able to infect bacteria of that restriction—modification system with impunity. Thus, the genetic barrier provided by restriction is far from complete, irrespective of the mechanism of DNA transfer. Single-stranded DNA is known to be refractory to most restriction systems, which may explain why the transformation of chromosomal markers of B. subtilis, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus species seems not to be affected by the presence of a restriction system in recipient cells15. Many phages have specific mechanisms to evade the effects of a host restriction system, such as the absence or masking of restriction sites and the inactivation or modulation of restriction-modification enzymes14. The transfer of conjugative plasmids is also relatively resistant to type I restriction. The sites specific for some restriction enzymes have been eliminated from transmissible plasmids by mutation and selection16. Moreover, some conjugative plasmids carry the ard (alleviation of restriction of DNA) gene, which codes for an antirestriction protein 17. Another antirestriction mechanism is prolonged mating (transfer of multiple copies of plasmids or plasmid-mediated transfer of chromosomal DNA), which seems to protect against restriction by the transient saturation of the restriction system1820.

# DNA divergence and homologous recombination

To become replicable and stably inherited, chromosomal DNA transferred by transformation, transduction or Hfr conjugation must become integrated into the recipient chromosome. The efficiency of integration by homologous recombination depends on the genomic sequence divergence between species. The increase in DNA sequence divergence has been shown to reduce the rate of transformation between Bacillus species11, and also to reduce the efficiency of recombination in E. coli22. Divergence between recombination substrates reduces recombination by reducing the number of available MEPSs.

However, once initiated, strand exchange occurs despite large numbers of mismatches and even large insertions24.25. During this stage of recombination, fidelity is controlled by the mismatch-repair system (Box 1). This has been confirmed by experiments in vitro in which purified MutS and MutL proteins have been shown to block RecA-catalyzed strand transfer between DNA substrates that differ by $3 \%$ , without affecting strand transfer between identical substrates26. The mismatchrepair system is a potent inhibitor of recombination between genomes of related bacterial species. Enzymes that recognize and process mispaired and nonpaired bases have been identified in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes23,50; their primary function is the control of genetic integrity. The mismatch-repair system controls the fidelity of chromosomal replication by eliminating DNA-biosynthetic errors (substitution and frame-shift mutations), transposon excision and the degree of genetic diversification associated with homologous recombination (chromosomal rearrangements and interspecies recombination). In both systems, the strand specificity of repair is governed by secondary signals (nicks or unmethylated GATC sequences) that may be located far from the mismatch. # The SOS system

The SOS system is induced when environmental conditions are adverse for the growth of bacterial cells (see Box 2), but it is also induced under normal environmental conditions28. SOS induction has been also detected during conjugation20, restriction of foreign or host DNA29, transposition of Tn10 (Ref. 30) and IS1 (Ref. 31), when the system of postsegregational killing of the $\mathrm { F }$ plasmid is activated32 and when plasmid or phage DNA persists in single-stranded form33,34.

Induction of the SOS system is required for efficient conjugational and transformational recombination.

# Box 2. The S0S system

The SOS system is a set of physiological responses that is induced by exposure of bacterial cells to a variety of conditions that damage DNA and/or interfere with its replication36. This complex inducible system has two general features that favor the survival of stressed bacterial populations: it enhances the capacity for DNA repair and it increases genetic variation. Regulation of the SOS system is mediated by RecA and LexA. The LexA protein acts as repressor for more than 20 genes, including the recA and lexA genes, whereas the RecA protein is involved in inducing the SOS system. The inducing signal is thought to be persistent single-stranded DNA to which RecA binds and becomes activated as a coprotease that promotes proteolytic cleavage of the LexA repressor (as well as of some phage repressors, such as $\gimel$ CI), thus derepressing the SOS regulon. The SOS functions involved in DNA repair are nucleotide excision repair, recombinational repair and SOS mutagenesis. SOS-promoted genetic variability includes targeted (to DNA lesions) and nontargeted mutagenesis, chromosomal rearrangements, transposon excision and transposition, and intra- and interspecies recombination20.

# Questions for future research

How can a bacterial species be defined?   
What is the extent and the evolutionary impact of recombination in nature?   
Is the rate of recombination similar in different populations of the same bacterial species?   
Is there an influence of stressful environmental conditions on the genetic barriers?   
Are the mechanisms that control genetic exchanges in bacteria universal? For example, do the genomic sequence divergence and the mismatch-repair proteins cause meiotic sterility in hybrids between closely related eukaryotic species?

Weak SOS induction, as detected during E. coli intraspecies conjugation, is required for efficient recombination, mainly through the overproduction of the RecA protein20. The SOS-inducing signal during intraspecies mating is probably the transient presence of singlestranded donor DNA. Very strong induction of the SOS response during conjugation between S. typhimurium and E. coli stimulates recombination in recipient cells through high concentration of the RecA and RuvAB proteins (see Fig. 1)20. Genetic characterization of the SOS response during interspecies mating suggests that the strong and persistent activation of RecA is probably caused by the reduced efficiency of early RecA-catalyzed recombination steps as a consequence of DNA divergence20. However, even during strong SOS induction, the mismatch-repair system is a powerful inhibitor of interspecies recombination.

Similarly, the SOS response is induced in B. subtilis cells in the competent state in the absence of exogenous DNA-damaging treatments35. The initial competencespecific induction of the RecA protein seems to be independent of the SOS regulon. However, in competent B. subtilis cells, single-stranded gaps are formed in chromosomal DNA, which together with transforming single-strand DNA provide a substrate for the activation of RecA. Activated RecA then derepresses SOS functions, leading to a further increase in RecA concentration, which enhances the transformation efficiency. It is possible that the level of SOS induction during transformation with divergent DNA is higher than that during transformation with identical DNA20.

In E. coli other SOS functions may be involved in the control of genetic exchanges; such as prophage induction36, restriction alleviation37, inhibition of RecBCD exonucleolytic activity38, increased permeability of the outer cell membrane39, higher transformation efficiency of $\mathrm { C a C l } _ { 2 }$ -treated cells40, induction of the himA gene (the protein product of which is involved in site-specific recombination41) and increased transposon mobility42. Because so many SOS functions are implicated in genetic exchange, genetic exchanges might improve the survival of stressed bacterial populations, in which SOS functions are induced, by increasing their genetic variability.

# Role of natural selection

Genetic exchanges in bacteria usually involve a very small fraction of the genome, which reduces the chances for deleterious mixing of incompatible functions². However, the frequency of recombination observed in natural populations depends upon both the recombination rate and natural selection. If the recombinants are selectively deleterious, they will be lost from the population and never observed. If they are selectively neutral, as one might expect for functionally equivalent 'housekeeping' proteins, most may be lost by genetic drift43-45, but some may be retained. If the recombinants are selectively advantageous, they will be retained in the population at a higher frequency than the selectively neutral ones. A class of genes where recombinants could be selectively advantageous are those for which variability is a selective advantage (for example, $\mathfrak { p i l i } ^ { 4 6 }$ , the restriction—modification system in $E . \ c o l i ^ { \bar { 4 } \bar { 7 } }$ and flagellin in Salmonella enterica48). For these genes intragenic recombination could be a major source of allelic diversity. The rate of interspecies recombination is very low, but the rare recombinants can be preserved in the cases where there is a strong selection for such recombinants49. The probability of such an event happening is greater when organisms colonize a new territory or when a population is confronted with environmental challenges. In those cases, the predominant genotype may be less than optimal, and there is greater opportunity for a new recombinant to survive and to spread in the population.
