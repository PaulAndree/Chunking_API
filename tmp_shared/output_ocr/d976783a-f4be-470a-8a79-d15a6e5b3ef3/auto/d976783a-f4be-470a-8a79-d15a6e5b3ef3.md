ABSTRACT: Genomic sequence data provide evidence for a common origin of life and for its evolution by genetic variation via mutation and recombination. This paper discusses the fundamental dialectic paradigm of evolution—stability versus variability—at the crossroads of molecular genetics, population genetics, ecology, and the emerging science of experimental evolution. Experimental evolution of molecules, viruses, and bacteria can be used not only to test some basic evolutionary hypotheses but also to create new organisms for applications in biotechnology, agriculture, and medicine.

How is the stability of DNA sequence maintained? Because DNA has no special chemical robustness, unlike some crystals, its structure is constantly corroding (e.g., oxygenfree radical damage to the bases), hydrolyzing (e.g., losing entire bases or just their amino groups leaving no, or wrong, base-pairing information for DNA polymerases), and being exposed to environmental physical and chemical insults. However, a panoply of proteins has evolved whose action, at several molecular levels and through diverse strategies, results in the observed stability of genetic information. The double-strand structure is essential in allowing a special class of proteins, DNA protection and repair proteins, to efficiently maintain the original nucleotide sequence despite frequent chemical insults on the fine DNA structure and despite the intrinsic imperfection of DNA replication machinery. Here is a list of some well known and extensively reviewed6 strategies:

# (A) Template maintenance:

Protection: Numerous proteins scavenge free radicals, degrade them, or prevent thei formation, thereby decreasing the effective DNA damage.

Repair: (i) Repair by damage reversion, such as enzymatic photoreversal of UVinduced pyrimidine dimers or transalkylation of alkylated bases (alkyl group transfer from DNA to the acceptor protein).

(ii) Repair by damage excision, such as removal of damaged bases by specific DNA Nglycosylases and removal of damage-carrying oligonucleotides by the nucleotide excision repair systems.

(iii) Repair by recombination is the only mechanism able to restore the sequences destroyed by double-strand breakage or double-strand crosslinks. The process involves "invasion," or pairing, of broken DNA ends with an intact identical molecule (e.g., sister molecule), copying from it the missing sequence and then separating the two interacting molecules.

# (B) Nucleotide pool maintenance:

The pool of four substrates for DNA synthesis is equilibrated through the activity of a complex control network. The pool is also chemically "sanitized" from aberrant residues such as dUTP (by the dUTPase) and 8-oxo-dGTP ( by MutT).

(C) High fidelity DNA copy machine:

Only when the ingredients for DNA synthesis are of high chemical purity (due to the strategies in A and B) is high fidelity DNA reproduction possible. High precision of DNA synthesis results from the selection of complementary dNTPs by the DNA polymerase (error rate about $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ ) and the excision of the just misincorporated wrong nucleotide by the "proofreading" ${ \mathfrak { z } } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb 5 } ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease. Therefore, the error rate of the replication machinery is about $1 0 ^ { - 7 }$ .

(D) Postreplicative mismatch correction:

This "quality control" system can act, in bacteria, independently of the DNA replication process by a sophisticated multi-step mechanism that involves detection of the error (mismatched or unpaired bases) by the MutS protein, strand discrimination (by the MutH protein), which targets the excision repair process to the new strand, and recopying the parental strand. Curiously, this system also edits some other aspects of DNA metabolism, including genetic recombination.7

(E) Chromosome and genome integrity mechanisms:

The high-fidelity maintenance and copying of DNA sequences are complemented by numerous mechanisms of global chromosomal maintenance. These include inactivation of parasitic transposing elements, prevention of recombination between repeated sequences, precise mitotic and meiotic chromosomal segregation, and the like.8

# MECHANISMS OF GENETIC CHANGE

Long-term survival of natural populations relies on their capacity to adapt to unpredictable environmental changes via genetic variability. Genetic variability is generated by mutation and recombination. Until recently, it was generally assumed that the mutation rates are the lowest possible given the cost of antimutator mechanisms. The dogma of molecular and evolutionary biology was that mutation is an unavoidable stochastic event because of the limits of the efficiency and fidelity of biological processes, a trade-off between the ideal and the real. However, it is possible to select, in the laboratory, bacterial mutants with increased precision of DNA or protein synthesis; therefore, it is clear that there was no durable selective pressure in nature for maximal fidelity. Perfection in the maintenance of genetic information may be too costly in energy and time. An example is bacterial ribosomal mutants resistant to high concentrations of streptomycin that have acquired very high ribosomal fidelity in the process of protein synthesis. Such mutants cannot grow any more without streptomycin (streptomycin addiction), the drug that increases the ribosomal error rate.1 Thus, in living systems, at least in bacteria, messy and efficient is more fit than precise and inefficient. These arguments will take yet another dimension when we consider long-term fitness, that is, evolutionary robustness, in relation to genetic error rates. This review shows that a significant recurrent selective pressure for genetic "messiness" must exist because several control systems in bacteria, some upregulated, some downregulated, act to increase mutation rates under strong metabolic or genotoxic stress conditions12,13 as well as under any condition allowing for the improvement of fitness1 This appeare counterintuitive either because the cost in thetrade- between mutation and adaptation was intuitively overestimated for mutation or because the benefit of adaptation was underestimated. It will become apparent that selection for genetic fidelity is balanced by selection for efficiency.

# MUTATION AND RECOMBINATION IN ADAPTIVE EVOLUTION

Random mutation produces new alleles, whereas genetic recombination produces new combinations of pre-existing alleles. This property of genetic recombination improves the effectiveness of mutagenesis because it allows for sorting of adaptive from deleterious mutations. One way to cut down the cost of adaptive mutagenesis is exemplified by the inducible mutator systems present in wild-type cells in which transient mutator activities are genetically pre-programmed. Such mutator activities are turned on only under strong selective pressure (FiG. 1C). Bacteria appear to use a very simple trick under strong selective pressure to render genes hypermutable: incorporation of microsatellite-like sequences within the coding frame. Moxon called such genes contingency genes; typically these are genes encoding bacterial surface antigens and restriction/modification systems (see Field et al., this volume). The reason for hypermutability of microsatellites (simple runs of one to six base motifs) is that all known DNA polymerases skip or add one or a few motifs when copying such sequences. This polymerase slippage error is up to $1 0 ^ { 4 }$ times more likely than a base substitution error, but it is very effectively corrected by the mismatch repair system.31 Thus, in wild-type cells, but much more so in mismatch repair-deficient mutators, such microsatellite sequences will make genes highly susceptible to frameshift mutagenesis.32

On account of the specificity of the mismatch repair system, an argument can be made that the mutation rates in microsatellites of contingency genes have been maximized. It is intriguing that the microsatellite motif in the same contingency genes is different in different bacterial species; for example, it is mononucleotide runs in Helicobacter pylori and Mycoplasma genitalium, and tetra- and pentanucleotide runs in Neisseria gonorrhoae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Escherichia coli. It turns out that Helicobacter and Mycoplasma appear not to have a functional general mismatch repair system (i.e., these species are natural mutator species), whereas the former do. Considering the specificity of the mismatch repair system in bacteria (highly efficient repair of one and two unpaired bases, but poor or no repair of four or more unpaired bases3) and the fact that DNA polymerase slips more readily the shorter the repeat motif,34 then the conclusion is that the mutation rate at microsatellites in contingency genes has been maximized. (This idea was put forward by M.R. following a lecture by R. Moxon during the International Summer School, "Molecular Basis of Bacterial Infection," August 30September 12, 1998, in Spetsai, Greece).

# EVOLUTION OF IMPROVED GENES IN THE TEST TUBE

Stemmer, Arnold, and their colleagues have developed an ingenious method for in vitro evoluion  "mproved" genes called the sexual R" or "molecular breeding"35.36 ee also Arnold, this volume). Genes in living organisms have evolved towards an optimization of their activity in the context of homeostasis of the cell, rather than to maximum activity, or efficiency, such as we might wish in an industrial, biotechnical, context. Sexual polymerase chain reaction (PCR) shows the value of recombination in adaptive evolution of genes. Genes can be highly mutagenized by the error-prone PCR conditions, then cloned and expressed in bacteria, and finally tested for increased or modified activity. Little success can be obtained with very high rates of mutation for the reasons just discussed: rare beneficial mutations will be swamped by the excess of deleterious mutations. The practice of sexual PCR involves nicking the mutated PCR products by DNaseI, denaturing and reannealing, then repairing by DNA polymerase before cloning and testing. The result is that mutations have been hyper-recombined, or shuffled, by this procedure. Selection or large-scale screening identifies gene copies with greatly modified activity. Even a "backcross," as in plant breeding, is of great value: PCR-mediated crossing of the pool of highly mutated gene copies with the initial gene copy allows separation of the rare "advantageous" mutation from frequent deleterious mutations. This is how individual deleterious, neutral, and advantageous gene mutations can be identified.36

Thus, recombination, by sorting mutations, has improved the efficacy of random mutagenesis. It may be less than coincidental that in bacteria, mutagenesis and recombination often are co-controlled as in SOS induction and mismatch repair deficiency.21

# GENERALITIES AND CONCLUSIONS

Living organisms are highly organized adaptive systems. Whereas the maintenance of organization requires conservation, adaptation requires variation, that is, genetic change. Thus, the perpetuation of life depends on fine tuning the dialectic: conservation versus change. Recent evolutionary experiments with bacteria suggest that there are several mechanisms that assure high mutation rates when survival is at stake (Fig. 1). Some are in the realm of molecular biology, such as inducible mutator systems affecting, potentially, every stressed cell. Others are in the realm of population genetics and appear to tune mutation frequencies of large bacterial populations by selection of cells with a constitutive mutator phenotype. Such mutators, although selectively disadvantageous because of their high random mutation load, are frequently present in natural populations.37,38 Mutators initially appear by random mutation in so-called mutator genes and can then be selected indirectly, for example, because of their propensity to generate mutations that happen to include rare adaptive mutations. In cells bearing an advantageous mutation created by the mutator effect, the mutator mutation will "hitchhike" with the adaptive mutation. However, following adaptation, the fitness of mutators may decrease because of the accumulation of deleterious mutations (Muller's ratchet). This may explain the rise and fall of mutators in natural and experimental populations of bacteria.

Mismatch repair-deficient mutators may directly contribute to the divergent evolution of bacteria, including their speciation. Mutators rapidly accumulate sufficient genomic sequence divergence, to strongly inhibit genetic recombination, that is, to create genetic barriers, through the activity of the mismatch repair system.23 Therefore, evolution of bacterial species could be accelerated by transitions between mutator and nonmutator states.20
