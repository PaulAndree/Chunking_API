# Mutagenesis and Cell Transformation by Ultraviolet Irradiation: Many Hypotheses for Few Results1, 2

Miroslav Radman,3 Silvio Spadari, and Giuseppe Villani3. 5

ABSTRACT-UV light-induced mutagenesis in bacteria is a genetically controlled process dependent on induction of some cellular functions, provoked initially by unrepaired photolesions in the DNA. Experiments on the extent and fidelity of in vitro DNA synthesis on UV-irradiated templates by bacterial and mammalian DNA polymerases suggest a crucial role for ${ \pmb { 3 } } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb S } ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease (proofreading) activity in UV light-induced mutagenesis. Two-stage carcinogenesis (initiation and promotion) is discussed in terms of two-stage mutagenesis (mutation fixation in the DNA and mutation expression). A unifying concept for both mutational and viral malignant transformation is proposed.-Natl Cancer Inst Monogr 50: 121127, 1978.

UV irradiation, ionizing irradiation, and many powerful chemical mutagens and carcinogens provoke in bacteria permanent genetic changes (mutations) and temporary metabolic and phenotypic changes (SOS induction) which include induction of proviruses in lysogenic bacteria (13). SOS induction is a prerequisite for UV-induced mutagenesis in bacteria (4). That DNA lesions and not some other metabolic effect of UV irradiation cause SOS induction has been demonstrated by the triggering of the cellular mutagenic and repair capacity (SOS repair) in unirradiated Escherichia coli, which have received DNA from UV-irradiated donor cells by conjugation (5). Hence intact bacteria are not "competent" to perform UV mutagenesis, and that competence is temporarily induced only after damage to cellular DNA (6).

A certain superficial similarity exists between the phenomenologies of UV mutagenesis in bacteria and of two(or multi-)stage carcinogenesis in animals (3). Chemically induced and radiation-induced tumors in animals and chemically induced and radiation-induced cell transformation in tissue culture can be produced with high frequencies as the result of a two-stage process: "initiation" by a mutagen and subsequent "promotion" by repeated exposures to tumor promoters such as phorbol esters (7-9). Since there is a strong qualitative $( I O )$ and quantitative $( I I )$ correlation between mutagenicity in bacteria and carcinogenicity in animals, either initiation or promotion, or both, are likely to be related to mutagenesis for a series of chemical carcinogens. Alternatively, mutagenesis could be correlated with carcinogenesis in a noncausal relationship, just as mutagenesis and prophage induction are correlated in bacteria (4).

# A SCENARIO FOR UV-MUTAGENESIS

In addition to genetic analyses of UV mutagenesis (16), this initial approach to study UV mutagenesis in vitro suggests the following scenario.

# UV Mutagenesis in Bacteria

Essentially, only pyrimidine dimers that have not been eliminated by the constitutive, prereplicative excision repair before being reached by the DNA replication machinery are potentially mutagenic lesions (1). Bacterial DNA replication machinery cannot elongate nascent DNA chains past pyrimidine dimers, probably because of polymerase idling due to associated ${ \mathfrak { z } } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb 5 ^ { \prime } }$ exonuclease proofreading activity. However, DNA replication continues by reinitiation beyond pyrimidine dimers, leaving gaps in the newly synthesized strands opposite dimers in the parental strands (18), which are repaired in $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli by recombinational exchanges $( I 8 )$ in an essentially error-free fashion (1). Pyrimidine dimers causing gaps that cannot be repaired by recombination probably also provoke SOS induction due to the continuous idling of DNA polymerases. A change in the nucleoside monophosphate to triphosphate ratios in the intracellular pool (as a consequence of polymerase idling and/or DNA degradation) could lead to the activation of the IP cleaving phage λ repressor (19) and perhaps some cellular repressors of the "SOS functions" (1, 2, 20). A possible result of SOS induction might be a modification of the DNA replication machinery, such as the inhibition of the ${ \pmb 3 } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb S } ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease activity of DNA polymerases (2), which should result in both DNA synthesis past pyrimidine dimers (increased survival) and decreased replicational fidelity (mutagenesis).

# UV Mutagenesis in Mammalian Cells

The prereplicative excision repair of pyrimidine dimers that eliminates potentially mutagenic lesions is deficient in the fibroblasts of some patients with xeroderma pigmentosum, and this leads to a "dose reduction" for UV mutagenesis (21). Several phenomena in radiation biology are consistent with the existence of an inducible DNA repair system in mammalian cells (3), and in particular the increased survival of irradiated viruses when assayed on irradiated host cells (22). This increase in survival raises the possibility that mammalian cells may also be mutable by the inducible SOS repair. The efficient in vitro DNA synthesis by highly purified DNA polymerase $\pmb { \alpha }$ is unlike in vivo DNA synthesis in mammalian cells, in which all, or nearly all, pyrimidine dimers cause gaps in the newly synthesized DNA (23). This discrepancy could be accounted for by our preliminary evidence (table 2) that a ${ \pmb 3 } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb 5 ^ { \prime } }$ exonuclease may be associated with, but is not an integral part of, mammalian DNA polymerase $\pmb { \alpha }$ Its function may be analogous to bacterial polymerase-associated ${ \mathfrak { z } } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb S } ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease in increasing the fidelity and preventing DNA chain elongation past pyrimidine dimers (table 2). In this instance, the inactivation or dissociation from polymerase of the ${ \mathfrak { z } } ^ { \prime }$ to ${ \pmb 5 } ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease may be required for UV mutagenesis in mammalian cells like that proposed for bacterial mutagenesis.

# A UNIFYING CONCEPT FOR MALIGNANT TRANSFORMATION INDUCED BY MUTAGENS AND VIRUSES

As mentioned previously, correlations between mutagenicity and carcinogenicity do not necessarily imply causality between the two phenomena. In particular, we know that mutagens can also exert epigenetic effects (SOS induction) in bacteria which, among other effects, are also prerequisites for mutagenesis. Therefore, we can only speculate about possible genetic mechanisms of malignant transformation with the aim of producing testable hypotheses to provoke experimental tests. Text-figure 2 illustrates a unified working hypothesis for mutational- and virus-induced cancer. This hypothesis was inspired by the postulated mechanism of SOS induction in $\pmb { \cal E } .$ coli $( I { \xrightarrow { } } 4 )$ that has been incorporated into the concept of two-stage carcinogenesis, i.e., "initiation" by mutagens and subsequent "promotion" by essentially noncarcinogenic promoters (7, 24).

The basic assumptions of the hypothesis are: 1) No introduction of new genetic information is required for malignancy. 2) Since promoted cells show most phenotypes of the transformed cells (25, 26), the transformed state is being considered as a state of permanent promotion. Temporary and permanent promotion (i.e., transformation) are initially caused by an intracellular IP, which might be a plasminogen activator [for further discussion, see (2)]. 4) Multiple phenotypic expressions of transformed cells are due to activation of many genes and/or gene products by the IP, which is supposed either to cleave or activate the cleavage of genetic repressors, or to activate inert gene products by limited proteolysis. 5) The IP may be the plasminogen activator temporarily induced by tumor promoters, such as phorbol esters (25). 6) This temporary induction of the IP during promotion can occur by inactivation of the repressor of the IP by the direct binding of phorbol esters to the repressor. 7) Permanent induction of the ${ \pmb I } { \pmb P }$ gene can occur by inactivation of its repressor either mutationally (text-fig. 2, schemes A and B3) or by a viral gene product (schemes B1, B2, B3). A list of references and source information possibly relevant to the proposed models is presented below.

# REFERENCE MATERIAL RELEVANT TO THE PREMISES OF THE HYPOTHESIS

1) Trypsin-like proteases are likely mitotic inducers in normal cells. Trypsin activates G1-blocked isolated nuclei (27) and penetrates cell and nuclear membranes (28). Activation of chick erythrocyte nuclei by active HeLa nuclei in cell hybrids is decreased by inhibitors of the trypsin-like proteases, TLCK and TAME (29). The mitotic activity of the HeLa nucleus can be suppressed by an excess of inactive chick erythrocyte nuclei in heterokaryons, and this is facilitated by TLCK and TAME (30).

2) Tumor promoters (phorbol esters) are powerful inducers of a temporary production of a plasminogen activator by normal cells in vivo and in vitro [(25, 26, 31) and references therein].

3) Permanent production of a plasminogen activator is correlated with the transformed state, independent of the transforming agent (32, 33).

4) Some natural and synthetic inhibitors of trypsin-like proteases have been shown to inhibit tumorigenesis in experimental initiation-promotion carcinogenesis in mice (31) and, curiously enough, also to suppress UV mutagenesis and UV induction of prophage λ in $\pmb { \cal E } .$ coli (2, 20).

5) The appearance of plasminogen activator in 2 and 3 requires active RNA and protein synthesis (25, 32, 33).

6) Chemically induced cell transformation may result from the mutagenesis of a single locus (34, 35). (Compare with text-fig. 2A.)

7) Mutagen-induced and spontaneous transformations appear recessive in cell hybrids between malignant and normal cells as long as a specific normal chromosome or chromosomes are present in the heterokaryon $[ ( 3 6 , 3 7 )$ and references therein]. In other words, cancer can be suppressed but not "erased," since it reappears following chromosome loss from heterokaryons $( 3 \theta )$ (Compare with text-fig. 2A.)

8) Whatever the transforming agent, mutagen or virus, various tumor cells do not complement in cell hybrids, i.e., heterokaryons remain malignant (38).

9) The transformed character of cells recently transformed by a tumor virus appears dominant in cell hybrids (39, 40). (Compare with text-fig. 2 B1, B2, B3.)

10) In virus-transformed cells, the plasminogen activator is a cell and not a virus type (32, 33). This discourages the B1 model if the plasminogen activator is the IP.

11) Continuous production of an active viral early gene product (T-antigen) is necessary to maintain virus-induced transformation (41). This discourages model B3(r).

12) The phenomenon of abortive, transitory transformation by simian virus 40 and polyoma viruses (42, 43) indicates that stable integration of viral DNA into host genome may not be necessary for the establishment of transformation but rather for its maintenance. This further discourages B3(r) and B3(d) models. In conclusion, some version of B2 is our preferred model for viral transformation. T-antigen, or a part of it, could be the anti-repressor of the ${ \pmb I } { \pmb P }$ gene (text-fig. 2, B2, solid line) or the repressor of its repressor (text-fig. 2, B2, broken line). B3(d) model still remains an attractive alternative to the B2 model. [Compare with (39).]

# A COMMENT

The proposed model (text-fig. 2) was chosen exclusively for its minimal complexity and not as an attempt to account for the genetic mechanisms of various types of malignant transformation. It is meant to illustrate the basic idea rather than to insist on the precise mechanism of induction or activation of the IP. Ideas for promotion and transformation were borrowed from microbial genetics, e.g., induction of the IP in promotion (2) is like induction of the lac operon (44), i.e., through reversible inactivation of the repressor by the binding of effector molecules. Transformation is like induction of prophage $\pmb { \lambda }$ and the pleiotropic SOS induction $( I , ~ 2 )$ ,i.e., through proteolytic cleavage (19) of repressors $( 1 , 2 , 2 0 )$ an anti-repressor protein is known in phage P22 that inactivates P22 repressor as well as the repressors of several unrelated phages including λ (45); B3(r) and B3(d) models were obviously inspired by the biology of bacteriophage $\pmb { \mu }$ and insertion sequences in $\pmb { \cal E } .$ coli.

The malignancy in the proposed model should persist only as long as active IP is produced and as long as the substrates (repressors and/or inert precursor proteins) remain susceptible to its action. Therefore, several mechanisms of suppression of cancer, which can be either pleiotropic (i.e., for all phenotypes of transformed cells) or partial (i.e., for one or few phenotypes only), are inherent to the model. These possibilities will be published elsewhere.

# MUTATION FIXATION, MUTATION EXPRESSION, AND PROMOTION

Mutation fixation following UV irradiation of bacteria takes place during the first postirradiation DNA replication round $( I )$ The fixation of $\mathbf { x }$ -ray-induced malignant transformation in cell culture requires two (46) or more (47) postirradiation generations. However, the problem of the expression of recessive mutations is expected to be more complex in diploid mammalian cells than in bacteria. It is likely that two events are required for the appearance of mutant (or transformed) mammalian cells: the mutation fixation in the DNA and a segregational event. If the mutagen-induced cell transformation is the consequence of a recessive mutation (37), this should require functional homozygosity for its expression in diploid somatic cells. Homozygosity can be brought about either by mitotic recombination (exchanges which occur between mutant and wild-type chromatids, to be segregated into different daughter cells in the region between the mutation and the centromere) following S-phase, or by chromosome loss by nondisjunction, or chromosomal rearrangements (48).

Since tumor promoters act as general inducers (26) and also increase the yield or recovery of mutagen-initiated cancer (7, 8), three testable hypotheses for promotion in somatic mutation theories of cancer can be envisaged. 1) Promoters could induce mitotic recombination or chromosome loss and/ or rearrangements, 2) promotion could be mutation expression through derepression of otherwise silent, mutated genes (49). 3) Promoters could act as co-mutagens by amplifying the mutagenic effect of unrepaired DNA lesions, analogous to SOS repair in bacteria (2, 3). The problem of mutation expression remains the same in 3 as in 1 or 2. Experiments are being initiated in our laboratory to distinguish between 1, 2, and 3.

Most of the preceeding general discussion on the mechanisms of mutation fixation and expression and on the nature of malignant change applies par excellence to UV irradiation. At effective mutagenic and carcinogenic doses, UV light almost exclusively causes damage to nucleic acids. Furthermore, the deficiency in a repair system which removes UV.

induced pyrimidine dimers from the cellular DNA is accompanied by the mutation- and cancer-proneness to UV light [(21) and references therein]. UV irradiation has acted as a complete carcinogen in humans, animals $( 5 0 )$ , and in cell culture $( 5 I )$ and as an initiator when the transforming effect is amplified by or requires a tumor promoter [(8) and references therein]. Since high doses of an initiator also provoke the metabolic effects of promotion (52), the complete carcinogenesis by high doses of initiators can be interpreted in terms of the two-stage model (2, 3).
