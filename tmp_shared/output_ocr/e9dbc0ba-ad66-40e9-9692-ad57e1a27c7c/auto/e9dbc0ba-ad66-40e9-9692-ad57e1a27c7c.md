# GATC sequence and mismatch repair in Escherichia coli

# Françoise Laengle-Rouault, Geneviève Maenhaut-Michel and Miroslav Radman1

Département de Biologie Moléculaire, Université Libre de Bruxelles, 1640-Rhode-St-Genese, Belgium, and 1Laboratoire de Mutagénèse, Institut Jacques Monod, C.N.R.S. Université Paris VII, Tour $_ { 4 3 - 2 }$ , Place Jussieu 75251 Paris Cedex 05, France

Communicated by M.F.Perutz

The Escherichia coli mismatch repair system greatly improves DNA replication fidelity by repairing single mispaired and unpaired bases in newly synthesized DNA strands. Transient undermethylation of the GATC sequences makes the newly synthesized strands susceptible to mismatch repair enzymes. The role of unmethylated GATC sequences in mismatch repair was tested in transfection experiments with heteroduplex DNA of phage $\$ 174$ without any GATC sequence or with two GATC sequences, containing in addition either a G:T mismatch $( E a m ^ { + } / E a m ^ { 3 } )$ or a G:A mismatch (Bam+ /Bam16). It appears that only DNA containing GATC sequences is subject to efficient mismatch repair dependent on E. coli mutH, mutL, mutS and mutU genes; however, also in the absence of GATC sequence some mut-dependent mismatch repair can be observed. These observations suggest that the mismatch repair enzymes recognize both the mismatch and the unmethylated GATC sequence in DNA over long distances. The presence of GATC sequence(s) in the substrate appears to be required for full mismatch repair activity and not only for its strand specificity according to the GATC methylation state.

Key words: $\phi 1 7 4 ,$ /heteroduplex DNA/transfection/methylation/ mutagenesis

# Introduction

Methyl-directed mismatch repair in Escherichia coli improves the fidelity of DNA replication by a factor $1 0 ^ { 2 }$ to $1 0 ^ { 3 }$ (Glickman and Radman, 1980). The removal and repair of replication errors (both mispaired and unpaired bases) (Glickman and Radman, 1980; Kramer et al., 1984; Wagner et al., 1984; Dohet et al., 1985, 1986) results from excision of the mis-incorporated residue from the newly synthesized strand followed by resynthesis, copying the parental strand. The excision-resynthesis tract appears to be long, sometimes ${ \bf > } 1 0 ^ { 3 }$ nt both in vivo (Wildenberg and Meselson, 1985; Wagner and Meselson, 1976) and in vitro (Lu et al., 1983). The newly synthesized strands appear to be recognized by the transient undermethylation of GATC sequences (Radman et al., 1980; Pukkila et al., 1983), which are subsequently methylated by the dam-methylase on the N6 position of the adenine residue (Marinus, 1973; Lyons and Schendel, 1984).

Although it is clear that full methylation of the GATC sequences prevents mismatch repair (Lu et al., 1983; Pukkila et al., 1983; Wagner et al., 1984), the mechanism of the methyldirection is not known. For example, the GATC sequences may be negative control elements (which prevent mismatch repair when methylated) or positive control elements (which stimulate mismatch repair when unmethylated). The negative control hypothesis predicts that DNA devoid of GATC sequences should be as efficiently mismatch repaired as the unmethylated GATCcontaining DNA, but without any strand direction, whereas the positive control hypothesis predicts that a DNA devoid of GATC sequence would not be an efficient substrate for the mismatch repair system. This work demonstrates that phage $\phi 1 7 4$ DNA requires GATC sequence(s) for efficient repair. Therefore we suggest that the mismatch repair enzymes must recognize both the mismatch (G:T and G:A in this study) and the non-methylated GATC sequence on the same DNA molecule in order to excise the mismatch from the strand containing the unmethylated GATC sequence.

# Results

Heteroduplex DNA is a DNA duplex containing two complementary strands which do not carry identical genetic information, i.e. which carry one or more mismatched or unmatched base pairs (bp). The basic genetic property of such heteroduplex molecules is that on replication they segregate genetically, i.e. a single DNA molecule yields mixed progeny which contains genotypes of the two individual heteroduplex strands. However, genetic segregation of heteroduplex DNA occurs only if (i) no mismatch repair occurs before DNA replication and (ii) both DNA strands replicate and yield viable progeny. Thus if only one heteroduplex molecule enters the transfected cell, mixed progeny indicates the absence of mismatch repair. Under the standard EDTA-lysozyme transfection procedure with $\phi 1 7 4$ DNA that we have used, the probability for two molecules to penetrate the same transfected cell is very low (Benzinger, 1978). However, the 'pure' infective centers (i.e. infective centers containing only one of the two genotypes carried by the two heteroduplex strands) can arise not only as the consequence of mismatch repair but also by any kind of asymmetric DNA replication (e.g. rolling circle replication or a strand loss). The comparison of the fraction of mixed infective centers produced following transfection with heteroduplex DNA of mismatch repair proficient and mismatch repair deficient $E .$ . coli provides a genetic estimate of mismatch repair in vivo (cf. Dohet et al., 1985).

# Discussion

The aim of the experiments reported was to test whether GATC sequences are involved in strand-direction of mismatch repair as inhibitors of repair when methylated, or as required co-factors for mismatch repair when unmethylated (see Introduction). Our results support the hypothesis that the unmethylated GATC sequence is actively involved in mismatch repair. The basic argument is that GATC-free DNA $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ heteroduplexes yield more mixed infective centers in transfection experiments with mismatch repair proficient $( m u t ^ { + } )$ $E .$ coli $( \sim 2 0 \%$ , see Tables I and II), than the $^ { \bullet } 2$ GACT' $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ heteroduplex DNA $( \sim 5 \%$ , see Tables I and II). Experiments with phage $\lambda$ heteroduplex DNA have established that a low fraction of mixed infective centers in $E .$ . coli mut + transfections, as compared with mut\~ (H, L, S or U) transfections, is indicative of the activity of $E .$ coli methyl-directed mismatch repair system (Dohet et al., 1985). Therefore, experiments with $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 ~ a m ^ { + } / a m$ heteroduplex DNAs suggest that most mut-dependent repair of the G:T mismatch at position 587 and of the G:A mismatch at position 5276 is also GATC sequence dependent.

In GATC-free DNA heteroduplexes, a slightly higher fraction of mixed infective centers in transfections of mut mutants than with mut + may be indicative of some weak mut-dependent but GATC-independent repair, in particular for the G:A mismatch. The maximum $32 \%$ mixed infective centers obtained from mut transfection, as compared with up to $90 \%$ with phage λ, heteroduplexes (Wagner et al., 1984; Dohet et al., 1985) may be a consequence of asymmetric rolling circle DNA replication (Baas and Jansz, 1972) or perhaps of some mut and GATC-independent mismatch repair. The bias in the proportion of the progeny of heteroduplex strands (Table II, line 7) in GATC-free DNA indicates that the nature of the mismatch (G:A) and/or the proximity of the nick (see Figure 1) may also influence repair of the asymmetric DNA replication.

It was suggested that nicks may in general strand-direct mismatch repair and that the only role of the GATC sequence is to provide nicks by a GATC-specific endonuclease (Manarelli et al., 1985). However, in our experiments, the nick at the PstI site (Figure 2) in our $\mathbf { \epsilon } ^ { \bullet } \mathbf { 0 }$ GATC' heteroduplexes does not appear to complement for either the GATC sequence or any of the four mut gene mutations (Table I). (We are now testing whether a nick in the GATC sequence may complement any of the mut mutations. Furthermore, preliminary results indicate that the ligation of the $P s t \mathbf { I }$ nick does not significantly change the pattern of mismatch repair. This could mean that this nick is readily sealed by the $E .$ coli ligase upon transfection or that this nick has no major effect on mismatch repair.)

One can consider that a significantly lower fraction of mixed infective centers in '2 GATC' heteroduplex transfections of the mutH mutant, compared with other mut mutants, indicates some residual repair activity because such observations were made with phage M13 (Kramer et al., 1984) and $\lambda$ (C.Dohet, R.Wagner and M.Radman, unpublished observations) heteroduplexes. Nearly total absence of mismatch repair in mut mutants, when tested with phage λ DNA heteroduplexes (Radman et al., 1980; Pukkila et al., 1983; Dohet et al., 1985) and the potent mutator effect of mut mutants (Glickman and Radman, 1980), argue against a general alternative mismatch repair system. Some weak residual mismatch repair in mut mutants (C.Dohet, R. Wagner and M.Radman, unpublished observations) and some specialized sequence-specific mismatch repair (Lieb, 1983) are likely to operate in E. coli. Therefore we cannot assess the relative contributions of these putative mismatch repair events or of the well known asymmetry of rolling circle replication of $\$ 124$ DNA (Baas and Jansz, 1972) to the generation of pure infective centers.

Studies on mismatch repair in phage λ heteroduplex DNA have shown that G:A mismatches are sometimes not readily recognized and repaired by the $E _ {  }$ coli mismatch repair system (Kramer et al., 1984; Wagner et al., 1984; Dohet et al., 1985). However, recent studies have established a key role of neighbouring bps in the reparability of the G:A mismatch, and the reparability of the G:A mismatch in position 5276 in $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ DNA is consistent with the rule of reparability established in λ DNA heteroduplexes (M.Jones, R.Wagner and M.Radman, submitted).

We cannot exclude the possibility that the differences in the repair pattern of the studies G:T and G:A mismatches between the '0 GATC' and '2 GATC' heteroduplexes are due not only to the absence and presence of GATC sequences, but also to some difference in the sequence of the entire insert (55 and 64 bp respectively). However, the fact that the GATC methylation of one strand directs mismatch repair almost exclusively to the unmethylated strand (Table II) argues for a predominant role of the GATC sequences. Recent work of R.Lahue, S.S.Su and P. Modrich (unpublished) shows that in vitro mismatch repair, in $E .$ coli extracts, depends on the presence of GATC sequences in the substrate heteroduplex DNA. Thus the GATC sequence may act as a co-factor in cis for mismatch repair in DNA, since the presence in trans of a large number of unmethylated GATC sequences in $E .$ coli DNA heteroduplex transfections of dam mutants did not affect the efficiency of mismatch repair in $\mathbf { \epsilon _ { 0 } }$ GATC' heteroduplexes (Tables I and II). $\mathbf { N } ^ { \bullet }$ Adenine methylation of the GATC sequence appears to inactivate its co-factor activity in cis on the methylated strand only (Table II; Lu et al., 1983; Pukkila et al., 1983; Wagner et al., 1984). The implication for the enzymatic mechanism of mismatch repair in $E .$ coli is that the mismatch repair proteins probably recognize both the mismatch and the unmethylated GATC sequence, which are sometimes over several hundred nt [minimum $\sim 4 0 0$ for the G:T $( E a m ^ { + } / E a m 3 )$ mismatch and $> 1 0 0 0$ for the G:A $( B a m ^ { + } / $ Bam16) mismatch, see Figure 1]. Endonucleolytic scission must then occur on the strand with unmethylated GATC sequence, perhaps at the site of the GATC sequence (Lu et al., 1984). In other words, the E. coli mismatch repair system may act as a GATC-specific, mismatch-stimulated, endonuclease/exonuclease activity. Such a molecular mechanism could account for the accuracy in strand discrimination during mismatch repair of the transient hemimethylated newly replicated DNA.

# DNA preparation

Single-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ DNA of diverse mutants was extracted with phenol at $5 5 \%$ (Scheckman et al., 1971) from CsCl purified phages (Radman, 1976). Closed circular RFI DNA was prepared as described (Godson and Vapnek, 1973) except for purification on neutral sucrose gradients (instead of alkaline gradients).

# Construction of circular heteroduplex DNAs

Different heteroduplex DNA species were constructed by annealing single-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 \ a m ^ { + }$ DNA and linearized double-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ DNA of Eam3 or Bam16 phages (see Figure 2). Table IV shows all six heteroduplex species, which vary in the nature of their insert (i.e. $\mathbf { \nabla } ^ { \bullet } \mathbf { 0 }$ GATC' or $^ { * 2 }$ GATC'), their GATC methylation and the nature and position of the mismatch. Unmethylated RFI DNA, which provides the complementary strand for heteroduplex DNA, was produced in the HF 4733 dam3 mutant, methylated DNA in HF $4 7 3 3 ~ d a m ^ { + }$ . The presence of inserts was tested by the HaeIII restriction enzyme (Müller and Wells, 1980); the presence of GATC sequences was tested by Sau3A (which cuts irrespective of methylation) and the state of methylation was tested by the restriction enzymes MboI (which cuts only unmethylated GATC) and DpnI (which cuts only methylated GATC).

The scheme for heteroduplex preparation of the $E a m ^ { + } / E a m ^ { 3 }$ heteroduplexes is shown in Figure 2. The double-stranded RFI DNA was linearized by unique site-cleavage with PstI enzyme (Boehringer) (see also Figure 1) to allow annealing with the competing viral strand. Two-fold excess of single-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 E a m ^ { \bar { + } }$ DNA was added to the linearized RFI DNA, heated to $9 5 \mathrm { ^ \circ C }$ for 3 min in 0.01 M Tris, pH 8.5, 0.01 M NaCl and $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ M EDTA, chilled in iced water, then annealed after addition of 0.3 M NaCl, at ${ \mathfrak { 6 2 } } ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ for $2 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ , followed by slow cooling to room temperature. Circular duplex DNA was purified in neutral $5 - 2 0 \%$ sucrose gradient (Beckman SW50 rotor, 45 000 r.p.m. for $1 9 0 \ \mathrm { m i n }$ at $2 0 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C } )$ . Only heteroduplex molecules form relaxed circular duplexes, which were identified by electrophoretic analysis in agarose gels (Figure 2). The contamination of circular heteroduplex by circular single-stranded DNAs was insignificant, and contamination by the linear homoduplex was irrelevant due to its biological inactivity in transfection.

# Transfection and scoring of infective centers

Spheroplasts for transfection were prepared by the lysozyme-EDTA procedure (Benzinger, 1978). Transfection was performed at $3 7 ^ { \circ } \mathbf { C }$ for $8 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ and the infective centers were plated on the HF 4733 $( \mathbb { S } \mathfrak { u } ^ { + } )$ indicator. Isolated infective centers were picked and phage progeny streaked on individual plates the same day to avoid phage diffusion on plates. The discrimination between three kinds of infective centers (containing pure $a m ^ { + }$ progeny, pure am progeny and mixed am+/am progeny) was achieved using a mixed indicator lawn [2/3 HF 4704 $( \mathbb { S } \mathfrak { u } ^ { - } )$ , 1/3 HF 4733 $( \mathbb { S } \mathfrak { u } ^ { + } )$ of overnight cultures]. Eam3 and Bam16 phages give turbid plaques and wild-type phages clear plaques on such a mixed indicator lawn.

Transfections were performed at least three times with each DNA preparation. Different DNA preparations of the same heteroduplex yielded essentially identical results.

Transfection efficiency was in the range of $1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ transfected cells per DNA heteroduplex at a multiplicity of about one DNA molecule per cell.
