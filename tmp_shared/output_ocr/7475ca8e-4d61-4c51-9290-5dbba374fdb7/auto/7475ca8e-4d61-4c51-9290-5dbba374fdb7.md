# Protection and Host Cell Repair of Irradiated Lambda Phage I. Irradiation of Normal Phage with Ultraviolet Light

M. RADMAN\*, A. ROLLER\*\* and M. ERRERA Laboratoire de Biophysique et Radiobiologie, Faculté des Sciences, Université libre de Bruxelles

Received March 10, 1969

Summary. Phage λ, irradiated with ultraviolet light in the presence and absence of the free-radical scavenger and proton donor, cysteamine, have been assayed on normal and DNA-repair deficient bacteria. It has been concluded that cysteamine protects the phage against lesions of a type which can be repaired by the bacterial DNA-repair systems. Presumably, these are pyrimidine dimers.

# Introduction

DNA, exposed to ionizing radiations, is partially protected by the presence of certain sulfhydryl compounds, such as cysteamine (Cy) (BAcQ and ALExANDER, 1961). Protection is probably due to the ability of such compounds to act as proton donors and scavengers of free radicals (ORMERoD and ALExANDER, 1963). It has been reported that, in the case of Tl phage exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light, there is no protection by Cy (HoTz, 1963). The present experiments were undertaken to determine whether a protective effect of Cy is masked by the DNA repair systems of the host bacterium on which the irradiated phage are assayed. This has indeed been found to be true in the case of phage λ. The mechanism of protection by Cy is discussed; it is probably due to the partial suppression of pyrimidine dimer formation.

# Discussion and Conclusions

The results demonstrate that Cy protects $\lambda$ from UV irradiation. The lesions which are prevented by its presence are not repaired by uvr $\boldsymbol { C }$ bacteria, although they are completely repaired by normal bacteria. This strongly suggests that these lesions are pyrimidine dimers, which are the only lesions known to be in large part reparable by WT, and irreparable by uvr mutants (SETLow and CARRIER, 1964; BOYCE and HOWARD-FLANDERS, 1964).

Rec $A ^ { - }$ mutants, in the presence or absence of $\mathrm { C y }$ ,are able to repair many lesions which cannot be repaired by uvr $C ^ { - }$ Included are some lesions which are prevented by Cy. That rec $A ^ { - }$ gives curves which are very similar to WT may be explained by the finding that the rec $A ^ { - }$ bacterial mutation does not totally eliminate recombination repair. Rec A mutants excise normally (CLARK et al., 1967) and the missing function affecting λ survival is partially provided by the λ red gene (EcHoLs and GINGERY, 1968; RADMAN et al., in preparation). In the λcI 857 used in these experiments, the red gene is intact.

A possible mechanism for Cy protection is suggested by a recent work of MILvy and PuLLMAn (1968). They have shown chemically that the amino group of Cy is electrostatically bound to the negatively charged phosphate of nucleotides or DNA, and probably, in the latter case, specifically to the $\mathbf { 5 ^ { \prime } }$ phosphate of thymine. By means of electron spin resonance, they have also demonstrated that energy from ionizing radiations is transferred from the other bases to thymine, and thence to Cy. If such a transfer of energy, absorbed by the bases during UV irradiation, is similarly transferred to thymine (GuERon et al., 1966) and then to Cy, then this might be a mechanism for preventing formation of thymine dimers.

The experiments would indicate that Cy reduces the number of thymine dimers by about $4 0 \%$ . This is now being checked chemically.
