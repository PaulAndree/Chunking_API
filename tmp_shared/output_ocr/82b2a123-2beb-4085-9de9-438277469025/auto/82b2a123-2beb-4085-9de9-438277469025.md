# Molecular Mechanisms of Induced Mutagenesis. Replication in vivo of Bacteriophage $\$ 174$ Single-stranded, Ultraviolet Light-irradiated DNA in Intact and Irradiated Host Cells

Perrine Caillet-Fauquet, Martine Defais And Miroslav Radman

Départmente de Biologie Moléculaire Université Libre de Bruxelles Rue des Chevaux 67, B 1640 Rhode St Genèse, Belgium (Received 17 February 1977, and in revised form 20 June 1977)

Genetic analysis has revealed that radiation and many chemical mutagens induce in bacteria an error-prone DNA repair process which is responsible for their mutagenic effect. The biochemical mechanism of this inducible error-prone repair has been studied by analysis of the first round of DNA synthesis on ultraviolet light-irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA in both intact and ultraviolet light-irradiated host cells. Intracellular $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { I } 7 4$ DNA was extracted, subjected to isopycnic CsCl density.gradient analysis, hydroxylapatite chromatography and digestion by single-strand-specific endonuclease ${ \bf S } _ { 1 }$ Ultraviolet light-induced photolesions in viral DNA cause a permanent blockage of DNA synthesis in intact Escherichia coli cells. However, when host cells are irradiated and incubated to fully induce the error-prone repair system, a significant fraction of irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA molecules can be fully replicated. Thus, inducible error-prone repair in $\pmb { \cal E }$ .coli is manifested by an increased capacity for DNA synthesis on damaged $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA. Chloramphenicol $( { \bf 1 0 0 } ~ \mu { \bf g } / { \bf m l } )$ , which is an inhibitor of the inducible error-prone DNA repair, is also an inhibitor of this particular inducible DNA synthesis.

# 1. Introduction

There is considerable evidence (for reviews see Radman, 1975; Witkin, 1976) supporting the hypothesis (Radman, 1974) that radiation and most (but not all) chemical mutagens exert their mutagenic effect through induction of a transitory error-prone DNA repair process (Witkin, $1 9 7 5 a$ ; Defais et al., 1976). Hence, DNA lesions produced by these agents are necessary but not sufficient to cause mutations. The fact that ultraviolet light irradiation of lambda or $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phages does not cause a significant increase in phage mutation frequency (Weigle, 1953; Miura & Tomizawa, 1968; Defais et al., 1971; Bleichrodt & Verheij, 1974), while irradiation of the host cell increases the frequency of mutations in both intact and u.v.-irradiated phage (Ichikawa-Ryo & Kondo, 1975; Bleichrodt & Verheij, 1974), supports this hypothesis. The induction of new Escherichia coli gene functions required for the mutagenic DNA repair is indicated by the observation that chloramphenicol inhibits this repair (Defais et al., 1976). Mutagenic repair can be heat-induced in tif (Castellazzi et al., 1972; Witkin, 1974) and dnaB mutants of $\pmb { \cal E }$ . coli (Witkin, 1975b; Caillet-Fauquet $\pmb { \& }$ Defais, 1977). Since induction of prophage lambda exhibits similar characteristics (see Defais et al., 1971,1976), it is thought (see Witkin, I976; Defais et al., 1976)

that the induction of the error-prone DNA repair system may occur via a mechanism comparable to that for lambda induction, i.e. by inactivation of (a)cellular repressor(s) (see Roberts & Roberts, 1975).

In an attempt to study the biochemical mechanism of this inducible mutagenic DNA repair ("SOS repair", Radman, 1974,1975; see review by Witkin, 1976), we took advantage of the fact that the single-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA appears to be subject to SOS repair (Tessman & Ozeki, 1960; Bleichrodt & Verheij, 1974; Das Gupta & Poddar, 1975). Since neither excision nor recombination repair can act on singlestranded DNA molecules, these two known repair mechanisms are unlikely to be responsible for SOS repair. Similar conclusions were drawn from genetic studies with phage lambda (Radman $\&$ Devoret, 1971; Defais et al., 1971: Blanco $\&$ Devoret. 1973). Furthermore, genetic evidence suggests that u.v.-induced mutations of $\phi \mathrm { X I } 7 4$ phage are produced in the irradiated host cells only during the first round of DNA synthesis; that is, in the passage from single-stranded to the double-stranded form of $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA (Bleichrodt & Verheij, 1974). In both intact and irradiated host cells, we have followed this transition from single-stranded to double-stranded DNA. The incoming $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phage DNA contained between five and eight u.v.-induced photolesions, predominantly pyrimidine dimers. It appears that a single photolesion in $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phage DNA is sufficient to block completely DNA synthesis in intact host cells (see also Benbow et al., 1974). However, SOS repair, induced by u.v. irradiation of the host cells, often permits continuation of DNA synthesis past photolesions. This process probably involves incorporation of non-complementary nucleotides: that is. mutagenesis (Radman et al.., 1976,1977).

# 3. Results

# (a) Experimental plan

The synthesis of the full complement to the viral strand is indispensable for the development of $\phi \mathbf { X 1 7 4 }$ phage, since this strand is used as the template for transcription (Sinsheimer, 1959) and for rolling circle replication (Dressler & Wolfson, 1970). Therefore, if the principal cause of killing of $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ by ultraviolet light is due to failure to synthesize a full, continuous strand (-) complementary to the viral strand $( + )$ , then the SOS repair would be expected to improve the cellular capacity to synthesize the $(  )$ strand. Consequently, the principal aim of this study was to determine the extent of DNA synthesis on viral DNA templates containing u.v.- induced photolesions both in intact and irradiated host cells.

The experimental scheme is shown in Figure 1. In order to follow the fate of parental $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ single-stranded DNA containing an estimated† average of 5·5 u.v.-induced pyrimidine dimers (see Appendix), only parental strands were radioactively labeled. To prevent the loss of double-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA due to rolling circle replication, $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ Aam18 was used to infect HF4704, a non-suppressing host. The $\pmb { A }$ gene product is a highly specific endonuclease (Henry & Knippers, 1974) necessary for the initiation of rolling circle replication. In order to produce maximum induction of the SOS repair, HF4704 uvrA- cells were u.v. irradiated $( 7 \ \mathbf { J } \mathbf { m } ^ { - 2 } )$ and incubated for 30 minutes before infection (Defais et al., 1976). Either intact or u.v.-irradiated phage $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ was adsorbed to intact and irradiated HF4704 hosts and incubated for various times before DNA extraction (see Materials and Methods). Extracted $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { I } 7 4$ DNA was then subjected to three different analyses, all aimed to provide measures of the extent of DNA synthesis in the first replication round (see Fig. I).

# (c) Analysis of intracellular $\phi X I 7 4$ DNA by hydroxylapatite chromatography followed by CsCl density-gradient centrifugation

CsCl density-gradient centrifugation analysis of the total extracted intracellular $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA (Figs 2 and 3) cannot distinguish clearly the relative contributions by SOS repair to initiation and to elongation in the first round of DNA synthesis on u.v.-irradiated viral DNA. We have used hydroxylapatite chromatography to separate $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA containing a double-stranded segment of at least 100 nucleotide pairs (Wilson & Thomas, 1973) from the completely single-stranded DNA. Thus, all molecules that have undergone partial DNA replication will bind to the hydroxylapatite column and will be eluted with $\mathbf { 0 } { \cdot } \mathbf { 3 } \ \mathbf { \mathbf { u } }$ -phosphate buffer (see Materials and Methods).

DNA was fractionated by hydroxylapatite chromatography into single-stranded DNA and DNA containing double-stranded regions (fraction I and II in Table 2). Each fraction was then analyzed in CsCl density gradients to determine the average degree of double-strandedness (i.e. extent of DNA synthesis), as described in the legends to Figure 2 and 3 (see also legend to Table 2).

Table 2 shows the results of one such experiment. The increased fraction of molecules containing a double-stranded region indicates that u.v. irradiation of the host cell increases the proportion of irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { l } 7 4$ DNA molecules that attempt replication (column F). When this fraction is multiplied by the average degree of double-strandedness (column $\mathbf { E }$ ), an estimate of the amount of replication of total DNA input from irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phages is obtained (column G). In the experiment shown. induction by u.v. irradiation of SOS repair in the host cells increased the replication of irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { I } 7 4$ DNA from $6 { \cdot } 6 \%$ to $4 3 \%$ . The average degree of double-strandedness of the fraction II DNA extracted from intact host cells infected with phages carrying five pyrimidine dimers per genome was about $1 9 \%$ . This observation is consistent with the notion that each single pyrimidine dimer in the template strand permanently blocks DNA synthesis. # 4. Discussion

Single-stranded phage $\phi \mathbf { X 1 7 4 }$ DNA was used in this work as the substrate for inducible mutagenic DNA repair in vivo. The data presented are in agreement with the hypothesis that in untreated $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli cells, u.v.-irradiated phage $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA is replicated to the first photolesion only, whereas in irradiated cells replication sometimes extends beyond photolesions. These photolesions are predominantly, and perhaps exclusively, pyrimidine dimers (G. Villani, S. Boiteux & M. Radman, unpublished work). Benbow et al. (1974) and Zuccarelli et al. (1976) also studied the size distribution of the replicating u.v.-irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { I } 7 4$ molecules in unirradiated cells and found that the size of double-stranded segments was inversely proportional to the u.v. dose given to the phage before infection, a result expected if one lethal photolesion blocked replication of single-stranded DNA.

Their inability to detect molecules containing two or more double-stranded segments excluded the possibility of frequent reinitiations beyond the first photolesion. However, to argue further for this hypothesis, some aspects of the physiology of $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { l } 7 4$ phage must be considered for quantitative interpretation of our results.

Francke & Ray (1971) and D. Ray (personal communication), who were the first to observe inhibition of $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA synthesis by u.v.-irradiation, have pointed out that the single-stranded DNA may have remained unreplicated because of abortive penetration of irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA into the cell, possibly due to a protein-DNA crosslink. Our data indicate that the DNA of a fraction of well-adsorbed $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phages, which cannot be detached from the host cells by four successive washes (see Materials and Methods), often does not replicate at all (Fig. 2(a)). The proportion of this DNA that remains single-stranded is enhanced when phages have been irradiated (Fig. 2(b); Table 2). Francke $\&$ Ray's interpretation probably accounts for this failure to initiate replication, an event which sometimes occurs normally and is potentiated with u.v.-irradiation of the phage. The proportion of molecules in which no significant DNA synthesis took place is estimated here by hydroxylapatite chromatography (Table 2). When neither phage nor host cell was irradiated, the failure to engage in DNA synthesis was found in 0 to $3 0 \%$ of the viral DNA (see Results and Fig. $\mathbf { \nabla } ^ { 2 } ( \mathbf { a } )$ ). In the experiment shown in Table 2, irradiation of phages with a dose of $7 0 ~ \mathrm { J m } ^ { - 2 }$ increased the fraction of non-replicating molecules to $6 5 \%$ , and irradiation of the host cell decreased the fraction of non-replicating irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { l } 7 4$ molecules to $3 8 \%$ . However, the class of irradiated molecules in which both initiation and some elongation of DNA synthesis did occur (fraction II in Table 2) is of particular interest in relation to our hypothesis for the mechanisms of SOS repair and induced mutagenesis.

All the approaches used in this work show clearly that u.v.-induced DNA lesions, probably pyrimidine dimers (see below), block elongation of DNA synthesis on the viral strand (see also Francke & Ray, 197l; Benbow et al., 1974). The estimated length of the double-stranded segment is between $8 \%$ and $2 0 \%$ (Tables 1 and 2; Figs 4 and 5) in the presence of an average of 4 and 5.5 pyrimidine dimers or 5·8 and 7.5 lethal hits per viral genome produced by doses of $5 0 \ \mathbf { J } \mathbf { m } ^ { - 2 }$ and $7 0 \ \mathrm { J m } ^ { - 2 }$ , respectively (see Appendix). DNA synthesis in vitro on u.v.-irradiated, primed $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { I } 7 4$ DNA, carried out either by $E$ . coli DNA polymerase I or polymerase III, is also blocked at the first pyrimidine dimer and the blockage is alleviated by monomerization of pyrimidine dimers by the photoreactivating enzyme (G. Villani, S. Boiteux & M. Radman, unpublished work). In three independent experimental approaches, an increase in the relative amount of double-stranded $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ DNA from u.v.-irradiated phage was observed when phage DNA was extracted from irradiated cells, as compared to the yield of double-stranded DNA of the same phages from unirradiated cells. This increase in double-stranded versus single-stranded $\phi \mathrm { X I 7 4 }$ DNA species could arise if more initiations occurred at the origin of DNA synthesis, and/or if more initiation occurred at some other initiation sites, and/or if occasional elongation of nascent DNA chains past u.v.-induced photolesions did occur. Table 2 shows that more initiations at the origin can occur, probably only in experiments where DNA synthesis did not initiate on all phage DNA (e.g. Fig. 2(a)). We cannot exclude the possibility that initiations at sites other than the origin do occur in irradiated cells. However, we argue that our results indicate replication past u.v.-induced photolesions in irradiated cells. All three types of experiments show that fully replicated parental DNA from irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ phage appears in irradiated host cells; the experiments with $\mathbf { S _ { 1 } }$ endonuclease being the most convincing (Fig. 5(b)). The fraction of fully replicated molecules detected was always found to be larger than the fraction of molecules expected to be lesion-free assuming random distribution of u.v.-induced photolesions in the population of phage $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ genomes (see Results). We find no argument to support the trivial explanation of the latter results such as an improved relative recovery of the double-stranded DNA species from irradiated host cells, either by a preferential loss of double-stranded DNA from non-irradiated cells or by a preferential loss of single-stranded DNA from irradiated cells. No preferential loss of the parental DNA label was detected when total radioactivity was recovered from infected cells and analyzed in alkaline sucrose gradients (results not shown), and the absolute recovery of the fraction of parental DNA that became double-stranded was always higher when irradiated host cells were used (e.g. see Figs 4 and 5). Furthermore, the estimates given above of the fraction of RFI DNA versus the fraction of u.v.-induced photolesion-free molecules were made taking into account the initial input radioactivity (see $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ endonuclease experiments); hence we prefer to argue that the increase in the amount of the irradiated, fully replicated $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { l } 7 4$ DNA is due to de novo synthesis of this DNA species rather than to the relative loss of the other DNA species.

How could some irradiated $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA molecules fully replicate in irradiated hosts? It is hard to conceive a mechanism for elimination of pyrimidine dimers (or other photolesions) from single-stranded DNA, other than monomerization, as in photoreactivation. The latter process would be expected to lead to an increase in survival but not an increase in mutagenesis, which is a prominent feature of SOS repair. Our experiments were performed in the absence of exposure to light to avoid this possibility (see Materials and Methods). Also, the host cells were deficient in excision repair.

A working hypothesis to account for our results is shown schematically in Figure 6. DNA lesions, presumably pyrimidine dimers in this case, block the elongation of growing DNA chains (Fig. 6, molecule b). The presence of such replication-inhibiting DNA lesions in host DNA would induce the cellular SOS repair system. This process would require de novo protein synthesis and one or more products to allow the DNA replication machinery to proceed across pyrimidine dimers (Fig. 6, molecule c). In support of this idea, it is found that a minority of u.v.-induced mutations occur in DNA sequences which do not bear two neighboring pyrimidines (J. Miller, personal communication).

In conclusion, it appears likely that the u.v.-induced mutagenic repair of singlestranded $\phi \mathbf { X } \mathbf { I } 7 4$ DNA might be the result of an erroneous DNA synthesis, which could account for both increased survival (SOS repair) and for induced mutagenesis (Fig. 6). This process could either involve an unknown inducible DNA polymerase, perhaps similar to the mammalian terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (Chang & Bollum, 1971; Witkin, 1975c) and/or result from a modification of the normal DNA replication machinery (Radman et al., 1977). This hypothesis for u.v.-induced mutagenesis also applies to the double-stranded DNA genomes. In u.v.-irradiated $E$ .coli and F-episome DNA replication, pyrimidine dimers are not copied initially. However, unlike in $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4$ DNA, reinitiations of DNA synthesis beyond pyrimidine dimer blocks do occur in both strands leading to continuation of DNA replication with gaps in the newly synthesized strands (Howard-Flanders et al., 1968). At low u.v. doses (survivals higher than $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ ), in the absence of excision repair, the majority of these gaps are repaired by single-strand exchanges among daughter molecules (Rupp et al., 1971) in an essentially error-free fashion, while a minority of gaps (perhaps those that cannot be repaired by strand exchanges) require SOS repair (Sedgwick, 1975). The filling of these minority gaps, which is sensitive to chloramphenicol inhibition, could account for the totality of u.v.-induced mutagenesis in $E$ coli (Sedgwick, 1975). We suggest that the latter gap-flling is performed by the same erroneous DNA synthesizing machinery causing u.v.-induced mutagenesis in $\phi \mathbf { X } 1 7 4 $ phage (Fig. 6).
