Evolution, the fundamental strategy of Life, is an interplay of genetic variation and phenotypic selection. The availability of genomic sequence data for a large number of organisms is providing the most solid proof of a common origin of life and of its diversification by mutation and recombination. On the other hand, we are learning to use synthetic DNA and RNA sequences, viruses and bacteria to follow, and create, evolutionary processes in the laboratory. Experimental evolution can be used to test some basic evolutionary hypothesis but also to create new biodiversity for use in biotechnology, agriculture and medicine. This a conceptual paper, a discussion of the fundamental paradigm of evolution - stability vesus variability-at the crossroad ofmolecular genetics, population genetics, ecology and the emerging science of experimental evolution.

# Molecular Biology of Genetic Change

Long-term survival of natural populations depends on their capacity to adapt to unpredictable environmental changes via genetic variability. Genetic variability is generated by mutation and recombination. Until recently, it was assumed that the mutation rates are the lowest possible given the cost of antimutator mechanisms. The dogma of molecular and evolutionary biology was that mutation is an unavoidable stochastic event due to the limits of the efficiency and fidelity of biological processa trade-off between the ideal and the real. This thinking may have been influenced by the irrational, anthropomorphic (perhaps religious) thoughts rather than by the Darwinian evolutionary thinking. # Mutation and Recombination in Adaptive Evolution

Random mutation produces new alleles wheres genetic recombination produces new combination of pre existing alleles. This property of genetic recombination improves the quality of mutagenesis for the following reason. Most newly produced mutations are either deleterious or neutral; very few are selectively advantageous, i.e. adaptive. Our estimates for bacteria are that the former are about 10.000-fold more frequent than the latter (14). For more complex genomes this ratio is even higher, for simpler genomes it is lower This is because mutations are Darwinian, i.e., random, rather than Lamarckian, i.e., targeted to the gene under selectire pressure. # Evolution of Iproved Genes in the Test Tube

Stemmer, Arnold and colleagues invented an ingenious method for in vitro evolution of "improved" genes called the "sexual PCR" or the "molecular breeding" (13, 18). All genes in the living organisms have evolved towards an optimum in the context of the homeostasis of the cel, rather than to a maximum activity, or efficiency, as we may wish it in an industrial, biotech, context. Sexual PCR shows the value of recombination in adaptive evolution of genes. Genes are highly mutagenized by the error-prone PCR conditions, then cloned and expressed in bacteria and finally tested for improved or modified activity. Little success can be obtained for the reasons discussed above: rare "improving" mutations will be accompanied by the excess of deleterious mutations in the same gene. The practise of sexual PCR involves nicking of the mutated PCR products by DNasel, denaturing and reannecaling, then repairing by DNA polymerase before cloning and testing. The result is that the mutations have been hyper-recombined or shuffled by this procedure. The testing revels a remarkable success in detecting copies with greatly modified activity. A 30.000-fold increase in enzymatic activity and a change in enzymatic specificity have been achieved. Even a back-cross", like in plant breeding, is of great value: PCR crossing the pool of highly mutated gene copies with the initial gene copy allows to separate the rare advantageeous" mutation from frequent deleterious mutations (18). This is how individual deleterious, neutral and advantageous mutations can be identified (18).

Thus, recombination - by sorting mutations - has iproved the practical quality of random mutagenesis. It may be less than coincidental that often mutagenesis and recombination are co-controlled in bacteria: e.g. SOS induction and mismatch repair deficiency (8).

# Summary and Conclusions

Living organisms are highly organized adaptive systems. Whereas the maintenance of organization requires conservation, adaptation requires variation, i.e., genetic change. Thus, the perpetuation of Life depends on the fine tuning of the dialectic: conservation versus change (Figure 1). Recent evolutionary experiments with bacteria suggest that there are several mechanisms which assure high mutation rates when life is at stake. Some are in the realm of molecular biology, such as the inducible mutator systems affecting potentially every stressed cell. Others are in the realm of population genetics and appear to tune mutation frequencies of large bacterial population by selection of cells which bear a genetic defect conferring to them a constitutive mutator phenotype. Such mutators, although selectively disadvantadeous because of their high random mutation load, are frequently present in natural populations (7). Mutators appear initially by random mutation in the so-called mutator genes and then they can be selected for indirectly, e.g., because of their propensity to generate all kinds of mutations including rare adaptive mutations. In cells bearing advantageous mutation created by the mutator effect, the mutator mutation will "hitch-hike" with the adaptive mutation until it will create a deleterious mutation (1). Thus, following adaptation, the fitness of mutators will keep decreasing due to the accumulation of deleterious mutations. Hence the rise and the fall of mutators in natural and experimental populations of bacteria.

En lieu of an epilogue, mutators appear to be shortterm winners and long-term looser. They win here and now but loose elsewhere and laters.
