# Introduction

It is generally assumed that the decrease in recombination with decreasing homology between recombining DNAs is caused by the inhibitory effect of sequence heterology on the strand exchange process; thus, heterologous DNAs do not recombine. But how do the recombination enzymes know what is the degree of homology between two DNA sequences? A "search for homology" identified in vitro apparently involves attempted strand exchange by the Escherichia coli RecA protein (Gonda and Radding 1983). There is ample evidence that once initiated, the process of asymmetric single-strand transfer from one molecule to another is notoriously insensitive to base pair mismatches (Das Gupta and Radding 1982; Gonda and Radding 1983). RecA-catalysed strand exchange in vitro still occurs with as much as $30 \%$ of dispersed base-substitution differences (Das Gupta and Radding 1982). Heteroduplex structures with large single-stranded loops resulting from RecA-dependent strand exchange across a kilobase of deletion -—insertion heterology are generated in vivo (Lichten and Fox 1984).

In a recent review on the effects of mismatch repair systems on genetic recombination, I postulated that the general mismatch repair system "proofreads" heteroduplex DNA as it is generated by strand exchange by aborting those heteroduplex intermediates that contain well-recognized mismatches (Radman 1988). This short review presents only the key evidence for the basic features of the proposed model and some of its immediate implications for chromosome stability, speciation, and evolution. An extensive review was presented elsewhere (Radman 1988).

# Mutants deficient in mismatch repair

Mutants deficient in mismatch repair have been well characterized in the bacteria $E$ coli, Salmonella typhimurium, and Streptococcus pneumoniae (reviewed by Claverys and Lacks 1986; Radman and Wagner 1986; Modrich 1987; Meselson 1988). Escherichia coli and S. typhimurium mutants mutH, mutL, mutS, and mutU (same as uvrD, uvrE, and recL), and S. pneumoniae mutants hexA and hexB all show increased spontaneous mutation and recombination rates (mutator and hyper-rec phenotypes, respectively). In addition, the four E. coli mut mutants show the Tex (transposon excision) phenotype reflecting increased rates of spontaneous precise recombination between very short repeated sequences flanking integrated Tn10 and Tn5 transposons, thus leading to precise transposon excisions (Lundblad and Kleckner 1985). These phenotypes imply that the functioning MutHLSU and HexAB systems act to prevent spontaneous mutagenesis and recombination. Yeast pms mutants with an apparent defect in mismatch repair (Bishop et al. 1987) also show a moderate mutator effect and an increased rate of strand exchange events (5:3 segregations) in meiosis (Williamson et al. 1985). The yeast Rad3 gene resembles the E. coli mutU (uvrE, uvrD) gene in many respects: the mutants are defective in excision repair, some mutants (e.g., reml) are mutators and hyper-rec, and both genes code for an ATPase —DNA helicase activity (Golin and Esposito 1977; Sung et al., 1987). Since the involvement of mismatch repair in correction of replication errors has been extensively reviewed (see Radman and Wagner 1986; Modrich 1987). I summarize here evidence for the prevention of recombination (antirecombination) by the MutHLSU and HexAB systems and propose molecular mechanisms and genetic ramifications of such enzymatic activity. In addition, a summary of the recently discovered specialized very short patch mismatch repair (VSPMR) systems is presented along with their hyper-recombination effects. The patch size of the resynthesized strand following displacement or excision of one strand in the course of mismatch repair determines the effect of mismatch repair in recombination; if heteroduplex DNA contains two or more mismatched genetic markers, the long-patch mismatch repair (LPMR) will tend to destroy the heteroduplex region and thus prevent exchange between the markers that could occur by localized mismatched repair or by recombination following replication of the heteroduplex. In contrast, VSPMR will generate clustered multiple exchanges between markers by generating patchwork sequences in heteroduplex DNA regions (see Fig. 1). Therefore, when discussing effects of mismatch repair systems in genetic recombination, it is useful to classify them into LPMR and VSPMR systems whenever applicable.

# Antirecombination by long-patch mismatch repair

LPMR in $E .$ coli requires either unmethylated ${ \pmb 5 } ^ { \prime }$ -GATC $. 3 ^ { \prime }$ sequences (Langle-Rouault et al. 1986; Lahue et al. 1987) or nicks (single-strand interruptions) (Langle-Rouault et al. 1987) for its successful operation. The requirement for unmethylated GATC sequences accounts for the activity of LPMR on the newly synthesized, transiently undermethylated DNA strands and therefore for correction of replication errors in $E .$ coli. Long excision -resynthesis tracts (up to several kilobases) are hallmarks of both MutHLSU and HexAB systems (reviews Claverys et al. 1986; Radman and Wagner 1986; Modrich 1987). The role of individual Mut proteins in E. coli LPMR is now becoming understood (Modrich 1987).

Since S. pneumoniae lacks the methylation system for GATC sequences, it was proposed that transiently unligated ends flanking the mismatch in heteroduplex DNA may strand direct the HexAB LPMR system (Guild and Shoemaker 1976; reviewed by Claverys and Lacks 1986). Such an effect of nicks on E. coli LPMR was demonstrated both in vivo (LangleRouault et al. 1987) and in vitro (Su et al. 1989) and presents a key feature of LPMR for understanding its effect on genetic recombination. The requirement for the MutU protein (which is the E. coli DNA helicase II) in LPMR suggested that the LPMR system could abort or decompose the heteroduplex DNA (Radman 1988) as illustrated in Fig. 1 (see the legend). In view of the proposed hypothesis, the hyper-rec effect of E. coli mutants deficient in mismatch repair, both in intrachromosomal $l a c Z \times l a c Z$ crosses (Konrad 1977) and in interchromosomal (conjugational) lacZ $\times$ lacZ crosses (Feinstein and Low 1986), is observed only because genetic markers are used to measure recombination. These genetic markers (and perhaps also some genetically silent sequence difference in the crossing region) generate mismatches in the course of DNA strand exchange which can be detected by the mismatch repair enzymes. In other words, mismatch repair would have no effect on recombination between perfectly homologous DNA sequences. This has been shown recently: integration of a plasmid into phage $\lambda$ DNA via a homology provided by a short cloned sequence (mouse $\mathbf { V } _ { \mathrm { H } }$ immunoglobulin genes) (Shen and Huang 1986) was not affected by the $E .$ coli mutS mutation when the homology was $100 \%$ ; however, the frequency of recombination decreased drastically in $m u t ^ { + }$ , but only moderately in mutS bacteria, as the homology decreased from 100 to about $80 \%$ (Hollifield 1987. This result directly supports the notion that, in the absence of LPMR, strand exchange itself is a low-fidelity process and that the LPMR system acts to abort mismatch-bearing heteroduplex DNA (Radman 1988 and Fig. 1) or perhaps to inactivate heteroduplex recombinants (see Doutriaux et al. 1986).

These conclusions could also have been reached on the bases of studies with S. pneumoniae where the HexAB system aborts the entire integrated single strand of over $5 \ \mathbf { k b }$ because of a single base-pair mismatch caused by the genetic marker (Mejean and Claverys 1984). Recombination frequencies with markers generating unrecognized mismatches or with any markers in hexAB mutants, indicate that the efficiency of antirecombination by the HexAB LPMR can be up to $9 9 \%$ per single well-recognized mismatch (reviewed by Claverys and Lacks 1986; Radman 1988). Because pneumococcal genetic transformation measures gene conversion involving singlestranded DNA patches, whereas the plasmid-phage $\lambda$ cointegration system measures only crossovers involving double-strand splices (Hollifield 1987), the reviewed work shows that both fundamental recombination events are subject to LPMR-mediated mismatch-stimulated antirecombination. By decomposing mismatched heteroduplexes, LPMR appears to act as a proofreading system in genetic recombination assuring its fidelity with respect to homology of DNA sequences involved in strand exchange.

The principal obstacle to an earlier recognition of this pattern was that the preferred system to study genetic recombination in E. coli, the bacteriophage $\lambda$ , was not significantly affected by LPMR, either in DNA replication (Caillet-Fauquet et al. 1984) or in recombination (Gussin et al. 1981; Lieb 1981; reviewed by Radman 1988). And yet, experiments with heteroduplex DNA of phage $\lambda$ provided the best evidence for mismatch repair in $E .$ coli (reviewed by Radman and Wagner 1986; Meselson 1988). This paradox is probably caused by the limited availability of mismatch repair enzymes, only a few molecules and presumably concentrated around cellular DNA (Radman and Wagner 1986). These can act on long-lived heteroduplexes in transfection experiments but not on rapidly replicated and packaged lytic cycle λ DNA. Jones et al. (1987a) have provided the most explicit evidence for the LPMR and VSPMR effects on genetic recombination (Fig. 1) using phage $\lambda$ (see also Raposa and Fox 1987).

It is possible that mismatch repair also acts preferentially on chromosomal DNA, as compared to exogenous plasmid DNA, in mammalian cells. Differential effects of base-pair mismatches on intrachromosomal versus extrachromosomal recombination of the same sequences have also been observed in mouse cells; the decrease in homology between two tk genes from 100 to $81 \%$ decreased their intrachromosomal recombination 1000-fold and their extrachromosomal recombination only 3- to 15-fold (Waldman and Liskay 1987). Thus, the fidelity of intrachromosomal recombination is also higher than that of extrachromosomal recombination in mammalian cells, perhaps because of mismatch repair.

# LPMR "proofreading" in recombination suggests molecular mechanisms for chromosomal stability and evolution of species

Mismatch-stimulated antirecombination by LPMR, as illustrated in Fig. 1, could prevent recombination involving repetitive (or simply repeated) sequences that would generate diverse chromosomal aberrations such as deletions, insertions, inversions, and translocations. Single base substitutions in deletion-prone short repeated sequences in $E .$ coli drastically decrease the frequency of deletions (Albertini et al. 1984), whereas the MutU mutation increases the frequency of the same deletion event (J. H. Miller, personal communication). The MutU (helicase II) function(s), and somewhat less the MutL, S, and H functions, apparently prevent precise transposon excision occurring by recombination between short terminal repeats generated by transposon insertion events (Lundblad and Kleckner 1985). This effect of helicaseII (but not of other Mut functions) is particularly strong in an experimental system involving single-stranded DNA and, apparently, a copy-choice mechanism of transposon excision (Brunier et al., 1989). This discrepancy, along with the observation that mut $U$ mutants show significantly stronger hyper-rec effects in $E ,$ coli Hfr $\times \mathbf { F } ^ { - }$ and $\mathbf { F } ^ { \prime } \times \mathbf { F } ^ { - }$ crosses (Feinstein and Low 1986) and Tex (transposon excision) effects than other mut mutants, suggests that helicase II may be antirecombinagenic even in the absence of mismatches (e.g., by its entry into DNA duplex at free ends or nicks).

Sequence polymorphism of the principal families of dispersed repeated sequences in mammalian cells (e.g., AluI and KpnI) is high enough $( 7 0 - 9 0 \%$ homology Karija et al. 1987) to assure numerous mismatches in any heteroduplex involving such sequences (except for precise sister exchange) and therefore to assume efficient antirecombination by LPMR. Indeed if the rarity of genetically harmful "illegitimate" recombination among intersperced repeated sequences is largely due to the activity of the LPMR system, then the astonishing regularity of their occurrence throughout the genome suggests that the "junk" or "selfish" DNA (Orgel and Crick 1980; Doolittle and Sapienza 1980) may serve to prevent mitotic (and diminish meiotic?) recombination. Thus, instead of the popular view that repetitive DNA sequences promote genetic diversification via chromosomal rearrangements (e.g., Alberts et al. 1983), their primary selective advantage may be to prevent recombination, i.e., to maintain chromosomal stability. Rare chromosomal rearrangements involving such sequences may represent occasional failures of LPMR to abort recombination or the failure of some other mechanisms that also prevent recombination between repetitive sequences (see Rothstein et al. 1987; Radman 1988). These rare DNA rearrangements may represent a relatively low price paid for the gain in chromosomal stability and for the evolutionary potential discussed below.

Divergence in intron sequences may also serve to prevent intragenic recombination. Clearly, only precise sister chromatid exchange (SCE) would generate mismatch-free heteroduplexes and thus would not be prevented by LPMR. This would allow for frequent SCE, which is presumably useful for DNA repair (reviewed by Radman 1988) but make harmful mitotic recombination (viz. homozygosis in cancer and disease) very infrequent. The degree of sequence polymorphism in human populations (about $0 . 1 - 0 . { \bar { 3 } } \% )$ could be adequate for the prevention of mitotic recombination between maternal and paternal chromosomes in diploid human cells.

The proposed system for the maintenance of high fidelity of homologous recombination by the mismatch repair helicase activity would be most economical if the diversified extragenic sequences were also recombination initiation sites; except for SCEs, initiated strand exchanges would be readily aborted by mismatch repair enzymes (rather than propagated and aborted by helicase-mediated melting of long heteroduplex DNA). The gradient pattern of meiotic recombination frequencies in many genes of diverse fungal species (the "polaron," reviewed by Whitehouse 1982) indeed suggests that the strand exchange process may usually initiate in the extragenic regions (see also Thuriaux 1977). Strand exchange initiating in diversified extragenic sequences would neatly satisfy the requirement for recombinational fidelity, namely, avoidance of interspecific recombination, mitotic recombination, and chromosomal rearrangements. Such a role for diversified extragenic sequences would account for species-specific sequence motifs of repetitive sequences (permitting homospecific meiotic recombination) and for the sequence polymorphism of the basic repetitive motif within the genome (preventing mitotic recombination and rearrangements). Consequently, one would have to postulate that LPMR activity is less stringent in meiosis (as reflected in the relatively high fraction of 5:3 segregation events in fungi) than in mitosis (as reflected in the quasi absence of mitotic recombination in mammalian cells).

Frequency of recombination in meiosis is finely tuned: meiotic recombination is several orders of magnitude more frequent than mitotic recombination, and its frequency per unit length of DNA decreases proportionally with increasing genome size (Thuriaux 1977). It is not known whether this evolutionary adaptation of recombination frequencies in meiosis correlates with the amount and (or) distribution of diversified non coding DNA sequences. The proposed hypothesis predicts an inverse relationship between recombination and amount of sequence polymorphism.

As for the relationship between sequence divergence and recombination, it is well known from yeast and plant genetics that homeologous chromosomes (i.e., chromosomes from related species coding for the same functions but sharing only about $80 - 9 0 \%$ homology) do not recombine in spite of millions of homologous base pairs, whereas a couple of kilobases of perfect homology will recombine even when embedded in nonhomologous chromosomes ("ectopic recombination') (Jinks-Robertson and Petes 1985; Lichten et al. 1987). I suggest that this paradox can be resolved for by mismatchstimulated antirecombination preventing homeologous recombination between chromosomes from different species (just as discussed for repetitive sequences that bear similar degree o divergence). Were this the case, then mismatch-stimulated antirecombination by LPMR can be expected to initiate and (or) accelerate speciation upon a preceding sequence diversification of newly arising species. Effective prevention of meiotic recombination, and therefore sterility caused by meiotic chromosomal nondisjuction, would be determined by two interdependent factors: the quantity, nature, and distribution of the sequence divergence, and the intrinsic activity of the LPMR system. The prediction that LPMR is the primary causo steriliy ninterspecific and intergeneic crosses was tested in bacterial crosses (C. Rayssiguier and M. Radman, unpublished). The vast majority of genes of the two bacterial genera Salmonella and Escherichia are functionally equivalent (mutations in one bacterium can be complemented by the respective wild type genes from the other), and yet they usually share only 75 to $90 \%$ homology (Ochman and Wilson 1987). Thus, Escherichia and Salmonella chromosomes are homeologous. Indeed, conjugational crosses between Salmonella and Escherichia are sterile, recombination being absent or very low depending on the locus analysed (Baron et al. 1959; Eisenstark 1965; Mergeay and Gerits 1983). The prediction that mismatch repair deficiency may activate intergeneric recombination was fulfilled; when the restriction-deficient $\mathbf { F } ^ { - }$ Salmonella typhimurium recipient of Escherichia coli Hfr DNA is also deficient in mismatch repair, the frequency of intergeneric recombinants increases by $1 0 ^ { { \bar { 3 } } . }$ to $1 0 ^ { 6 } \cdot$ fold (C. Rayssiguier and M. Radman, unpublished results).

# Hyper-recombination by VSPMR

Figure 1 shows that heteroduplex DNA that escapes LPMR can be further hyper-recombined by the VSPMR process, thus generating a patchwork of parental sequences on the repaired strand. This has been most directly demonstrated in parallel experiments with heteroduplex DNA and genetic crosses (Jones et al. 1987). Results from other laboratories support the proposed scheme (reviewed by Radman 1988). The first VSPMR was postulated by Lieb (1983) and demonstrated in several laboratories (Jones et al. $1 9 8 7 a$ $1 9 8 7 b$ ; Lieb 1987; Zell and Fritz 1987). This system specifically repairs a G:T mismatch to G:C when the G:T occurs in an $E .$ coli C (A or T) AGG sequence at the two positions where 5-methylcytosine (5meC) is found, i.e., sites CC (A or T) GG where $\mathrm { 5 m e C }  \mathrm { T }$ deamination would generate a G:T mismatch (see Lieb 1983; Jones et al. 1987b; Zell and Fritz 1987). Thus, this VSPMR would counteract the 5meC - T deamination events by the repair back to G:C.

More VSPMR systems are being discovered; two others also unidirectionally repair a specific mismatch to a specific match (Lu et al. 1989; P. Modrich, personal communication; M. Fox, personal communication). Whether these new VSPMR systems act only to repair the "leftovers" of LPMR following replication to conserve some "privileged"" short sequences or to generate diversity in the course of genetic recombination remains to be explored.

In conclusion, in the course of genetic recombination, mismatch repair systems (LPMR and VSPMR) can conserve (antirecombination) and diversify (hyper-recombination) genetic information by virtue of their respective repair patch size (Fig. 1).
