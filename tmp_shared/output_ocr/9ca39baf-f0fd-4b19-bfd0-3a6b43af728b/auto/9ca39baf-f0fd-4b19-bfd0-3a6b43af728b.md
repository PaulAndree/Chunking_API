# Involvement of Escherichia coli Mismatch Repair in DNA Replication and Recombination

R. WAGNER, C. DOHET, M. JONES, M.-P. DOUTRIAUX, F. HUTCHINSON,\* AND M. RADMAN Laboratre  Mutagéèe, Istu Jacques MonoRUniversi ar 5251 ar Cedex 5, \*Department of Molecular Biophysics and Biochemistry, Yale University, New Haven, Connecticut, 06511

The idea that there might be a process capable of recognizing and repairing incorrect base pairs in double-stranded DNA (mismatch repair) was originally proposed to account for results obtained from the study of genetic recombination. In particular, it was suggested that the repair of mismatched bases in heteroduplex regions of DNA (i.e., regions formed by strand exchange between DNA molecules with similar but not identical sequences) could be involved in gene conversion (Holliday 1964) and could account for the phenomenon of high negative interference (White and Fox 1974). That mismatch repair in $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli might be involved in correcting replication errors was suggested by the finding that mutants deficient in mismatch repair have a mutator phenotype (Nevers and Spatz 1975; Rydberg 1978; Glickman and Radman 1980). Perhaps to a large extent because of its apparent involvement in mutation avoidance, mismatch repair has recently been widely studied. We will describe here much of what is known of Escherichia coli mismatch repair and discuss how the characteristics of the process may affect its role in genetic recombination.

# Correction of Replication Errors

For a process of mismatch repair to be able to correct replication errors, it must be able to distinguish newly synthesized strands from parental strands and must selectively preserve the sequence of the latter. In E. coli, a means of strand discrimination for the mismatch repair system is provided by adenine methylation in GATC sequences (Wagner and Meselson 1976; Radman et al. 1980; Pukkila et al. 1983). Mismatch repair is greatly reduced in regions of DNA in which GATC sequences are fully adenine methylated (Pukkila et al. 1983) and repair occurs preferentially, if not exclusively, on unmethylated strands of hemimethylated heteroduplexes (Radman et al. 1980; Pukkila et al. 1983). Because newly synthesized strands are transiently undermethylated, i.e., methylation lags somewhat behind replication, mismatch repair in E. coli is believed to occur primarily on newly synthesized strands immediately behind the replication fork.

It seems clear that fully methylated GATC sequences inhibit mismatch repair. Although it is possible that unmethylated GATC sequences function as binding sites or activating sites for mismatch repair enzymes, it is not clear that mismatch repair requires unmethylated GATC sequences. In fact, there is preliminary evidence suggesting that unmethylated GATC sequences may not be required. Mismatch repair of fully methylated heteroduplexes is reduced relative to repair of unmethylated heteroduplexes, but not to the same level as observed in mutL bacteria, which are deficient in mismatch repair (Table 1). Therefore, it may be only the rather close spacing of inhibitory (methylated) GATC sequences that keeps mismatch repair of methylated DNA from being completely normal. If so, then a mismatch closely flanked by methylated GATC sequences should be refractory to repair. Alternatively, unmethylated GATC sequences may be necessary for full activation of the mismatch repair system such that any repair detected in fully methylated DNA may be some residual activity of the unactivated repair enzymes. However, it has not been possible to demonstrate the necessity for unmethylated GATC sequences (G. Maenhaut-Michel et al., unpubl.) or to show that the distance from a mismatch to unmethylated (Table 2), or hemimethylated (not shown), GATC sequences has any effect on the extent of repair of the mismatch, as might be expected if GATC sequences were binding or activating sites for mismatch repair enzymes. (However, it may be that the distance between the GATC sequences in the region studied [ca. 750 bp] was too short to allow the detection of such an effect.)

# Mismatch Repair and Recombination

If mismatch repair can occur, albeit to a limited extent, even in fully methylated DNA, then the process may contribute to the recombination of close markers even in fully methylated DNA. In fact, limited mismatch repair may be more effective in recombining close markers than fully active mismatch repair which, at least under some conditions, apparently involves long excision tracts (ca. 1000-3000 bases) (Wagner and Meselson 1976; Lu et al. 1983) which could include more than one mismatch (co-repair) and, thus, make it difficult to produce a recombinant genotype in heteroduplex DNA. The ambivalent role of mismatch repair in recombination is perhaps most clearly illustrated in the results of experiments measuring recombination frequencies for various pairs of close markers in wild-type and mismatch repair-deficient (mutH, mutL, mutS) bacteria. For a given pair of markers, some mut mutations increase the recombination frequency relative to wild type, whereas others decrease it (Glickman and Radman 1980). In addition, the effect of a given mut mutation is not the same for every pair of markers (M.F. Bourguignon-Van Horen and M. Radman, unpubl.).

# Specificity of Mismatch Repair

It has recently been shown that not all mismatched base pairs are recognized and/or repaired with equal efficiency. The transition mismatches (G: T and A:C) appear to be efficiently repaired, whereas three of the six transversion mismatches (G: A, C: T, and C:C) are not (Dohet et al. 1984; Table 3). In addition, it may be that the extent of repair of at least some of the transversion mismatches is affected by neighboring nucleotide sequence, since the same transversion mismatch (G:A or C:T) is repaired to a somewhat different extent in three different positions (not shown). In general, transition mismatches appear to be repaired with the same high efficiency regardless of location (Table 2). The situation for frameshift/wild-type heteroduplexes is somewhat more complex. In unmethylated DNA, repair of heteroduplexes with a single unpaired base (i.e., one lacking the corresponding base on the complementary strand) is relatively efficient (Table 3). In most cases, the repair occurs by insertion of the complementary base into the shorter strand. If only the strand without the unpaired base (i.e., the shorter strand) is methylated, repair is reduced (Table 4). Therefore, if hemimethylation mimics the situation at the replication fork, it appears that the $\pmb { E }$ coli mismatch repair system is more efficient in repairing deletion frameshifts than insertion frameshifts.

The $E .$ coli mismatch repair system does not appear to act on large unpaired regions. Unmethylated heteroduplexes formed between wild-type strands of λ DNA and strands prepared from phages with an insertion (IS1 sequence) of about 800 bp in the cI gene do not appear to be repaired on either strand by the mut-dependent mismatch repair system (Table 3). There is, however, a suggestion from the data that some system may repair such loops, perhaps by preferentially cutting the loop out of the DNA.

The fact that the $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli mismatch repair system appears to recognize most effectively transition mismatches and $( - 1 )$ deletion frameshifts accounts for the finding that the mutator effects observed in mutH, mutL, mutS, and mutU mutants, which are deficient in mismatch repair, and dam mutants, which have undirected mismatch repair, are due primarily to an increase in transition and deletion frameshift mutations (for review, see Cox 1976; Glickman 1979). The pattern of mismatch repair and the mutator character of the mismatch repair-deficient mutants suggest that, in $E _ { i }$ coli, transversion mismatches and addition frameshifts either arise much less frequently during DNA replication than transition mismatches and deletion frameshifts or are corrected by some repair system that is not detected in our experimental system, perhaps because it is replication dependent.

The difference in repairability of different mismatches can account for the inconsistent effect of mismatch repair-deficient mutants on recombination frequencies. For example, a pair of markers, both of which give poorly repaired mismatches in mutant/wildtype heteroduplexes, might be expected to show reduced recombination under mismatch repair-deficient conditions. In contrast, a pair of markers, both of which give well-repaired mismatches that are most often repaired in one event on a single strand (co-repair), might show increased recombination under conditions where mismatch repair is less efficient. In addition, markers may behave differently when crossed with markers of different repair characteristics. For example, a marker giving well-repaired mismatches may recombine poorly with a similarly repaired marker (co-repair) and may recombine more frequently with a marker giving poorly repaired mismatches.

It does not appear that all types of markers can be accounted for in the studies discussed above. There exist markers that recombine much more than predicted with all other close markers tested (Lieb 1983).

# Undirected Mismatch Repair and Recombination

dam mutants (deficient in adenine methylation and, therefore, having undirected mismatch repair) are more sensitive than wild-type $E .$ coli to base analogs, ethylmethanesulfonate (EMS), $N .$ -methyl $. N .$ -nitro $. N .$ . nitrosoguanidine (MNNG), methylmethanesulfonate (MMS), and UV. In addition, dam recA, dam recB, dam lexA, and dam polA double mutants are inviable (for review, see Radman and Wagner 1984). Because these sensitivities and the lethality of the double mutants can, in many cases, be alleviated by the addition of a mutation affecting mismatch repair (mutH, mutL, mutS, mutU), it has been suggested that mismatch repair may act on both strands of unmethylated DNA to produce double-strand breaks (Glickman and Radman 1980). (The repair of double-strand breaks in E. coli has been shown to be an SOS-inducible function, i.e., one that requires gene products controlled by the LexA repressor [Krasin and Hutchinson 1981], which would account for the lethality of the dam recA and dam lexA double mutants). When a mixture of genetically distinguishable unmethylated DNA molecules with and without mismatches is used to transfect wild-type and mismatch repair-deficient (mut) bacteria, the transfection efficiency of DNA containing mismatches, relative to DNA without mismatches, is lower in wild-type than in mut bacteria (Table 5). Relative transfection efficiencies are identical when DNA containing mismatches is hemimethylated or when the mismatch is nonrepairable. These results indicate that undirected mismatch repair can be lethal. The fact that a single mismatch appears to be sufficient to cause killing suggests that the mechanism of lethality may involve simultaneous, or nearly simultaneous, attack on both strands at a single mismatch to produce a doublestrand break.

dam mutants show increased recombination frequencies (Marinus and Konrad 1976) which may be the result of having frequent recombinogenic lesions, i.e., double-strand breaks, in their DNA. In bacteriophage recombination, mismatches in unmethylated DNA, or unmethylated regions of DNA, could be sites of double-strand breaks which in turn could provoke further exchanges and, thus, account for the phenomenon of high negative interference.

In eukaryotes, DNA adenine methylation is virtually nonexistent and, therefore, unlikely to direct mismatch repair. If $E .$ coli-like mismatch repair enzymes are present in eukaryotic cells, it may be that repairable mismatches provoke double-strand breaks, which, at least in yeast and mammalian cells, can be efficiently repaired by means of recombination with an intact homologous DNA molecule (see Rothstein; Lin et al.; both this volume). If mismatches in the DNA of higher organisms provoke double-strand breaks, recombination between sister chromatids to restore parental sequences would have the same effect on mutation avoidance in eukaryotes as methyl-directed mismatch repair does in $E$ coli. In such a situation, sister chromatid exchanges may reflect those mismatch repair events that terminate with crossing-over.
