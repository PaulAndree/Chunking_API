# Summary

The involvement of the E. coli methyl-directed and very short patch (vsp) mismatch repair systems in bacteriophage $\gimel$ recombination has been studied. Genetic crosses and heteroduplex transfection experiments were performed using A phages with sequenced mutations in the c/ gene. The results indicate that methyldirected repair does operate during bacteriophage λ recombination but generally does not contribute to the formation of recombinants involving close markers. Vsp repair apparently acts during bacteriophage λrecombination to produce recombinants involving close markers because its action does not involve extensive excision tracts. Marker-specific hyperrecombination and the apparent clustering of genetic exchanges in bacteriophage $\gimel$ recombination can be accounted for by the action of the vsp repair system.

# Introduction

The idea that there might bea process capable of rnizing and repairing incorrect base pairs in doublestranded DNA (mismatch repair) was originally proposed to account for results obtained from the study of genetic recombination (Holliday, 1964). In particular, it was suggested that the repair of mismatched bases in heteroduplex regions of DNA (i.e., regions formed by strand exchange between DNA molecules with similar, but not identical, sequences) could be involved in gene conversion and could contribute to the phenomenon of high negative interference (Whitehouse, 1965; Fogel and Mortimer, 1969; Leblon and Rossignol, 1973; White and Fox, 1974; Wildenberg and Meselson, 1975; Radman et al., 1978). More recently, the results of crosses between relatively close markers in bacteriophage λ have indicated that recombination frequency is generally proportional to the number of nucleotides separating the markers (Gussin et al., 1981; Lieb, 1981). This dependence on separation and the fact that recombinant genotypes formally requiring three crossover events arise at much lower frequencies than those requiring only a single event have led to the conclusion that mismatch repair does not significantly contribute to the formation of recombinants in crosses involving close markers in bacteriophage $\gimel$ (Gussin et al., 1981; Lieb, 1981).

However, markers exist that do not behave normally in crosses with close markers, but rather give excess recombinants (Lieb, 1983). These markers have been shown to result from $0 : 0  \tilde { \Gamma } : \mathsf { A }$ transitions in the sequences CAGG→TAGG and CCAG→CTAG (Lieb et al., 1986). It was suggested that these markers do not increase the number of recombination events that terminate between them and other markers, as they might by stimulating the initiation of recombination events or by blocking the formation of hybrid DNA. Rather, it was proposed that mismatches formed in hybrid DNA between these markers and their wild-type alleles are substrates for a specialized form of mismatch repair that is capable of acting in recombining DNA to create recombinant genotypes from recombinational intermediates not normally yielding recombinant genotypes, namely, those in which both markers of a pair are included in hybrid DNA. Because recombinant frequency is increased even when the markers are separated by as few as ten base pairs, it was proposed that the repair process involves short excision tracts. This proposed repair has come to be known as very short patch (vsp) repair (Lieb, 1983).

Although vsp repair can account for the cross data, an alternative explanation is possible. While conventional methyl-directed mismatch repair may not normally contribute to the formation of recombinant genotypes for close markers, it may, nonetheless, operate in heteroduplex DNA formed during bacteriophage $\gimel$ recombination. Such operation would not be detectable in crosses if the normal excision tract invariably includes both mismatches of a reasonably close pair. In other words, no mismatch repair and active mismatch repair with extensive excision tracts both require that recombinants be produced by events terminating between markers, i.e., events not including both markers in hybrid DNA. Thus, markers that, when included in hybrid DNA, form mismatches that block excision tracts of the mismatch repair system would be hyperrecombinogenic and could be expected to exhibit all of the characteristics of the markers proposed to be involved in vsp repair.

These two alternatives have been distinguished by the results of a series of transfection experiments using λ DNA heteroduplexes prepared by annealing DNA from phages with one of the hyperrecombinogenic markers with DNA from phages with its wild-type allele. Two heteroduplexes are formed by such annealing, one with a G:T mismatch and the other with an A:C mismatch. It was found that heteroduplexes with the G:T mismatch can be repaired even under conditions (fully adenine-methylated DNA) where methyl-directed mismatch repair does not normally operate (Jones et al., 1987). The repair requires a functional dcm (E. coli cytosine methylase) gene and some, but not all, of the functions required for methyldirected repair, and acts to restore the G:C base pair by replacing the thymine in the G:T mismatch (Jones et al., 1987). The repair (presumably vsp repair) is sequence (CAGG) specific, position specific (only mismatches adjacent to the A:T base pair of the sequence are recognized), mismatch (G:T) specific, and base (T) specific, and is apparently responsible for repairing deaminated 5-methyl cytosines (Jones et al., 1987).

# Results and Discussion

It is generally assumed that vsp repair increases recombination frequencies by repairing only one of the two mismatches formed when both markers of a pair are included in hybrid DNA (Lieb, 1983). In order to study most directly the effects of vsp repair on recombination, we have constructed heteroduplexes of bacteriophage $\gimel$ DNA that mimic the recombinational intermediate on which vsp repair is supposed to act. The heteroduplexes contain two mismatches, one formed by the c/857 mutation and its wild-type allele and one formed by the am6 mutation and its wild-type allele. The c/857 mutation is a temperaturesensitive mutation that results in clear plaques at $4 2 \circ \circ$ and turbid plaques at $3 2 \circ 0$ The am6 mutation is one of the hyperrecombinogenic markers discovered by Lieb (1983) and is suppressible by an E. coli suppressor such that it results in clear plaques under $\mathsf { s u } ^ { - }$ conditions and turbid plaques under ${ \mathfrak { s u } } ^ { + }$ conditions.

Transfected bacteria are plated before lysis to form infective centers and the genotypes of the individual phages determined by picking and replating. By replating on $\pmb { s u } ^ { - }$ bacteria at $3 2 \circ \mathtt { C }$ and ${ \mathfrak { s u } } ^ { + }$ bacteria at $\yen 200$ it is possible to detect unambiguously repair at any of the four mismatched bases in the heteroduplex (see Table 1).

The heteroduplex presented in Table 1 contains a G:T mismatch recognized by both the vsp and methyl-directed repair systems (position 100, am6: wild-type) and a G:T mismatch that is well repaired by the methyl-directed repair system (position 199, c/857: wild-type). Under conditions where both systems are operating (unmethylated heteroduplex in wild-type bacteria), the repair of both markers on the same strand appears to predominate. The fact that these classes of events virtually disappear when both the methyl-directed and vsp repair systems are inactivated (mutL bacteria) confirms that repair rather than strand loss or contamination of the heteroduplex preparations by homoduplexes is responsible for the results. The bias in favor of repair of the R strand persists in dcm bacteria, which are deficient in vsp repair but not methyldirected repair (Jones et al., 1987), suggesting that the bias observed in wild-type bacteria is not solely a result of the action of the vsp repair system (see Dohet et al., 1985, 1986).

When only vsp repair is fully functional (mutH bacteria) (Jones et al., 1987), the predominant repair event is the replacement of T by C in the G:T mismatch at position 100 (am6). The finding of this class of repair events clearly indicates that the majority of vsp repair events does not extend 100 nucleotides. However, the finding of a number of repair events apparently involving both mismatches may suggest that some vsp repair events do extend as far as 100 nucleotides. If so, then, assuming that vsp repair events terminate with some constant probability per nucleotide, closer mismatches should be more often involved in double repair events. However, no increase in the fraction of double repair events was observed when the mismatches were only 10 base pairs apart (Table 3). In addition, a similar fraction of double repair events was observed when the mismatch at position 100 was A:C, which is not recognized by the vsp repair system (Table 2). Thus, these experiments confirm that vsp repair events rarely, if ever, extend 10 nucleotides. It may be that the double repair events observed in mutH bacteria reflect some residual activity of the methyl-directed repair system. (Residual methyl-directed mismatch repair activity has been detected in mutH bacteria [Kramer et al., 1984; Längle-Rouault et al., 1986, 1987]).

Heteroduplexes were prepared with mismatches at positions 98 and 100 (am6). The results of transfections with these heteroduplexes reveal extensive repair of both mismatches (Table 3). It may be that vsp repair frequently involves excision tracts of two bases or more. However, the finding of a large "no repair" class may indicate that vsp repair does not operate efficiently on these heteroduplexes, perhaps because they normally contain a 3 base long region of single-stranded DNA formed as a result of having two mismatches separated by only a single base pair. The corepair observed may be a result of residual activity of the methyl-directed repair system (compare with Table 2). The normal action of the vsp repair system in double-stranded DNA may be to remove only the unpaired thymine in the G:T mismatch.

In an attempt to determine the relative efficiencies of the vsp and methyl-directed repair systems, heteroduplexes were prepared with one strand fully GATC-methylated and the other unmethylated (Table 4). When the am6 mutation is on the unmethylated strand, virtually all repair in wildtype bacteria occurs on that strand and involves repair of both mismatches. When the am6 mutation is on the methylated strand, the predominant repair event is still repair of both markers on the unmethylated strand, suggesting that, at least in transfections with hemimethylated heteroduplex DNA, methyl-directed repair acts more rapidly than vsp repair. Therefore, methyl-directed repair should be able to prevent vsp repair from fixing mutations when vsprepairable mismatches (G opposite template T) arise as replication errors.

A series of genetic crosses was performed to determine the extent to which heteroduplex processing of the type observed in transfection experiments is involved in bacteriophage $\gimel$ recombination and to determine the relative roles of the methyl-directed and vsp mismatch repair systems. The crosses involved phages carrying the markers used in the heteroduplex transfection studies and were performed basically as described by Lieb (1983). Phages also carried an amber mutation in either the N gene (Nam53) or the O gene (Oam29). (The N and O genes flank the c/ gene on the bacteriophage $\gimel$ genetic map.) $\ N ^ { + } \mathsf { O } ^ { + }$ recombinants were selected on $\mathtt { s u } ^ { - }$ bacteria and the fraction of ${ \tt c } ^ { + }$ recombinants among $\mathsf { N } ^ { + } \mathsf { O } ^ { + }$ recombinants was determined. c/ markers were arranged so as to require formally three crossover events to obtain $N ^ { + } C ^ { + } O ^ { + }$ recombinants (Lieb's Configuration Il crosses). The results are presented in Figure 1. All crosses involving am6, which can form a mismatch repairable by the vsp repair system (crosses 2, 3, and 4), give an increased fraction of ${ \mathfrak { c } } ^ { + }$ recombinants relative to control crosses not involving am6 (cross 1), regardless of marker separation. The increases observed in mutL, mutS, and dcm bacteria, which are deficient in vsp repair in heteroduplex transfection experiments (Tables 1 and 3), are smaller than those observed for crosses in those strains in which vsp repair is fully operational (wild-type, mutH, and mutU bacteria). The fact that there is any increase at all in mutL, mutS, and dcm bacteria may reflect the occurrence of some residual vsp repair or unidirectional excision in these mutants. The largest fraction of ${ \mathfrak { c } } ^ { + }$ recombinants is observed when crosses are performed in bacteria deficient in methyldirected repair, but proficient in vsp repair (mutH and mutU bacteria) (Jones et al., 1987).

Vsp repair clearly functions during bacteriophage $\gimel$ recombination. Because it acts only on a specific base (T) of a specific mismatch (G:T) in a specific sequence (CAGG or CTGG), and involves little, if any, excision, vsp repair can create recombinants between pairs of close markers when both are included in hybrid DNA (compare Tables 1 and 2). Therefore, vsp repair is a likely source of high negative interference in bacteriophage $\gimel$ crosses, i.e., the apparent clustering of recombination events (Amati and Meselson, 1965). Support for this idea comes from the finding that two of the most commonly used markers in studies of bacteriophage λ recombination, Pam3 and Pam80, can form mismatches with their wild-type alleles that should be recognized by the vsp repair system (Reiser, 1983) and behave similarly to clam6 in crosses with close markers; i.e., the frequency of $P ^ { + }$ recombinants is decreased in mutL and mutS strains and increased in mutH and mutU strains (Radman et al., 1978; Glickman and Radman, 1980). The results of Pam80 $\textit { \textbf { \textsf { f } } + }$ heteroduplex transfection experiments directly support this hypothesis (Meselson, 1987; S. Raposa and M. S. Fox, unpublished data). A similar phenomenon of hyperrecombination has been discovered in S. pneumonaie and has been shown to involve repair of a G:A mismatch at the fourth position of a ATTCAT sequence (Lefevre et al., 1984; Sicard et al., 1985).

Although there is no evidence from the data in Figure 1 that methyl-directed repair contributes to the formation of recombinants, the finding that inactivating the methyldirected repair system, but not the vsp repair system (mutH and mutU bacteria), increases the fraction of recombinants indicates that methyl-directed mismatch repair does operate on recombinational intermediates produced during bacteriophage λ recombination. The effect of methyl-directed repair appears to be to eliminate potential vsp-mediated recombinants, presumably by corepair of both markers on the same strand in hybrid DNA. Corepair by the methyl-directed repair system is sufficiently frequent in transfection experiments that, in the absence of vsp repair (dcm bacteria), no recombinant genotypes have been found (Tables 1 and 2).

It appears that using E. coli strains deficient in vsp repair (e.g., dcm strains) will virtually eliminate the hyperrecombinogenic effect of the mismatches recognized by the vsp repair system and the apparent clustering of genetic exchanges that results from their repair. Thus by performing crosses in vsp repair—deficient (e.g., dcm) bacteria it should be possible to map closely linked mutations more accurately than in wild-type bacteria.
