# Genetic requirements for hyper-recombination by very short patch mismatch repair: Involvement of Escherichia coli DNA polymerase I

Senka Dzidic \* and Miroslav Radman Institut Jacques Monod, Tour 43, 2 Place Jussieu, F-75251 Paris Cedex O5, France

Summary. It has been established that very short patch (VSP) mismatch repair, depending in Escherichia coli on MutL, MutS and Dcm functions, is responsible for the hyper-recombinogenic effect of a class of genetic markers. We show that VSP repair requires the presence of the complete DNA polymerase I enzyme. The absence of endonuclease activities involved in the repair of base-loss sites, Nth, Nfo and Xth, does not affect VSP repair. Implications for the mechanism of the VSP repair are discussed.

Key words: Escherichia coli  Bacteriophage λ  DNA polymerase — AP-endonuclease - Gene conversion

Mismatched base pairs (mismatches) arise in DNA in the course of at least three processes: (a) DNA replication (replication errors); (b) DNA recombination (formation of heteroduplex DNA by strand exchange between homologous but non-identical sequences); and (c) deamination of 5- methyl-cytosine (5-meC) to thymine yielding a G:T mismatch (reviews Claverys and Lacks 1986; Radman and Wagner 1986; Modrich 1987; Meselson 1988).

Two mismatch repair systems have been well characterized in Escherichia coli (Radman and Wagner 1986; Meselson 1988; Modrich 1987): (a) methyl-directed mismatch repair which, because it requires unmethylated GATC sequences, acts on newly synthesized strands to eliminate replication errors in wild-type $E$ coli; and (b) very short patch (VSP) mismatch repair which acts only on the G:T mismatch in defined positions $( ^ { * } )$ in specific sequences, i.e. ${ } ^ { * } \mathrm { C }$ A/T G G and $\mathbf { C } ^ { \mathrm { ~ * ~ } } \mathbf { C }$ A/T G (Lieb 1985; Lieb et al. 1986; Jones et al. 1987a, b; Meselson 1988; Zell and Fritz 1987). VSP repair acts unidirectionally, i.e. by the $\mathbf { G } ; \mathbf { T } $ G:C repair pathway (Jones et al. 1987 a, b; Meselson 1988), independently of any DNA methylation (i.e. it acts on nonreplicating DNA). Therefore, the genetic effect of the VSP repair is the prevention of mutations arising from $5 { \cdot } \mathrm { m e C } $ T deamination in the C C A/T G G sequence (Coulondre et al. 1978; Lieb 1985; Jones et al. 1987a, b; Zell and Fritz 1987) and hyper-recombination (i.e. excessively high recombination frequency) for genetic markers originating from $\mathbf { C } \to \mathbf { T }$ transition mutations: C A/T G $\mathbf { G }  \mathrm { T }$ A/T G G and C C A/T $\mathbf { G } \to \mathbf { C }$ T A/T G (Lieb et al. 1986; Jones et al. 1987a, b; Meselson 1988). The scheme in Fig. 1 illustrates the mechanism of the hyper-recombination effect of the VSP repair proposed by Lieb (1983) and demonstrated by Jones et al. (1987b). A very similar hyper-recombination mechanism was demonstrated in Streptococcus pneumoniae which, however, acts on a different mismatch (G:A) in a sequence (A T T A A T) which is not related to any known DNA modification (Sicard et al. 1985).

The aim of this work was to explore the genetic requirements for VSP repair other than the known MutL, MutS and Dcm functions (Jones et al. 1987a, b; Lieb 1987; Zell and Fritz 1987). Among the known DNA repair processes in E. coli, only the repair of base-loss sites (called AP-sites, for apurinic or apyrimidinic sites) acts by very short patch, i.e. repair tracts shorter than 10 bases (Kataoka and Sekiguchi 1982; Lindahl 1982; Friedberg 1985; Jones et al. 1987b).

There are several possibilities for repairing the G: T mismatch to the G:C pair at the site of 5-meC by removing few or no bases at all, e.g. (a) reamination of thymine to 5-meC; (b) demethylation of thymine to uracil allowing the uracil-N-glycosylase repair system to repair uracil via an AP-site; (c) a sequence-mismatch-and-base specific Nglycosylase which would remove the mismatched thymine creating an AP-site; or (d) some other enzymatic device. Hypothesis (a) has not been tested. In relation to hypothesis (b), we have shown that an ung mutant (deficient in uracilN-glycosylase) is fully proficient in VSP repair (M. Jones and M. Radman, unpublished experiments). Hypotheses (b), (c) and, perhaps, (d) leave an AP-site as intermediate in VSP repair, which, if repaired by the standard AP-site repair system, would require AP-endonucleases and DNA polymerase (Friedberg 1985; Kataoka and Sekiguchi 1982; Lindahl 1982). Therefore, we have tested $E$ coli mutants deficient in DNA polymerase I functions (either polymerase or $5 ^ { \prime } \to 3$ exonuclease) or in AP-endonuclease activities.

We conclude that either both DNA polymerase and $5 ^ { \prime }  3 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease functions of $E$ coli DNA polymerase I are required for VSP repair or that the DNA polymerase I enzyme is a structural component of the VSP mismatch repair complex (probably in addition to a functional involvement). As to the lack of any decrease of VSP repair in the nth and xth nfo mutants, it may be that VSP repair does not proceed via an AP-site intermediate, or that if it does, the repair of that specific AP-site is independent of general AP-site repair (or that the residual AP-endonuclease activity in these mutants is sufficient for fully efficient VSP mismatch repair). Enzymes with associated N-glycosylase/AP-endonuclease activities which act on AP-site intermediates independently of the general AP-site repair are known (see Friedberg 1985 for review). Our finding of the involvement of DNA polymerase I in VSP mismatch repair is in agreement with the proposal that DNA polymerase I is involved in short patch repair and DNA polymerase III in long patch repair processes (see Friedberg 1985 for review).
