# Introduction.

Good-correlations have been obtained between agents which damage DNA and mutagenicity [1, 2]. Mutagens have been classified by Drake and Baltz [3], and Radman et al. [4] into two groups :

a. Direct mutagens cause subtle modification of DNA bases in the template and/or in the DNA synthesis precursors : these do not inhibit DNA synthesis and their mutagenic effect is caused by direct mispairing.

b. Indirect mutagens cause bulky DNA lesions and thereby inhibit DNA synthesis. Representative mutagens belonging to these two classes are listed in table I ; it is interesting that MNNG acts both as a direct mutagen and as indirect mutagen [5]. This paper will deal only with chemical agents which act as indirect mutagens. We have chosen ultraviolet radiation as a convenient agent for induction of DNA lesions and we will discuss its effects on DNA replication.

The following lines of evidence permit the assumption that the pyrimidine dimers introduced in DNA by ultraviolet irradiation (C. Helene, this symposium) are representative of the DNA lesions produced by the other indirect mutagens listed in table I.

Aflatoxin $\mathbf { B _ { 1 } }$ $( \mathbf { A A F _ { 1 } } )$ , a potent mutagenic and carcinogenic compound [6, 7] which binds covalently to DNA, has been shown to inhibit in vivo DNA synthesis in various organisms [8, 9], as has been demonstrated for ultraviolet-induced pyrimidine dimers (see below). Furthermore low doses of $\mathbf { A A F } _ { 1 }$ can substitute for ultraviolet radiation in the induction of Weigle-reactivation of simian virus 40 in monkey kidney cells [10].

Mitomycin C, which produces cross links in DNA [11] has been found to cause inhibition of DNA replication in vivo [12]. Mitomycin C treated DNA and UV treated DNA both show a similar reduction in capacity to act as templates for E. coli DNA polymerase I synthesis [13].

3) The benzo(a)pyrene derivative, benzo(a)pyrene-diolepoxide, reacts covalently with nucleic acids [14] ; as a consequence of which the in vivo infectivity of BP-diolepoxide treated $\phi \tt { { x } \tt { { 1 7 4 } } }$ bacteriophage is drastically reduced. An equivalent reduction in the ability to support in vitro DNA synthesis is also observed when $\phi \mathbf { \times } \mathbf { 1 } 7 4$ DNA is modified by the same agent [15]. Ultraviolet irradiation of either $\phi \tt { x } 1 7 4$ bacteriophage or its DNA has the same effects [16, 17]. Finally all the indirect mutagens listed in table I are believed to induce in $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli a repair system known as $\mathfrak { a s o s }$ repair $\gg$ , which includes prophage induction in lysogenic bacteria ([18] ; Devoret, this symposium).



UV-radiation produces a variety of photoproducts in DNA, among which the intrastrand cyclobutane-type dimers of adjacent pyrimidines are the most common (Helene, this symposium). Wildtype strains of $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ coli can neutralize the potentially lethal effect of pyrimidine dimers utilizing either photoreactivation repair (Helene, this symposium) or excision repair (Laval, this symposium). Pyrimidine dimers that are neither photoenzymatically split nor removed from DNA by excision repair, have been shown to block DNA replication in vivo [19]. In addition, results obtained on the in vitro replication of denatured DNA either by $\pmb { { \cal E } }$ . coli DNA polymerase I or partially purified $\pmb { \alpha }$ polymerase from calf thymus indicated a serious depression of DNA synthesis by UV-irradiation of the template [20-13]. However, Rupp and Howard-Flanders [21] noted that excision deficient strains of $\boldsymbol { \mathscr { E } }$ coli can still tolerate the presence of a large number of pyrimidine dimers in their DNA and, using alkali sucrose gradient sedimentation to examine the DNA synthesized after UV-irradiation, detected discontinuities in the newly synthesized DNA which were slowly repaired. The correlation between the number of these post-replicational gaps and the number of pyrimidine dimers in the DNA led them to postulate that these gaps were formed opposite pyrimidine dimers [22]. Furthermore, using density labels to distinguish between preexisting (< parental $\gg$ ) and newly-synthesized ( $\measuredangle$ daughter $\gg$ )DNA, it was shown that parental DNA became incorporated into the daughter DNA strand with one exchange observed for every one or two pyrimidine dimers [23]. This led to the formation of models involving the filling of post replicational gaps with parental strand DNA through a recombinational type of repair [24], the mechanisms of which have been further elucidated by Ganesan [25].

In another set of experiments, single-strand DNA phage $\phi \chi 1 7 4$ was irradiated and infected into an intact host under conditions where excision repair and recombinational repair were unlikely to occur (i.e. passage from single to double strand form). The DNA was then extracted and found to be replicated only to the first pyrimidine dimer [16, 26]. In vitro studies have fully confirmed that pyrimidine dimers in $\phi \chi 1 7 4$ template act as an absolute block for DNA synthesis by E. coli DNA polymerases I and III [17, 27].

DNA synthesized in ultraviolet-irradiated mouse cells is smaller than that made in unirradiated cells, as indicated by its sedimentation in alkaline sucrose gradient [28]. Hence DNA synthesized on ultraviolet-irradiated DNA contains discontinuities, which are filled on subsequent incubation. The estimated distance between these discontinuities is comparable to the average spacing between pyrimidine dimers in the parental strand, suggesting that the replication machinery leaves gaps opposite the dimers. However, there is evidence suggesting that gaps opposite the pyrimidine dimers are filled with newly synthesized DNA without significant involvment of parental DNA and recombinational exchanges [28]. DNA replication in ultraviolet light irradiated HeLa cells has been studied with two different techniques : measurements of the kinetics of semiconservative DNA synthesis and DNA fiber autoradiography. The extent of DNA synthesis inhibition varied with the time after irradiation. After doses less than $\mathbf { 1 0 } \ \mathbf { J } / \mathbf { m ^ { 2 } }$ , the rate of DNA synthesis was initially depressed, but later showed some recovery. After higher doses of irradiation a constant low rate of synthesis was seen for at least the initial 6 hours [29]. Using the same techniques, DNA replication in ultraviolet irradiated chinese hamster cells has been studied. It was observed that after a dose of $5 \ \mathbf { J } / \mathbf { m ^ { 2 } }$ ,the rate of fork progression is the same as in nonirradiated cells, while the rate of replication is reduced by 50 per cent. However, after a dose of $\bf { 1 0 } \delta \ J / \bf { m ^ { 2 } }$ , a significant reduction in the rate of fork progression was also detected. Therefore it was suggested that at low doses of ultraviolet light irradiation the inhibition of DNA replication is due to a reduction in the number of functioning replicons, while at high doses the rate of fork progression is also affected [30].

The above data seem to indicate that UV-photoproducts, probably pyrimidine dimers, block, at least temporarily, the continuous progress of the DNA replication complex both in bacteria and mammalian cells. We would like to speculate here that this blockage could share a similar mechanism. We also speculate that an induced release of this blockage may result in a mutagenic event.

The discrepancy between the in vivo and in vitro DNA synthesis by mammalian DNA polymerases and the increase in the capacity of $\pmb { \mathfrak { a } }$ polymerase from calf spleen to copy UV irradiated template displayed by more purified enzyme fractions suggested the existence of some factor(s) which prevent(s) DNA synthesis past pyrimidine dimers. A $3 ^ { \circ }  5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease has been purified which can arrest DNA synthesis by purified $\pmb { \mathfrak { a } }$ polymerase on damaged template (fig. 3). We are currently testing the hypothesis that this exonuclease is a true $\pmb { \ll }$ proof reading $\gg$ exonuclease with biological functions analogous to those of polymerase-associated $3 ^ { \circ }  \mathbf { 5 ^ { \prime } }$ exonuclease of $E$ . coli [35] or phage T4 [36] and some preliminary results support this hypothesis (Spadari, Villani and Radman, in preparation). In addition, we are planning to prepare antibodies against this mammalian $3 ^ { \circ }  5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease to test the possibility of a tight association with DNA polymerases by immunoprecipitation in the crude cellular extracts. The isolation from rabbit bone marrow of a DNA polymerase with $\ll$ associated $\gg$ $3 ^ { \circ }  { \bar { \bf 5 } } ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease has been reported [37] ; our result using calf spleen however suggests that this association is broken in the course of purification. The relationship between the $3 ^ { \circ }  5 ^ { \circ }$ exonuclease from calf spleen and the DNAse III from rabbit bone marrow [38] is not clear although they appear to have different MW as estimated by sedimentation and gel filtration.

This research was inspired by our preceeding work on the mechanism of UV-induced mutagenesis in $E$ . coli [16, 17]. It was suggested that the DNA polymerase associated $3 ^ { \circ }  5 ^ { \prime }$ exonuclease provokes the arrest of DNA synthesis at each pyrimidine dimer (fig. 1) and hence could contribute to the absence of UV-induced mutagenesis when irradiated phage λ or $\phi { \tt X } { \tt I } 7 4$ infect intact host cells. This $\ll$ proof reading > exonuclease seems to repeatedly remove all incorporated dNMPs opposite pyrimidine dimers causing an $ { \langle { \langle { \mathcal Z } \rangle } }$ idling > of the DNA polymerase at each lesion i.e. turnover of dNTPs to free dNMPs in the absence of chain elongation (fig. 2).

It was therefore proposed that the mutagenic SOS-induction pathway [18] which is dependent upon $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli recA and LexA genes, probably leads to the synthesis of (an) inhibitor(s) of the $\pmb { \mathcal { Q } }$ proof reading $\gg$ activity, hence permitting the bypass synthesis [16] by misincorporation of non complementary nucleotides opposite pyrimidine dimers and also elsewhere in the DNA [17].

If this molecular scenario for the mutagenesis by non-pairing lesions is correct for E. coli, our results suggest that it could apply also to mammalian cells. Furthermore, it has been recently reported that induced reactivation (Weigle-reactivation) of UV-irradiated Herpes virus, as well as mutagenesis of both intact and UV-irradiated Herpes virus, can be triggered by UV-irradiation of the host cell and it is inhibited by cycloheximide [39] mimicking earlier findings with λ phage in $E$ . coli [40]. This finding supports the idea that induced mutagenesis in mammalian cells could be a genetically controlled process similar to that found in bacteria [18, 41], operating perhaps on the activity of the $\ll$ proof-reading > exonuclease.
