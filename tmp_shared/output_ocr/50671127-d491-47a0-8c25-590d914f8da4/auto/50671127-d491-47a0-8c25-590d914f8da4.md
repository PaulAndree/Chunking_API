# Introduction

Adaptation to diverse and changing environments constitutes a perpetual challenge to all microorganisms. Natural selection has retained a range of genetic mechanisms that facilitate adaptation to a wide variety of external stimuli (Moxon et al. 1994 and references therein). These mechanisms enable bacteria to modulate the activity of their genes in response to particular external conditions, thereby maintaining their fitness in a changing environment. Those mechanisms are sufficient only within certain limits of change and are not useful in all diverse and unpredictable environments. Bacteria, which are often confronted with persisting unfavourable environments, must be able to adapt genetically by natural selection; otherwise they may face extinction.

This adaptation is mostly driven either by genetic exchange or a genetic change due to a mutation. Because most newly arising mutations are neutral or deleterious, it was argued that the mutation rate has evolved to be as low as possible and nature selected for an optimal compromise between adaptability and adaptedness (Kimura 1967; Leigh 1973; Drake 1991). Despite this theory, it has been shown that mutators, alleles that increase mutation rate, could increase in frequency even in a stable, yet novel, environment such as chemostats (Gibson et al. 1970; Cox and Gibson 1974; Chao and Cox 1983). Recently simulation approaches have demonstrated that mutator alleles can play a role in adaptive evolution (Taddei et al. 1997a). These mutator alleles can accelerate the fitness increase by generating favourable mutations. During this process, mutators can get fixed as they hitchhike with the favourable mutations they have generated.

Among natural isolates of Escherichia coli, up to a few per cent are mutator clones showing higher mutation rates (Treffers et al. 1954; Jyssum 1960; Gross and Siegel 1981; LeClerc et al. 1996; Matic et al. 1997). Analysis of the clones with the highest mutation rates showed that their mutator phenotype is mainly due to defective alleles of one

of the mismatch repair genes encoding the methyl-directed mismatch repair system (MRS; also called long patch or general mismatch repair). Starting with wild-type laboratory strains of E. coli, it has been repeatedly shown that mutators could be isolated following different protocols. When characterized, most of them were due to defective alleles of one of the mismatch repair genes encoding MRS (Liberfarb and Bryson 1970; Hoess and Herman 1975; Mao et al. 1997; Sniegowski et al. 1997; Rosenberg et al. 1998).

Besides these genetic defects, MRS can become inefficient in some physiological conditions. We shall review the current knowledge about MRS regulation and describe new results where we have been able to measure MRS efficiency in vivo using a mismatch repair substrate.

The MRS is well conserved in most organisms, ranging from bacteria to man (Eisen 1998). Thanks to its mismatch repair abilities, the MRS is important for the maintenance of genomic integrity (Radman et al. 1995; Modrich and Lahue 1996). The MRS can prevent point mutation, repeat expansion and contraction, transposon excision and recombination by recognizing mismatches via MutSLHU proteins. The absence of MRS activities was shown to be implicated in various phenotypes such as cancer in mammals (for review see Modrich and Lahue 1996) and horizontal transfer enhancement in bacteria (Rayssiguier et al. 1989; Matic et al. 1995; Vulic et al. 1997). Heritable or transient alterations of MRS efficiency are therefore likely to produce diverse phenotypes affecting genetic stability. The potential impact of its inefficiency for evolution, pathogenesis and tumorigenesis will be addressed.

# Incidence of MRS-deficient mutators isolated in the laboratory

On account of an increased frequency of diverse kinds of mutations, strong mutators were isolated either as general mutators or mutators with enhanced frameshift mutagenesis (for recent reviews on all kinds of mutators see Miller 1996 and Horst et al. 1999).

The first two mutators isolated among laboratory strains were mutT and the 'Harvard' strain. The strain carrying the mutT1 (Treffers et al. 1954) mutation was obtained from an E. coli K-12 derivative strain. This new strain appeared after several routine stock-culture passages and had the same auxotrophies as the parental strain but exhibited a higher mutation rate.

In 1955 another mutator strain arose in the Department of Bacteriology at Harvard (Goldstein and Smoot 1955). This strain, selected accidentally during single-colony purification, differed from its nearest ancestor by its enhanced mutability. This mutator phenotype induced the accumulation of auxotrophies and was assumed to have arisen as a mutational event. It was located in the same region as mutT but not at the same locus (Zamenhof 1966). Both Treffer's and the 'Harvard' strains were isolated by chance without either mutagenic treatment or previous selection. Similarly, the first defective mutS allele and the conditional lethal mutator dnaQ49 were isolated by chance respectively by Siegel and Bryson (1964) and Horiuchi et al. (1978).

After the long-term evolution (by serial dilution) of 12 independent cultures of isogenic E. coli populations in a glucose-limited environment, the mutation rate was surveyed in each population (Sniegowski et al. 1997). During this evolution all populations accumulated several uncharacterized mutations which increased their fitness. After a few thousand generations, three populations exhibited a significantly higher and stable mutation rate compared to the parental strain (table 1).

To characterize modes of action of mutator alleles in $E .$ coli, Liberfarb and Bryson (1970) screened a population for an increase in rate of mutation to streptomycin resistance and T-phage resistance (table 1). They found approximately $1 \%$ of mutators among mutagenized populations. The conditional mutD5 mutator was also isolated from a mutagenized population (Degnen and $\mathbf { C o x \ 1 9 7 4 } )$ . Populations mutagenized by random insertion of transposons or phage Mu exhibit mutator mutants in frequency ranging from $3 \times 1 0 ^ { - 4 }$ to $1 0 ^ { - 3 }$ (table 1; Conrad et al. 1976; Shanabruch et al. 1981; Siegel et al. 1982). This technique yielded 16 MRS-deficient mutators in both $E .$ coli and Salmonella enterica (table 1).

Adaptive mutation studies widely used $\mathrm { L a c ^ { - } }$ frameshiftbearing cells that gave rise to $\mathrm { L a c ^ { + } }$ cells when incubated on lactose plates (for reviews see Foster 1998; Rosenberg et al. 1998). Six out of $5 5 \mathrm { { L a c ^ { + } } }$ stationary-phase revertants, with one or more associated unselected mutations, had heritable mutator mutations (Torkelson et al. 1997). Five of these six mutators had lost the function of an essential component of the MRS (table 1 and Rosenberg et al. 1998). Similarly, five mutators were isolated, after a single selection, from among 12,000 revertants of a $\operatorname { H i s } ^ { \cdots }$ strain of S. typhimurium (LeClerc et al. 1998). 2-Aminopurine (2-AP) is very toxic to a dam mutant; resistance is restored only by inactivation of any gene of the MRS. With such a selection, all 2-APresistant revertants of dam" strains appeared to carry a defective MRS gene (Glickman and Radman 1980).

The other mutators also isolated as frameshift enhancers from laboratory stocks were obtained after chemical mutagenesis. In 1975 Hoess and Hermann (1975) devised a procedure to select for mutants of $E .$ coli with enhanced rates of spontaneous frameshift mutation after mutagenic treatment (table 1). After several rounds of selection, nearly $100 \%$ of isolated mutant clones had a mutator phenotype. Mao et al. (1997) studied the proliferation of mutators in a population of $E .$ coli. They observed that the frequency of mutators in a population is closely related to the selection. From less than $\overline { { 1 0 ^ { - 5 } } }$ when no selection is applied, this frequency can rise to about $1 \%$ after a mutagenic treatment or a single selection, and to $2 5 \%$ after two successive rounds of selection. Finally, combining prior mutagenic treatment and three successive rounds of selection, the frequency of mutators can reach $100 \%$ . This relation between the frequency of mutators in a population and the applied selection (or mutagenic treatment) is supported by computer simulation (Tenaillon et al. 1999) and by the mutator frequency observed in the different experiments described above.

MRS mutations are strong mutators. Such constitutive mutators can accelerate the process of adaptation to a new environment. However, when adaptation is reached, frequent generation of deleterious and lethal mutations counterselects mutator mutants. An alternative to becoming mutator by mutating MRS genes could be to increase the mutation rate transiently during a period of stress. Modulation of MRS activity could be one of the ways cells can transiently become mutators (see Ninio 1991 for other ways of becoming transient mutator).

# Regulation of the MRS

The genetics and biochemistry of the MRS have been extensively studied, but much less is known about its regulation. We know that mutL and mutS are not inducible by diverse DNA-damaging agents such as UV and X-ray irradiation, the base analogue 2-AP, or the methylating agent $N$ -methyl$N ^ { \prime }$ -nitro ${ \mathbf { \nabla } } \cdot N$ -nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) (Pang et al. 1985; M. Radman and A. Bradenburger, unpublished).

# Saturation

Whereas mismatch repair genes appear to be noninducible, except for mutU (also known as uvrD) which is part of the SOS regulon (Siegel 1983), it was shown that MRS can be saturated in two genetic backgrounds. This was observed in fast-growing cells carrying the mutD5 mutator mutation that causes erroneous DNA synthesis due to nonfunctional proofreading activity of DNA polymerase III (Radman et al. 1981; Schaaper 1988, 1989; Damagnez et al. 1989; Schaaper and Radman 1989). In this mutant, the MRS is unable to repair the $\mathbf { M } 1 3 \mathbf { m p } 2$ phage heteroduplex DNA containing mismatches (see figure 1). MRS efficiency can be restored under these conditions either by slowing chromosomal DNA replication (Damagnez et al. 1989) or by overproducing MutL or MutH (but not MutS) proteins (Schaaper 1989).

The saturation of the MRS was shown to be implicated in the mutagenic effect of overexpression of retrons, encoding multicopy single-strand DNA (msDNA) molecules containing mismatches (Inouye and Inouye 1992; Maas et al. 1994, 1996). The increased mutagenesis provoked by these msDNAs is likely to be due to the sequestration of MRS proteins because increased mutagenesis was not observed when a retron without any mismatch was overproduced.

A defect in MRS can also affect recombination between diverged sequences (Rayssiguier et al. 1989; Matic et al. 1995; Vulic et al. 1997). Transduction of a heterospecific $E .$ coli DNA into S. typhimurium was shown to increase when cells were treated with MNNG a few hours before transduction (Mergeay and Gerits 1983). This observation suggests that MNNG treatment can also saturate MRS.

# Down regulation

Two recent papers reported that the amounts of two MRS proteins, MutS and MutH, but not MutL and MutU, are negatively regulated via Hfq and RpoS proteins during stationary phase of $E .$ coli (Feng et al. 1996; Tsui et al. 1997). One of the interesting questions raised by this observation was whether the efficiency of the MRS is also decreased during stationary phase. To directly assess the level of mismatch repair activity in stationary phase, we have used a heteroduplex-DNA transfection assay (figure 1). This assay, which involves genetic analysis of the progeny of an in vitro constructed phage $\mathbf { M } \mathbf { 1 } 3 \mathbf { m } \mathbf { p } 2$ heteroduplex DNA, had serendipitously revealed that late-log-phase bacteria may have somewhat decreased MRS activity (table 1 in Schaaper and Radman 1989).

Cells from different growth phases were transformed with a hemimethylated heteroduplex DNA containing a G:T mismatch in the lacZ gene, and the progeny of such a molecule were analysed (figure 1). Pure phage progeny in an infective centre derived from a single heteroduplex DNA can arise either by an asymmetric replication (strand loss) or by mismatch repair, whereas a mixed infective centre is necessarily derived from a nonrepaired heteroduplex. The results of transfection experiments clearly show that, from the late log phase on, bacteria progressively lose their mismatch repair capacity (figure 2a). However, bacterial cells from overnight cultures still show significant residual repair activity $2 7 \%$ mixed infective centres) compared with mutS mutants $6 4 \%$ mixed infective centres). The steady level of 60 to $65 \%$ of mixed infective centres obtained with ∆mutS suggests that the results obtained with wild-type bacteria reflect the loss of MRS activity rather than a different mode of M13 DNA replication in nongrowing bacteria.

To test whether our results are a consequence of the deficit of Mut proteins, the same analysis was performed in parallel with strains overproducing individual MutS, MutL or MutH proteins (figure 2b). It is clear that MutS overproduction largely prevents the loss of mismatch repair activity in nongrowing bacteria $( P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ; MutH overproduction has a significant $( P < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , but smaller, effect, whereas MutL overproduction has no effect. These results are in agreement with those of Winkler's group on the level of mismatch repair proteins in stationary phase (Feng et al. 1996; Tsui et al. 1997). The discrepancies between our results and those obtained in some other studies (Harris et al. 1997; Foster and Rosche 1999), which suggested that different MRS proteins might be limiting in the prevention of 'adaptive' and growth-dependent mutations during the stationary phase, may be due to the different physiological conditions experienced by the bacteria in our respective assays.

Mutators carrying a defective gene of the MRS are by far the most frequent when the mutator phenotype is surveyed among natural isolates (Gross and Siegel 1981; LeClerc et al. 1996; Matic et al. 1997) or selected in the laboratory. Protocols used to isolate mutator clones in the laboratory are sometimes biased in favour of MRS defects (e.g. when the selection is for reversion of a frameshift). However, even when the protocols used did not include reversion of frameshift, the majority of mutators selected for a general increase in mutation rate are those affected in the MRS (14 out of 16, see table 1). High prevalence of MRS mutants among strong mutators can be explained by three nonexclusive aspects: (i) their target size, which when taken together is 20-fold larger than that of mutT, (ii) the fact that they are nonessential, and (ii) their broad mutational specicity. The first point is supported by the correlation between the size of the antimutator genes and the frequency of their defective alleles (figure 3). One exception might be the relatively low frequency of appearance of mutU mutators. The mutU gene, encoding DNA helicase II, is the only one implicated in several pathways of repair, recombination and replication and is part of both the MRS and the SOS response (Friedberg et al. 1995 and references therein). This involvement of mutU in different systems, which may lead to pleiotropic effects in a mutU mutant, might be an explanation for its relative underrepresentation.

This gene is part of a superoperon with a complex arrangement of seven genes that are involved in several important cellular processes (Tsui et al. 1994). Thus, some mutations of mutL gene might be counterselected in nature, because of their interference with transcription of other genes from this superoperon and the resulting pleiotropic effects.

It is interesting to compare the high incidence of defective MRS genes among bacterial isolates (Matic et al. 1997) and their role in the evolution of fitness to the incidence of MRS deficiency among tumour cells (Loeb 1998 and references therein). Loeb reported that the high number of mutations found in tumour cells cannot be accounted for by the mutation rate observed in normal somatic cells and that it could be a manifestation of a mutator phenotype. The role of defective MRS in most hereditary non-polyposis colon cancer (HNPCC) has been demonstrated (Loeb 1998 and references therein). Microsatellite instability studies indicate that up to $30 \%$ of all sporadic tumours may exhibit a mutator phenotype (Loeb 1998).

An alternative to constitutive mutators, which are expected to accumulate deleterious mutations, are inducible mutators, which may allow bacterial populations facing stress to enhance their adaptability by increasing their mutation rate only under strong selective pressure. We report here some evidence that the MRS may be down regulated during periods of stress, in particular during stationary phase. This is in agreement with the spectrum of 'adaptive' mutations, which is typical of polymerase errors that are not corrected by MRS (Foster and Trimarchi 1994; Rosenberg et al. 1994). However, as the efficiency of the MRS gradually decreases between the exponential growth phase and the stationary phase (figure 2), some of the mutations arising during overnight culture could be due to uncorrected replication errors (Foster and Rosche 1999). In any case, the in vivo system we used is not suited to measure or detect 'adaptive' mutations. It only suggests that the MRS becomes less and less efficient in the repair of a mismatched heteroduplex phage DNA even from the end of the exponential growth phase. The efficiency of repair of such a molecule seems to be limited by the amount of mismatch repair proteins MutH and, particularly, MutS (figure 2). Whether this decreased repair efficiency is responsible for mutation in other replicons will depend on the timing of their replication versus repair in stationary-phase cells (Stahl 1988).

It is interesting that the MRS, which prevents all kinds of genetic alterations, should be only down regulated, whereas the inducible mutagenic SOS system is only up regulated (for a review see Taddei et al. 1997b). Although SOS induction and functional MRS have opposite genetic effects (one is a mutator and the other is an antimutator system), their opposite regulation (up and down respectively) under conditions of stress has the same effect, i.e. an increase in genetic variability. The synergy between the inefficiency of MRS and the induction of SOS (reviewed in Taddei et al. 1997b) endows bacteria with an impressive adaptive potential for increased variability, which can be triggered under stressful growth conditions.

Whether this increase in genetic variability has an adaptive value is related to the classical debate of evolutionary genetics about the optimal mutation rate. Is it as low as other constraints (speed of replication, energy cost) will allow (minimal model) or is it due to equilibrium between deleterious and beneficial mutation (optimal model)? This debate has been addressed by modelling for a population of cells under directional selection (Taddei et al. 1997a) and for exploration of an adaptive landscape (Magnasco and Thaler 1996). Taddei et al. have shown that an increased mutation rate is selected for during adaptation to a new environment (a result illustrated experimentally by long-term experimental evolution (Sniegowski et al. 1997)), but counterselected when the adaptive peak has been reached.

Situations of stress where cells transiently acquire a mutator phenotype could be another interesting evolutionary strategy. However, this attractive hypothesis is open to criticism. It is still unclear whether an increase in mutation frequency in response to stress would be good because it might help cells to find a beneficial mutation (genetic engineer hypothesis), or bad because it might lead to the accumulation of deleterious mutations but unavoidable given the level of stress (sick watchmaker hypothesis). In the case of transient MRS inefficiency this discussion is in need of knowing whether there are low levels of mismatch repair enzymes because their synthesis is costly and because DNA synthesis has slowed down, and/or whether such MRS inefficiency is mutagenic and hence adaptive at the end of the exponential growth phase.

Recent evidence that tumour cells containing a particular mutation in hMSH2 (human MutS homologue 2) become mutator only when maintained under nongrowing conditions (Richards et al. 1997) supports the adaptive hypothesis, as these tumour cells are likely to have acquired a selective advantage leading to their clonal expansion. The variable efficiency of MRS and the highly variable mutation rate observed among natural isolates of $E _ { i }$ coli (Matic et al. 1997) also provide an argument against the sick watchmaker hypothesis. In conclusion, the adaptive potential of an inefficient MRS due to an increase in mutation rate under adverse conditions offers an interesting evolutionary paradigm. Further mechanistic and population-genetic studies of mutagenesis driven by down-regulated MRS should lead us to better understanding of the relationship between cost and benefit of mutagenesis.
