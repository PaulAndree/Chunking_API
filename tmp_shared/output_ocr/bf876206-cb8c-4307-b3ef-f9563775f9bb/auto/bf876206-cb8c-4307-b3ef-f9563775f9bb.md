# Introduction

Genomes of complex organisms often bear over a million copies of diverse short (a few hundred bp) and long (several thousand bp) repeated sequences interspersed throughout the genome (eg SINÉs and LINEs in mammalian genomes [1]). Their sequence patterns suggest that they are degenerate transposable elements. If the crossing-over recombination frequency between such repeats were as low as $\pmb { 1 0 } ^ { - 6 }$ , the majority of cells in an organism would suffer at least one gross chromosomal rearrangement or aberration. Yet, only in rare cellular events, which are often at the origin of some hereditary disease or cancer, can one observe chromosomal aberrations caused by inter-repeat recombination. What are the molecular mechanisms for specifically suppressing recombination among diverse repeated sequences dispersed throughout animal and plant chromosomes while such chromosomes effectively recombine as mitotic sister chromatids (in particular during repair of DNA damage) and also as homologs in meiosis? What is the molecular basis for avoidance of out-of-register recombinations?

Whereas the standard answers like 'chromatin structure' or 'nuclear matrix attachement' may well receive some experimental support in the future, there is sufficient evidence to postulate a general mechanism for the tuning of recombination frequencies based on the key requirement for efficient generalized recombination, ie the perfect (or almost perfect) sequence homology. Efficient 'ectopic' recombination between only a kilobase of perfect homology embedded in non homologous yeast chromosomes [2] suggests that even in meiosis, with prominent pairing of homologous chromosomes, the opportunity to form a perfectly base-paired hybrid (or heteroduplex) joint region largely determines whether and where recombination will occur. Thus the longer the heteroduplex DNA region and the more rigorous the requirement for perfect base pair matching within such re gions, the higher the precision of homologous recombination excluding the out-of-register recombinations.

# Mechanisms

Consider a pair of identical sequence repeats at some distance; crossovers between the 2 repeats would cause deletion $( \mathbf { \hat { n } } \mathbf { g } \ \mathbf { \theta } 1 )$ . Consider now the effect on recombination of the 2 means of reducing the number of matching base pairs by say $20 \%$ . Shrinking or fragmenting the block of homology (eg from 1 to 0.8 kb) will minimally reduce recombination, whereas decreasing the degree of homology to $80 \%$ by introducing well-spread point mutations will reduce recombination to a negligible level. This is true for bacteria [3. 4] and for mouse cells (5], and the mechanisms of these effects are fairly well understood in bacteria such that we now have a new paradigm for exploring the molecular basis of chromosomal stability and evolution in eucaryotes. This new paradigm explains surprising observations that sequence divergence by well-spread point mutation differences prevents homologous recombination far more efficiently than blocks of heterology [3]. It appears that the recombination machinery of bacteria, such as Escherichia and Salmonella, is itself of quite low fidelity in that it requires sequence identity for only small blocks (called MEPS, minimum efficient processing segment) used to initiate the strand exchange process (6]. Once initiated, the strand exchange process will proceed even across large heterologies both in vitro [7] and in vivo [8]. However, recombination in wild type cells will be prevented by a low sequence divergence but not by large heterologies, because of the specificity of the mismatch repair system which edits genetic recombination. The editing consists of aborting those recombinational heteroduplex intermediates which bear well recognized single base-pair mismatches and/or frameshift mutation mismatches, but not those bearing loops of non-homology [9]. The Rec ABCD recombination system of $\pmb { \cal E }$ coli appears to only require $\approx 3 0$ bp sequence identity (MEPS) to initiate recombination, whereas the mismatch repair system imposes the requirement for sequence identity along the entire hybrid DNA_ joint length which is in the order of several kb [10].

Sequence polymorphism of repetitive sequences within eucaryotic chromosomes and chromosomal sequence polymorphism within populations of mammalian species is such that the only mismatch-free recombination events would be those involving sister chromatids at corresponding homologous sites. Sister chromatid exchanges (SCEs) are indeed the most frequently detected recombination events in mammalian cells. Therefore, it may be that the sequence divergence and polymorphism among homologous sequences of eucaryotic chromosomes on the one hand, and the activity of mismatch recognition and repair enzymes on the other, are the key elements of chromosomal stability. Indeed, mismatch repair deficiency causes increased rates of chromosomal rearrangements via RecA dependent recombination between sequence-diverged repeats in $\pmb { E }$ coli [11]. It may be that mismatch repair deficient mutants of mammalian cells would become intoierably unstable because of recombination among repetitive sequences and also between the polymorphic homologs (mitotic recombination) and because of the uncorrected replication errors.

Whereas it is now easy to imagine how to maintain the chronosomal stability by sequence divergence among repeated sequences, it is more difficult to imagine how the stability was assured following invasion and/or amplification of transposons when their sequences were identical. Sequence divergence by rare spontaneous mutations is not likely to be an efficient mechanism. However, an active and efficient mechanism may well exist. An extensive sequence analysis of eucaryotic chromosomes and genes strongly suggests that a duplication-targeted DNA methylation and methylation (5-meC)-mediated mutagenesis have functionally and recombinationally inactivated all highly homologous transposons, turning them into a functionally and recombinationally inert 'junk' DNA [12]. Most of the deficit of $\mathbf { C } \mathbf { p } \bar { \mathbf { G } }$ dinucleotides (substrates for methylation) apparently resulted from CpG -> TpG mutagenesis targeted to those duplicated DNA sequences which create contiguous homologies of $> \mathbf { 0 . 3 }$ kb. The pattern of partial $\mathbf { C _ { P } G }$ loss in single-copy housekeeping genes suggested specific homologous interactions between genes and incompletely processed retropseudogene sequences allowing us the estimate of the MEPS for this mutagenic homologous interaction to $\approx 0 . 3$ kb [12]. This estimate is similar to those of MEPS in homologous recombination within mouse chromosomes in cell culture [5]. Repeat-induced metnylation and point mutation (RIP) discovered in Neurospora [13] and methylation induced premeiotically (MIP, a term used by Rossignol et al) described in Ascobolus [14] present the best studied phenomena related to the above analysis suggesting the hypothesis illustrated in figure 2. Many endogenous or foreign sequences when repeated in these fungi (eg by ectcpic inserts) are heavily C-methylated following fertilization and prior to karyogamy in both species. However, in Neurospora but not in Ascobolus, methylation is accompanied by such frequent $\mathbf { C } \longrightarrow \mathbf { T }$ mutations $( e g$ up to $20 \text{‰}$ C ->T events in a single sexual generation) that an enzymatic deamination of 5-meC has been proposed [13]."

At this point, one can formulate a molecular model as a working hypothesis and a research project to study the strategy for prevention of excessive transposon spreading and of inter-repeat recombination processes. These would lead to chromosome scrambling via crossing over and to gene inactivation by gene conversion of active genes by their inactive pseudogene sequences. The basic strategy is to identify, mark and mutate sequence repeats longer than some minimum necessary for identification. Identification is based on homologous sequence interaction: a pairing test during the haploid germ line stage. Marking is based on DNA cytosine methylation (5-meC) and the mechanism may involve formation of some 4- or 3- stranded DNA structure (perhaps as an early intermediate in recombination) which is a specific substrate for the germ line C-methylase of initiation type. Heavy methylation (MIP) itself leads to gene inactivation and may immediately silence the transposons, as observed in plants [15]. Finally, mutagenesis is the result of either spontaneous or catalyzed 5-meC deamination to thymine, irreversibly inactivating the repeated genetic elements and providing the structural basis for prevention of homologous recombination by mismatch repair enzymes and by decreasing the number of MEPs for initiation of recombination. This 3-step process could account for the survival of chromosomes following invasion of 'parasitic' DNA elements, because it provides a plausible mechanism for transforming such elements rapidly into inert 'junk' DNA. Chromosomes of higher eukaryotes can be viewed as cemeteries of extransposable elements and retropseudogene copies 3) which "rest-in-peace' (RIP is indeed the original tombstone acronym).

# Evolution of chromosomes and species

On the basis of the preceding discussion and observations that the sequence of nucleic acid carriers of genetic information evolve much faster than that of proteins encoded by them, ie, evolution proceeds predominantly by neutral mutations [16], the following evolutionary scenario can be suggested. Bacteria and yeast evolve slowly, predominantly by neutral mutations in the 3-rd codon letters [17, 18]. When DNA is sufficiently diverged, the general mismatch repair system effectively prevents recombinational interactions, thus providing for a fertility barrier before the courtship/mating barriers have arisen, and eventually triggering a sympatric speciation process [3. 4, 19j. As the diverse repeated and transposable sequences have spread through genomes, their recombination became a threat to genome integrity, and the homology-dependent MIP and RIP evolved either to prevent directly the inter-repeat recombination or to assure their fast diversification, which in turn would prevent such recombination by the mismatch repair system. The sequence-constrained coding regions may have taken the route of fragmentation into exons shorter than MEPS in order to avoid being inactivated by RIP-ing and/or recombination with its processed retropseudogenes. Alternatively, if genes-in-pieces preceded RP-ing, then they would have been protected against RIP-ing from the beginning. Indeed, the current repertoire of exons shows that $< \hat { 3 } \%$ are longer than the putative mammalian MEPS of $\approx 0 . 3 ~ \mathrm { \textmu b }$ 1201.

After surviving the invasion of transposable elements and retropseudogenes by RIP mutagenesis, not only was there no more acute danger of disintegrating the genome by recombination  but just the opposite - a bonus was gained: thus accumulated sequence polymorphism would have contributed to chromosomal stability and recombinational isolation, $\pmb { e g }$ ,mitotic recombination between homologs would be diminished [3, 4, 19]. At a still higher level of sequence polymorphism the meiotic recombination is also expected to be suppressed, and consequently reproductive isolation towards evolution of new species can take place [3, 4, 19]. Meiotic recombination is clearly more permissive to sequence divergence than mitotic recombination: this may be achieved by enhancing the recombination activity in general, or by down regulating the mismatch repair activity, or by decreasing the length of MEPS and/or heteroduplex joint. This scenario offers a coherent correlation between the presence of noncoding DNA, as the principal carrier of sequence polymorphism, and an increased chromosomal stability, low somatic mitotic recombination and acceleration of the species evolution through inhibition of meiotic chromosome pairing and recombination. Hence, speciation and chromosomal stability may be 'phenotypes' of neutral mutations, which, because of mismatch repair specificity, would be stronger for transition mutations than for transversion mutations and therefore, perhaps by selection, could have contributed to the predominance of transition mutation differences between the newly diverged species (including those devoid of C-methylation [21]).

By accumulating noncoding 'junk' DNA in the course of successive DNA amplifications and RIPings, chromosomes would grow in size but not in information content. Recruiting gene and/or control functions from the evolving 'junk' sequences is one source of gaining new genetic functions. Another more efficicnt means could be via genoinic fusions of different unicellular organisms to eventually form the interspecies genome mosaics. Here also, the (partial) homology blocks such as commonly shared repeated sequences (eg, transposons or ribosomal RNA gene clusters) could have served as recombinational linkers to form new 'superspecies' with composite genomes enriched in functions/genes relative to each parent. Therefore, the appearance of mismatch repair mutants or conditions inhibiting mismatch repair activities may have precipitated formation of new species upon return to functional mismatch repair because of point mutations and genome rearrangements that would have accumulated during the mismatch repair deficiency. Thus, the tuning of mismatch repair activity could simultaneously affect genomic evolution at the level of point mutations and chromosomal rearrangements [4]. The evolutionary scenario described presents a molecular mechanism for evolution of species by neutral mutations, an evolution that can be driven 'internally' by repeat-induced mutagenesis and which could generate potential speciation niches even in the absence of selective pressures and geographical isolation.
